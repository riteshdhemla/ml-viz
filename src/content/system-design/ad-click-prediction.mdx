# Design an Ad Click-Through-Rate Prediction System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Meta / Google Ads"
  scale="billions of predictions/day · <10ms · calibrated probabilities"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

Ad CTR prediction is the highest-stakes ML slot on the internet — the model's
output is multiplied by a bid to decide what runs and what an advertiser pays.
Two things make it distinct from feed ranking: the prediction must be a
**calibrated probability** (not just a good ranking), and it feeds an
**auction** with real economics. It's also the domain with the best paper
trail — Google and Facebook both published unusually frank accounts of their
production CTR systems, and this case leans on them throughout. Walk it through
the **ML project loop** with the objective and evaluation stages front and
center.

## 1. Clarify the problem

- **Goal:** for each candidate ad, predict `P(click)` (and `P(conversion)`) so
  the auction can rank by **expected value** `bid × pCTR`.
- **Why calibration matters:** pricing (second-price / VCG) and budget pacing use
  the *absolute* probability, not just the order — a miscalibrated model
  overspends or underspends advertiser budgets.
- **Scope:** the prediction model + how it plugs into the auction. Out of scope:
  the bidding/budget optimizer internals (mention them).

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
Say "ranking is not enough — the auction needs a *calibrated* probability" in the
first minute. It separates candidates who've built ad systems from those porting
a feed-ranking answer.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | < 10 ms for hundreds of candidate ads | Tiny, heavily-optimized model; feature caching |
| Volume | Billions of predictions/day | Cost per prediction dominates infra spend |
| Calibration | pCTR ≈ observed CTR across slices | Explicit recalibration + monitoring |
| Freshness | Adapt to new ads/trends within hours | Online / frequent incremental training |

**Back-of-envelope:** scoring hundreds of ads in under 10ms means a model that's cheap
per item — wide-and-deep / factorization-based, not a giant transformer per ad.

<ThinkFirst question="Facebook measured what happens when a CTR model trained on one day's data is evaluated on each of the following days. What did the curve look like — and what does it force operationally?">
Monotonic decay: prediction quality degrades steadily as the gap between
training data and serving traffic grows — new ads launch, campaigns end, user
behavior shifts. Their conclusion was that **data freshness is worth more than
most modeling cleverness**, pushing retraining to at least daily and motivating
online learning. Google went all the way: FTRL-Proximal updates the model
per-example, in a streaming loop. A weekly batch retrain is not a smaller
version of this system — it's a different, worse system.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

Binary probability estimation under **extreme class imbalance** (CTRs are often
0.1–5%) and **huge sparse feature spaces** (billions of IDs). Framed as
**calibrated binary classification** with a logloss objective. The ranking that
matters is `expected value = bid × pCTR × quality`, computed in the auction.

*What was breaking before?* Rule-based / popularity ad selection ignores
context and can't price efficiently; a ranker without calibration breaks the
auction's economics.

<ThinkFirst question="CTR is around 1%, so 99% of your training rows are negatives. What's the standard fix — and its hidden cost?">
**Negative downsampling** keeps training tractable and balanced — but it
distorts the output: a model trained on downsampled data predicts probabilities
that are too high. In most systems that would be harmless (ranking survives a
monotone distortion); here the auction prices on the absolute number, so you
must analytically correct or **recalibrate** after downsampling.
</ThinkFirst>

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

- **Labels:** impressions → click (and click → conversion, delayed). **Delayed
  feedback** is a real problem: a "no click yet" label may flip later.
- **Features:** user, ad (creative embedding, advertiser, category), context
  (placement, device, time), and dense **cross features** (user × ad category).
  ID features are embedded; the embedding tables dominate model size.
- **Imbalance:** negative downsampling with **calibration correction** to recover
  true probabilities after resampling — Facebook's recipe: downsample negatives
  by rate $w$, then map the model output back with
  $q = p / (p + (1-p)/w)$
  ([He et al., ADKDD 2014](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2648584.2648589)).
- **Freshness:** the same paper showed model quality decays day over day when
  training lags serving — so labels flow into (at least) daily retrains, and the
  feature pipeline is streaming, not nightly.

<ThinkFirst question="Your feature space is billions of sparse IDs (every user × ad × placement cross). One weight per feature can't fit in serving memory. What are your levers before you reach for a smaller model?">
Three, all from Google's production playbook: **hashing** IDs into a bounded
space, an **L1-inducing online update** (FTRL-Proximal) so most weights are
exactly zero and never stored, and **probabilistic feature inclusion** — only
admit a feature once it has appeared enough times to plausibly matter. The
model stays expressive over billions of possible features while materializing
only the tiny fraction that earns its memory.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective + optimization)*

- **Baseline:** logistic regression with hashed cross features, trained online —
  Google's ads system ran on exactly this, with **FTRL-Proximal** per-coordinate
  updates over billions of coefficients
  ([McMahan et al., KDD 2013](https://research.google/pubs/ad-click-prediction-a-view-from-the-trenches/)).
  Still a shockingly strong, cheap baseline.
- **Feature learning:** Facebook's classic upgrade kept the LR but fed it
  **GBDT leaf indices** as learned categorical features — trees as automatic
  feature crossers — beating either model alone by **>3%** in normalized
  entropy ([He et al., ADKDD 2014](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2648584.2648589)).
- **Production today:** a **Wide & Deep / DLRM-family** network — a wide linear
  side that *memorizes* proven crosses plus a deep embedding side that
  *generalizes* to unseen ones, the split Google introduced for Play
  recommendations ([Cheng et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07792));
  at Meta scale the sparse embedding tables dominate model size
  ([DLRM](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00091)). Objective: logloss (a proper
  scoring rule → calibrated by construction, if not distorted).
- **Recalibration:** Platt scaling / isotonic regression on a held-out slice to
  correct residual miscalibration (and the downsampling bias).

<ThinkFirst question="The model emits pCTR = 0.03 for an ad. What consumes that number next, and what does that imply about the model's real job?">
The **auction**: ads are ranked by `bid × pCTR`, and the price the winner pays
is derived from the runner-up's expected value. So the model isn't producing a
relevance score — it's producing one factor in a *price*. Overestimate pCTR by
2× and you overcharge the advertiser by 2×; the model's real job is honest
probabilities, not just correct ordering.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Serving & the auction

Prediction feeds the **auction**: ads are ranked by `bid × pCTR` (× quality), and
the winner pays a price derived from the runner-up (second-price / GSP / VCG).
Budget pacing throttles delivery so advertisers spend evenly.

<WikiLink slug="ad-auction-and-bidding" title="Ad auctions — second-price, GSP, VCG" />

Serving: feature-fetch from a low-latency store, batch-score all candidate ads,
apply pacing/floor rules, run the auction — all under 10ms.

<ThinkFirst question="Which two offline numbers must you track — and how can one improve while the other silently loses money?">
**AUC** (ranking) and **calibration** (ECE / reliability curves). They're
independent: a new model can order ads better *and* systematically overpredict
probabilities, and since price = bid × pCTR, the calibration regression
misprices every auction while your AUC dashboard glows green. Track both, per
segment.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

- **Offline:** **AUC / logloss** for ranking + **calibration** metrics (ECE,
  reliability curves). AUC can improve while calibration degrades — track both.
- **Online:** A/B on revenue *and* advertiser ROI, with guardrails on user
  experience (ad load, relevance) and calibration by segment.
- **Counterfactual:** replay/off-policy estimation before spending live traffic.

<ThinkFirst question="Aggregate calibration is perfect: mean pCTR equals observed CTR to the third decimal. Why can the system still be losing money every hour?">
Because calibration errors can **cancel in aggregate**: overpredicting on video
placements while underpredicting on mobile ones nets to zero overall, yet every
video auction is overpriced and every mobile one underdelivers — advertisers on
each side notice even when your dashboard doesn't. Calibration is only
meaningful **per slice** (placement, advertiser vertical, device, ad age); the
aggregate number is a vanity metric. This is why calibration monitoring, not
AUC monitoring, is the pager-worthy alert.
</ThinkFirst>

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

- **Calibration drift** by slice (advertiser, placement) is the alarm that
  matters most — it silently distorts pricing.
- **Delayed conversions:** a conversion can land up to a month after the click,
  so recent "negatives" are really *censored* labels — model the delay
  distribution explicitly rather than mislabeling them
  ([Chapelle, KDD 2014](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Modeling-delayed-feedback-in-display-advertising-Chapelle/9eed333dcaade2c1dd32590eff6b0c0e670a5674)),
  and backfill labels as conversions arrive. The loop closes into **data**.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Calibrated prob + auction | Rank-only score | Pricing/pacing need absolute probabilities |
| DLRM (embeddings + crosses) | Deep transformer per ad | Latency/cost — the model runs billions of times/day |
| Negative downsampling + recalibration | Train on all negatives | Cheaper; recalibration recovers true probabilities |
| Logloss objective | Pure AUC optimization | Logloss is a proper score → calibrated; AUC ignores calibration |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="AUC went up but revenue dropped. What happened?">
Almost always **calibration**: a higher-AUC model that's miscalibrated misprices
the auction — over/under-bidding relative to true value. Check ECE and per-slice
reliability; recalibrate with isotonic regression before shipping.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you handle delayed conversions in the label?">
Model the delay: use a delayed-feedback model that treats "no conversion yet" as
censored rather than negative, or wait for a fixed attribution window and
backfill. Naively labeling recent impressions as negatives biases pCVR downward.
</Details>

<Details summary="A brand-new ad has no history — how is it priced?">
Content/advertiser-level features and category priors give an initial pCTR; an
exploration budget grants early impressions to gather signal (explore/exploit).
Cold start in ads is expensive, so exploration is bounded and tracked.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Ad CTR prediction must produce a **calibrated probability**, because it feeds an
  **auction** that prices on the absolute number.
- **Latency and cost** force small, embedding-heavy models (DLRM-style), not giant
  networks — it runs billions of times a day.
- **Data freshness beats model cleverness**: day-old training data measurably
  degrades predictions, which is why the field invented online learning (FTRL)
  and daily GBDT+LR refresh cycles.
- Track **AUC *and* calibration** separately — and calibration **per slice**; a
  ranking win with a calibration regression loses money.
