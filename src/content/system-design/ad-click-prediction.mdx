# Design an Ad Click-Through-Rate Prediction System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Meta / Google Ads"
  scale="billions of predictions/day · <10ms · calibrated probabilities"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

Here is what happens in the ten milliseconds after you open an app with an
ad slot. Hundreds of candidate ads are pulled for that slot. For each one, a
model predicts the probability you'll click it. Each prediction is
multiplied by the advertiser's bid to get an expected value, the ads are
ranked by that value, the winner is shown — and the *price* the winning
advertiser pays is derived from the runner-up's expected value. Then the
whole thing happens again, billions of times a day.

Notice what's unusual in that story: the model's output is not a relevance
score used for ordering — it is **a number that gets multiplied by money**.
That's what makes CTR prediction the highest-stakes ML slot on the
internet, and it's what separates this problem from feed ranking, which it
superficially resembles. A feed ranker only needs to get the *order* right.
An ads model must produce a **calibrated probability** — if it says 3%, the
ad must actually get clicked about 3% of the time — because pricing and
budget decisions consume the absolute number, not the ordering. Get the
order right but the magnitudes wrong, and every auction is mispriced while
every ranking dashboard looks healthy. This walkthrough follows the ML
project loop with the **objective and evaluation** stages front and center,
because that's where the calibration story lives.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
This case sits at the intersection of ML and auction economics. The assumed
background, spelled out:

- **CTR / CVR** — click-through rate (clicks ÷ impressions, typically
  0.1–5%) and conversion rate (purchases ÷ clicks). "pCTR" is the model's
  *predicted* click probability.
- **Second-price auctions (GSP / VCG)** — auction formats where the winner
  pays a price derived from the *runner-up's* bid rather than their own,
  which keeps truthful bidding rational — see
  [ad auctions](/wiki/ad-auction-and-bidding).
- **Calibration** — a model is calibrated when its predicted probabilities
  match observed frequencies (of everything predicted "30%", about 30%
  happens). **ECE** (expected calibration error) and **reliability
  diagrams** measure it — see
  [Platt scaling & isotonic regression](/wiki/platt-scaling-and-isotonic-regression).
- **Logloss and proper scoring rules** — logloss (cross-entropy) is
  "proper": it is minimized exactly when the model outputs true
  probabilities, so optimizing it *pushes toward* calibration. AUC, by
  contrast, only measures ordering and is blind to calibration.
- **Sparse ID features and embedding tables** — users, ads, and advertisers
  are categorical IDs from vocabularies of billions; each ID maps to a
  learned dense vector, and these embedding tables are most of the model's
  parameters.
- **Budget pacing** — advertisers set daily budgets; a pacing controller
  throttles how fast each budget is spent so it lasts the whole day instead
  of exhausting by 9 a.m.
- **Negative downsampling** — discarding most non-click rows during
  training to keep it tractable, at the cost of distorting the output
  probabilities (a central plot point below).
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

The system's job: for every candidate ad in an auction, predict `P(click)`
— and, one step further down the funnel, `P(conversion)` — so the auction
can rank ads by **expected value**, `bid × pCTR`. The advertiser says what
a click is worth to them (the bid); the model says how likely the click is;
the product is what the impression is worth.

Why isn't a good *ranking* enough? Because two consumers downstream use the
absolute probability, not the order. **Pricing**: in a second-price-style
auction, the winner's charge is computed from expected values, so an
overestimated pCTR directly overcharges someone. **Budget pacing**: the
pacing controller forecasts spend from predicted probabilities, so
miscalibration makes budgets exhaust too early or leave money unspent.
A model whose probabilities are all 2× too high produces the *same ranking*
as an honest one — and silently corrupts both of these systems.

Scope for the interview: the prediction model and how it plugs into the
auction. The bidding and budget-optimization internals are their own deep
system — name them to show you see the boundary, then stay out.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
Say "ranking is not enough — the auction needs a *calibrated* probability"
in the first minute. It cleanly separates candidates who've built ad
systems from those porting a feed-ranking answer to a problem with real
economics attached.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | < 10 ms for hundreds of candidate ads | Tiny, heavily-optimized model; features cached, not computed |
| Volume | Billions of predictions/day | Cost *per prediction* dominates the infra bill |
| Calibration | pCTR ≈ observed CTR, per slice | Explicit recalibration step + continuous monitoring |
| Freshness | Adapt to new ads/trends within hours | Online or frequent incremental training |

Do the back-of-envelope, because it forces the architecture: hundreds of
ads scored within 10ms means well under 100 microseconds per ad. That
budget admits an embedding-lookup-plus-small-MLP architecture; it does not
admit running a large transformer per candidate. The model family is
decided by arithmetic before any accuracy comparison happens.

## 3. ML problem framing

Formally: **calibrated binary probability estimation** with a logloss
objective, under two conditions that shape everything. First, **extreme
class imbalance** — CTRs run 0.1–5%, so nearly all training rows are
non-clicks. Second, **enormous sparse feature spaces** — the inputs are
mostly IDs (user, ad, advertiser, placement) drawn from vocabularies in the
billions, which is why embedding tables dominate the model. The final
ranking signal, `expected value = bid × pCTR × quality`, is assembled in
the auction, outside the model.

And the spine question — *what was breaking before this system?* Two
predecessors. Rule-based or popularity-based ad selection ignores the
user and context entirely, so it can't price impressions efficiently. And
an uncalibrated ranker — the naive "port feed ranking" answer — breaks the
auction's economics in exactly the way section 1 described.

<ThinkFirst question="CTR is around 1%, so 99% of your training rows are negatives. What's the standard fix — and its hidden cost?">
**Negative downsampling**: throw away most of the non-click rows so
training is tractable and the classes are balanced. The hidden cost is that
the model now sees a world where clicks are common, so its predicted
probabilities come out systematically too high. In a rank-only system that
distortion is harmless — it's monotone, the ordering survives. Here the
auction prices on the absolute number, so the distortion must be undone:
either analytically (the correction factor is a simple function of the
sampling rate) or by an explicit **recalibration** step after training.
</ThinkFirst>

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

**Labels** come straight from the serving logs: an impression either got a
click or it didn't, and a click either led to a conversion or it didn't.
The click label is fast and clean. The conversion label is not — a purchase
can happen days after the click, which means recent impressions labeled
"no conversion" may simply be conversions *that haven't happened yet*.
This **delayed feedback** problem means naive training on recent data
systematically underestimates conversion rates; the follow-ups section
covers the standard fixes.

**Features** fall into three groups, and one structural trick. User
features (demographics, engagement history), ad features (creative
embedding, advertiser, category), and context features (placement, device,
time of day) describe the players. The structural trick is **cross
features** — explicit conjunctions like "this user's interest category ×
this ad's category" — because click behavior is dominated by
*interactions*: sports fans click sports ads, and no amount of independent
per-feature signal captures that as directly as the cross does. All ID
features become learned embeddings, and those tables are where the model's
size lives.

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective + optimization)*

Start with the baseline every ads team still respects: **logistic
regression over hashed cross features**. It was the industry workhorse for
a decade, it's nearly free to serve, and it sets a surprisingly high bar —
if your deep model can't beat it, your deep model is broken.

Production models improve on it by learning the feature interactions
instead of hand-specifying them: the **DLRM / Wide-and-Deep / DeepFM**
family. The shared shape: sparse IDs → embedding lookups; explicit
pairwise interactions between embeddings (the "wide" or factorization
part); a small MLP over the concatenation for higher-order interactions
(the "deep" part). It's an architecture designed backwards from the serving
budget — almost all the capacity is in cheap embedding lookups, with only a
tiny network of actual compute per candidate.

The **objective is logloss**, and that choice is load-bearing: logloss is a
proper scoring rule, meaning it's minimized by the true probabilities, so
optimizing it pushes the model *toward* calibration rather than away from
it. Even so, residual miscalibration survives (from downsampling,
regularization, and distribution shift), so a final **recalibration** layer
— Platt scaling or isotonic regression fit on a held-out slice — corrects
the output before the auction sees it.

<ThinkFirst question="The model emits pCTR = 0.03 for an ad. What consumes that number next, and what does that imply about the model's real job?">
The **auction** consumes it: ads are ranked by `bid × pCTR`, and the
winner's price is derived from the runner-up's expected value. So the model
is not producing a relevance score — it is producing one factor in a
*price*. Overestimate pCTR by 2× and the advertiser is overcharged by 2×.
The model's real job is honest probabilities; correct ordering is merely a
consequence of honesty.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Serving & the auction

Follow the request one more time, now with the machinery visible. Candidate
ads arrive; the server fetches user and ad features from a low-latency
cache; the model batch-scores all candidates in one pass; pacing rules and
price floors filter the set; the auction ranks by `bid × pCTR × quality`
and computes the winner's price from the runner-up. The quality term is the
platform protecting its long-term ecosystem — an annoying ad with a high
bid shouldn't always win, because user attrition costs more than the bid
pays.

<WikiLink slug="ad-auction-and-bidding" title="Ad auctions — second-price, GSP, VCG" />

<ThinkFirst question="Which two offline numbers must you track — and how can one improve while the other silently loses money?">
**AUC** (ranking quality) and **calibration** (ECE / reliability curves).
They are independent axes: a new model can order ads better *and*
systematically overpredict probabilities at the same time. Since
price = bid × pCTR, the calibration regression misprices every auction —
while the AUC dashboard glows green and everyone celebrates the launch.
Track both, sliced by segment, and gate launches on both.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

The offline story follows directly from the ThinkFirst above: **two
independent axes, both gating**. AUC and logloss measure ranking;
ECE and reliability curves measure calibration; and both must be sliced by
advertiser, placement, and category, because aggregate calibration can hide
a badly miscalibrated slice that misprices one advertiser's entire budget.

Online, the special wrinkle of ads A/B testing is that there are **two
customer populations** with opposed interests: users (who feel ad load and
relevance) and advertisers (who feel ROI). A model change can raise
platform revenue while quietly destroying a segment of advertisers' return
— so experiments track revenue *and* advertiser ROI, with user-experience
guardrails alongside. Before spending live traffic at all,
**counterfactual replay** — re-running logged auctions under the new model
to estimate what would have changed — cheaply filters out clearly bad
candidates.

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

The alarm that matters most in production is **calibration drift by
slice**. Aggregate calibration can hold steady while one advertiser
category drifts badly — and unlike a ranking regression, which shows up as
engagement loss, calibration drift is *silent*: nothing visibly breaks,
money just moves incorrectly. Per-slice reliability monitoring is the only
thing that catches it.

The second feedback problem is the delayed-conversion label from section 4:
as conversions trickle in over the attribution window, previously-negative
labels flip positive, and the training pipeline must reconcile them —
closing the loop back into **data**. Systems that retrain frequently for
freshness feel this hardest, since their most recent training data is
exactly the data whose labels are least mature.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Calibrated probability + auction | Rank-only score | Pricing and pacing consume the absolute number, not the order |
| DLRM-style (embeddings + crosses) | Large transformer per ad | The 10ms ÷ hundreds-of-ads arithmetic; the model runs billions of times/day |
| Negative downsampling + recalibration | Train on all negatives | Far cheaper, and the distortion is exactly correctable |
| Logloss objective | Direct AUC optimization | Logloss is proper → drives calibration; AUC is blind to it |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="AUC went up but revenue dropped. What happened?">
Almost always **calibration**. The new model ranks better but its
probabilities are shifted, so the auction over- or under-prices relative to
true value — and pricing errors flow straight to revenue. Check ECE and
per-slice reliability curves; recalibrate with isotonic regression before
concluding the model itself is bad.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you handle delayed conversions in the label?">
Treat "no conversion yet" as **censored, not negative**. Options: a
delayed-feedback model that jointly learns conversion probability and
conversion *delay*; or wait out a fixed attribution window before trusting
negatives and backfill labels as conversions arrive. Naively labeling
recent impressions negative biases predicted conversion rates downward —
worst exactly where you want freshness.
</Details>

<Details summary="A brand-new ad has no history — how is it priced?">
Cold start runs on content: creative embeddings, advertiser history, and
category priors give an initial pCTR without any impressions. Then an
**exploration budget** deliberately grants the new ad early impressions to
gather real signal — classic explore/exploit, but bounded and accounted
for, because in ads every exploratory impression has a literal price.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- The model's output is **a factor in a price**, not a relevance score —
  so it must be a **calibrated probability**, and calibration is a
  first-class metric, monitored per slice, forever.
- Latency arithmetic (hundreds of ads, 10ms, billions of requests/day)
  picks the architecture: **embedding-heavy, compute-light** DLRM-style
  models, not large networks.
- **AUC and calibration are independent axes** — a ranking win with a
  calibration regression loses money silently. Gate launches on both.
- Training tricks that distort probabilities (negative downsampling) are
  fine **only because** an explicit recalibration step undoes them; delayed
  conversions mean recent labels are censored, not negative.
