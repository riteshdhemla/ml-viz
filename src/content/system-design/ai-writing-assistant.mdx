# Design an AI Writing Assistant

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="Generative AI System Design"
  company="Jasper / Notion AI"
  scale="long-form · brand-voice control · grounded · preference-tuned"
  difficulty="intermediate"
/>

A marketing manager opens the writing assistant, uploads brand
guidelines and two product spec sheets, and asks for a 2,000-word blog
post — in the company's voice, at an eighth-grade reading level, with
every product claim tied to the specs. Any frontier model can produce
fluent prose about the topic in one second. That is precisely why
fluency is worth nothing here: the product's job is everything fluency
doesn't cover.

Hold that thought, because it's the design thesis of this case. The
base model is a commodity — the same APIs are available to every
competitor. What a writing *product* sells is **control** (this
brand's voice, this format, reliably — not "usually"), **grounding**
(claims tied to the user's sources, because a hallucinated statistic
in a customer's published post damages *their* reputation, not the
vendor's), and **long-form coherence** (2,000 words that don't drift
or repeat). The system-design work is steering and constraining a
strong base model, then tuning it on human preference until it
matches this product's particular notion of good writing — which is
why the case reads through the ML loop's **objective → evaluation →
feedback** stages.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
The concepts this case builds on:

- **Decoding & sampling** — temperature, top-p, and friends: the
  knobs that trade determinism against creativity at generation time;
  from the NLP course's decoding lesson.
- **RAG** — retrieving the user's documents into context so
  generation can cite and stay faithful to them, rather than relying
  on the model's parametric memory.
- **LoRA fine-tuning** — cheap low-rank adapters that specialize a
  base model (here: one adapter per brand voice) without retraining
  it; from the fine-tuning course.
- **RLHF / DPO** — tuning a model on human preference pairs ("this
  draft over that one") so its outputs drift toward what people
  actually choose; the mechanism behind the feedback loop below.
- **Constrained decoding** — restricting generation so output
  *provably* matches a format (schema, section structure), rather
  than asking nicely in the prompt.
- **Faithfulness** — whether generated claims are supported by the
  provided sources; measured with
  [LLM-as-judge](/wiki/llm-as-judge) plus human audits.
- **Plan-then-write** — generating an outline first, then expanding
  sections against it; the standard structure for long-form
  coherence.
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

The goal: help users produce high-quality long-form text —
marketing copy, blog posts, emails, docs — in a **controlled** style
and format, grounded in provided material, faster than writing it
themselves.

The control axes are the product surface: tone, brand voice, length,
format and structure, target audience. Each must be *reliable* — a
voice that holds for nine paragraphs and slips in the tenth is a
support ticket. Grounding is the trust surface: for factual content,
claims must trace to the user's sources with citations, and where the
sources are silent, the assistant should hedge or omit rather than
invent. Scope: the generation product — control, grounding,
long-form structure, and the feedback loop that improves it.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
The differentiator isn't the model — it's **controllability** and
**grounding**. Frame the design as steering and constraining a strong
base model, then tuning it with preference data, and you've framed
the whole case correctly in one sentence.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & constraints

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Reliable tone/brand/format | Structured prompting, style conditioning, fine-tuning |
| Grounding | Faithful to sources; cite | RAG over user material; faithfulness checks |
| Long-form | Coherent multi-section docs | Outline-then-expand; section planning |
| Quality | Matches human preference | RLHF/DPO on preference data |

<ThinkFirst question="Any frontier model already writes fluent prose. So where does a writing product actually add value?">
In everything fluency doesn't cover. **Reliable control** — this
brand's voice, this format, every time; "usually on-brand" is a
failing grade for a professional tool. **Grounding** — claims tied to
the user's sources, because the hallucinated statistic ships under
the *customer's* name. **Long-form structure** — a 2,000-word piece
that holds its thread. The base model is a commodity available to
every competitor; the control system wrapped around it is the
product.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. GenAI problem framing

Formally: **controllable, grounded long-form generation** on top of a
base LLM — and each adjective maps to its own mechanism, which is the
clean way to present it. *Control* comes from three escalating tools:
structured prompting (system prompt plus style exemplars), optional
fine-tuning on a brand's corpus where prompting isn't reliable
enough, and decoding choices. *Grounding* comes from RAG over the
user's sources. *Long-form coherence* comes from **plan-then-write**
— outline first, then sections — rather than one long unstructured
generation.

The pre-generative baseline makes the value concrete: template
libraries (rigid — every output looks like the template) or human
copywriters (excellent but slow and expensive). Generative writing
adapts to any request; the engineering challenge is keeping it
on-brand and truthful while it does.

## 4. Model & generation *(spine: objective)*

The model strategy is **base + light adaptation**: a strong
instruction-tuned base model, with **LoRA adapters** fine-tuned per
brand where prompting alone can't hold the voice. Adapters, not full
fine-tunes, because the economics demand it — one adapter per
customer brand is cheap to train, cheap to store, and easy to update
when the brand guidelines change.

**Decoding is a product control surface**, not an internal detail:
lower temperature for factual, brand-safe copy; higher for ideation
and headline brainstorming — often literally exposed to the user as a
"creativity" slider. The **structure** strategy is plan-then-write:
outline → section drafts conditioned on the outline and prior
sections → a revision pass for consistency, which both keeps 2,000
words coherent and gives the user a steering point at the outline
stage, before any prose exists to be attached to.

<SamplingViz />

<ThinkFirst question="A customer uploads brand guidelines and source documents. Through what mechanisms do those actually shape the generated text?">
Three mechanisms, in increasing strength — and knowing the escalation
order is the design skill. **Retrieval**: the sources and guidelines
are pulled into context with the requirement to cite — cheap,
instant, works for facts. **Style conditioning**: voice exemplars and
explicit constraints (tone, reading level, format) in the prompt —
good enough for most brands. **A LoRA adapter** fine-tuned on the
brand's corpus — for when the voice is distinctive enough that
prompting keeps slipping. And orthogonal to all three: format
guarantees come from **constrained decoding**, because a schema
enforced at generation time holds where a polite prompt instruction
merely usually holds.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Grounding & control

The grounding pipeline in one pass: retrieve the user's sources and
guidelines into context; generate with citations attached to claims;
then *verify* — a faithfulness check that each claim is actually
supported by its cited source, with unsupported claims hedged,
flagged, or dropped. The verification step is what separates
"grounded" as a marketing word from grounded as a property: retrieval
puts the sources in the context window, but only the check confirms
the model actually stayed inside them.

Control mirrors it: style conditioning (exemplars and constraints, or
the brand adapter) shapes the prose, and structured output —
sections, bullet limits, JSON for structured documents — is enforced
by constrained decoding rather than requested and hoped for.

<WikiLink slug="text-generation-metrics" title="Text-generation quality metrics" />

<ThinkFirst question="Fluency is table stakes — every draft reads well. What do you actually measure to know the product is working?">
The things fluency hides. **Control adherence**: did the output match
the requested tone, length, and format — much of which is a
mechanically checkable constraint. **Faithfulness**: is every claim
supported by the provided sources — checkable by judge plus audit.
And the bluntest, best product signal of all: **edit rate** — how
much of the draft users rewrite before shipping it. A beautiful draft
that gets 80% rewritten failed, and no offline metric will tell you;
the edit distance will.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

The measurement stack, from model-side to product-side. **Human
preference and LLM-as-judge** carry overall quality: win-rate of the
new version against the old on matched prompts, with the judge
calibrated against human raters. **Faithfulness** is scored
claim-by-claim against sources — the grounded-generation gate.
**Control adherence** is checked as the constraint it is: tone via
rubric-based judging, length and format mechanically. And the
**product metrics** — acceptance rate and edit rate — are the ground
truth that keeps the offline metrics honest: when judge scores rise
but edit rates don't fall, the judge has drifted from what users
value, and it's the judge that's wrong.

## 7. Feedback & operations *(spine: feedback)*

The feedback loop is this product's compounding moat: every user
edit, regeneration, and thumbs-up/down is a preference signal, and
aggregated preference data feeds **RLHF/DPO tuning** plus prompt
improvements — so the model converges on *this product's* notion of
good writing, which a competitor calling the same base API doesn't
have. User edits are especially rich: they say not just "this draft
lost" but *what the winning draft looked like*.

Operationally: streaming for perceived responsiveness, model tiering
(a fast model for drafts and ideation, a stronger one for final
polish), and caching of retrieved brand material per customer. The
guardrails are content-product-shaped: plagiarism and
verbatim-source checks (the assistant must not launder a source
document into "original" copy), disallowed-content filters, and
AI-assistance disclosure where required.

## 8. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Plan-then-write | One long generation | Long-form coherence and a user steering point at the outline |
| Prompt + LoRA for voice | Full fine-tune | Cheap, adapter-per-brand, easy to update |
| RAG grounding | Rely on parametric knowledge | Faithfulness, freshness, citeability |
| Preference (RLHF/DPO) tuning | Prompt-only | Compounds user feedback into the product's own quality bar |

## 9. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Output is fluent but off-brand. How do you enforce voice?">
Escalate through the control stack: brand-voice exemplars and explicit
style constraints in the prompt first; if the voice still slips, a
LoRA adapter fine-tuned on the brand's corpus — prompting steers, but
an adapter *bakes in*. Just as important: measure voice adherence
explicitly (rubric-based LLM-judge calibrated with human ratings) so
"off-brand" is caught in evaluation before shipping, not discovered
by the customer's audience.
</Details>

<Details summary="The assistant invents statistics in a factual article. Fix?">
Close the grounding loop end to end: retrieve the user's sources into
context, require claims to be cited, then run the faithfulness check
— judge each claim against its cited source — and for anything
unsupported, hedge or omit rather than fabricate. The underlying
dynamic to name: ungrounded long-form generation is a hallucination
magnet, because plausible-sounding specifics are exactly what fluent
models produce under pressure to be concrete. RAG plus a faithfulness
gate is the standard remedy, and the gate is the part teams skip.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you keep a 2000-word piece coherent and on-topic?">
Structure over stamina: plan-then-write. Generate an outline, expand
each section conditioned on the outline *and* the sections already
written, then run a revision pass for cross-section consistency. A
single monolithic generation drifts and repeats because nothing holds
it to a global plan — the outline is that plan, and as a bonus it's
where the user can redirect the piece before any prose exists.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- The base model is a commodity; the product is **control, grounding,
  and long-form structure** — everything fluency doesn't cover.
- Control escalates: **prompt conditioning → LoRA voice adapters →
  constrained decoding** — each stronger and costlier than the last,
  applied as reliability demands.
- Grounding is a *verified* property: **RAG puts sources in context;
  the faithfulness gate proves the model stayed inside them**.
- Measure what fluency hides — **control adherence, faithfulness, and
  edit rate** — and compound user preference into the model with
  RLHF/DPO, because that feedback loop is the moat.
