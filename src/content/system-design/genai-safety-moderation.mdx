# Design a GenAI Safety & Output Moderation System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="Generative AI System Design"
  company="cross-industry"
  scale="every generation · low added latency · adversarial · policy-driven"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

Every other case in this track mentions its safety layer in a section
or two. This case *is* the safety layer — the cross-cutting system
that wraps any generative model (text, image, audio, video) and
decides three things for every single generation: is this prompt
allowed, may this output be returned, and how is the returned content
marked as AI-generated.

Three properties make it a distinctive design problem. It's
**adversarial**: unlike a spam filter facing indifferent traffic, a
meaningful slice of users is *actively trying to defeat this system*,
with unlimited retries and a community sharing techniques — so any
static defense loses on a timescale of weeks. It's **policy-driven**:
"allowed" isn't a fact but a taxonomy that evolves, with wildly
different error costs per category — the threshold for self-harm
content and the threshold for mild profanity should not be the same
number. And it's **ubiquitous with a latency budget**: it runs on
every generation of the products it protects, so it must add
almost nothing to latency or the product teams it serves will route
around it. The design answer to the first property is the case's
backbone: **defense in depth** — because no single layer survives an
adversary, the system is a stack of layers whose misses don't
overlap. It reads through the agentic loop's **guardrails** and
**evaluation** stages.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
The concepts this case builds on:

- **Jailbreaks** — prompts crafted to make a model violate its policy
  (role-play framings, encoding tricks, many-shot setups);
  **prompt injection** — instructions smuggled in via content the
  model processes — see
  [prompt injection & defenses](/wiki/prompt-injection-attacks-and-defenses).
- **Moderation classifiers** — small, fast models (fine-tuned
  encoders or distilled LLMs) scoring content against policy
  categories; cheap enough to run on everything.
- **RLHF / DPO & reward models** — alignment training that teaches
  the base model itself to refuse; a **reward/safety model** scores
  outputs for policy compliance, and appears twice in this design
  (training-time and serving-time). From the fine-tuning course.
- **Precision / recall & operating points** — the classification
  vocabulary this case reuses: safety recall (harm caught) vs.
  over-refusal (benign blocked), with per-category thresholds.
- **Red-teaming & attack success rate** — systematically attacking
  your own system; ASR is the fraction of attack attempts that get
  through — the headline robustness metric.
- **Watermarking & C2PA provenance** — invisible marks and signed
  metadata identifying content as AI-generated downstream; the
  accountability channel, distinct from blocking.
- **Distillation** — compressing a large model's behavior into a
  small fast one; how a heavyweight safety model becomes a
  serving-time classifier.
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

The goal, stated with its tension intact: prevent the model from
producing disallowed content and from being manipulated into it —
**without wrecking latency and without over-blocking benign use**.
That second clause is not a nicety; a safety layer that refuses
legitimate medical questions or blocks a novelist's dark scene is
failing at its job just as surely as one that leaks harm, and this
dual failure surface runs through every section below.

The adversarial framing deserves its own sentence in an interview:
this is an *arms race*, so the deliverable is not a filter but a
**loop** — defenses, measurement of attack success, and a pipeline
that turns today's successful attacks into tomorrow's training data.
Scope: the safety layer around any generation model; the policy
taxonomy itself is an input (written by policy teams), but the
system must make it enforceable, versionable, and auditable.

<Callout type="warning" title="Defense-in-depth, not one classifier">
No single filter is robust against a determined adversary with
unlimited retries. The design is layers — input moderation, a
hardened system policy, output moderation, and model-level alignment
— each catching what the others miss.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & constraints

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Coverage | Block disallowed in + out | Input + output classifiers per modality |
| Latency | Minimal added latency | Small fast classifiers; parallel checks |
| Robustness | Resist jailbreaks/injection | Adversarial training; layered defenses |
| Precision | Don't over-block benign use | Calibrated, per-category thresholds |
| Auditability | Explain + appeal decisions | Logging, reason codes, human review |

<ThinkFirst question="Your adversary gets unlimited retries against a static defense. What layers would you stack — and what does each catch that the others miss?">
Four layers, each with a distinct miss-population — which is the
whole reason stacking works. **Input moderation** catches overtly
disallowed *requests*, cheaply, before a generation is paid for — but
misses cleverly framed ones. **The hardened system policy** resists
*manipulation* (jailbreak framings, injected instructions) that
slipped past the input check — but no prompt armor is perfect.
**Output moderation** catches disallowed *generations* however they
were induced — the backstop that doesn't care how the attack worked —
but judges only what it can see in the output. **Model-level
alignment** makes the model itself refuse even when every wrapper
fails — the layer that's hardest to route around because it *is* the
model. Uncorrelated misses are the design goal; a single layer, however
good, loses the retry game.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. The layered pipeline *(spine: guardrails)*

The ThinkFirst's stack, in request order around each generation:

1. **Input moderation** — classify the prompt per policy category;
   block clearly disallowed requests before any generation cost is
   spent.
2. **Hardened system policy** — the model's instructions, structured
   so that user text and retrieved content cannot override them; the
   front line against jailbreaks and injection.
3. **Output moderation** — classify the generation (text toxicity,
   image NSFW and real-person likeness, per modality) before
   returning; block or regenerate on failure.
4. **Provenance** — watermark and tag what *is* returned (invisible
   marks, C2PA metadata), so generated content stays identifiable
   after it leaves the platform.

Note the asymmetry of layers 1 and 3: input moderation is an
*economy* measure as much as a safety one (don't pay to generate
what you'll block), while output moderation is the *authoritative*
gate — it judges what was actually produced, not what was asked for.

## 4. The classifiers & alignment

The **moderation classifiers** are deliberately small: per-category
fine-tuned encoders or distilled LLMs, sized to add single-digit
milliseconds. Per-category matters because the taxonomy demands
different operating points per harm class — one monolithic
"bad/not-bad" score can't express "maximum recall on CSAM,
precision-favoring on profanity."

**Model-level alignment** is the deepest layer: RLHF/DPO training
with a reward/safety model teaches the base model to refuse
appropriately *on its own*. And the reward model leads a double life
worth naming in an interview: at training time it aligns the base
model; distilled, it becomes one of the serving-time output
classifiers — the same learned notion of "policy-compliant" applied
at both ends.

**Humans** complete the system: uncertain cases and appeals route to
reviewers, and every reviewer decision becomes a training label —
the labeled data pipeline is the same machinery as the classic
content-moderation case, pointed at generations instead of uploads.

<RewardModelViz />

<ThinkFirst question="Safety recall hits 99.9%. Is the system good? What's the other side of the ledger?">
**Over-refusal.** Recall alone is trivially maximized — block
everything — and a safety layer that refuses benign medical
questions, legitimate security education, or dark-but-lawful creative
writing is silently killing the product while its recall dashboard
gleams. The honest objective is a calibrated operating point *per
category*: maximum recall where errors are catastrophic and
irreversible, precision-favoring where over-blocking mostly harms
legitimate use. Which means false positives are measured with the
same rigor as misses — a benign-prompt eval suite beside the
red-team suite, always.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

Four measurements, mapping to the two failure surfaces plus
robustness. **Safety recall** per category — the fraction of
genuinely disallowed content caught, held highest where harm is
catastrophic. **Over-refusal rate** — benign requests wrongly
blocked, measured on a curated benign-but-edgy suite (medical,
security education, fiction), because these are exactly the prompts
naive filters kill. **Attack success rate** on red-team and jailbreak
benchmarks — the headline robustness number, tracked over time as
attacks evolve. And **calibration audits**: LLM-as-judge plus human
review over sampled decisions, keeping the automated metrics honest.

<WikiLink slug="llm-as-judge" title="LLM-as-a-judge for safety scoring" />

<ThinkFirst question="Every generation now passes through multiple safety checks. How do you keep that from doubling latency?">
Make the checks small and concurrent rather than fewer. Distilled
per-category classifiers (never frontier models in the hot path);
**input checks run in parallel with prompt prefill**, not before it;
**output checks stream over tokens as they're generated**, so the
verdict is nearly ready when the last token lands; verdict caching
for repeated content. The design constraint behind the engineering:
safety that doubles latency gets quietly bypassed by the product
teams it serves — cheapness isn't an optimization here, it's what
keeps the safety layer *deployed*.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Operations & feedback

Operations is where the arms race actually runs. The
**red-teaming loop** is continuous: internal teams and external
programs attack the system, every successful technique becomes
training data for classifiers and alignment, and the attack-success
dashboard shows whether defense is keeping pace — a rising ASR
trend is the pager-worthy signal.

**Policy versioning and audit** make the system governable: every
decision logs with reason codes; policy and thresholds are versioned
together (a decision is only explicable against the policy version
that made it); appeals overturn *and relabel*, feeding corrections
back into training. And live **monitoring** watches both failure
surfaces at once — attack success and over-refusal — because a
defensive change that fixes one frequently regresses the other, and
only paired dashboards catch the trade.

## 7. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Layered defense-in-depth | Single moderation model | No one layer survives unlimited adversarial retries |
| Separate fast classifiers | Rely on model self-refusal only | Classifiers catch what alignment misses, and vice-versa |
| Calibrated per-category thresholds | One global threshold | Error costs differ hugely by category |
| Watermark + provenance | No marking | Downstream accountability & deepfake mitigation |

## 8. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Users jailbreak the model with role-play prompts. How do you respond?">
As an arms-race iteration, through every layer at once: harden the
system policy against the framing; add the pattern to adversarially
trained input/output classifiers; and fold it into alignment training
so the model refuses regardless of the wrapper. Then verify the fix
where it counts — attack success rate on the red-team suite — and
keep the pipeline that turns each new successful jailbreak into
training data. A fixed rule set loses this game; the loop is the
defense.
</Details>

<Details summary="Safety is blocking legitimate requests (e.g. medical questions). Fix?">
Treat over-refusal as the first-class failure it is. Calibrate the
per-category thresholds (the medical category's operating point
shouldn't be inherited from the weapons category), refine the policy
granularity — allow safe medical information while blocking dangerous
instructions, which is a policy distinction, not a threshold tweak —
and put the benign-edge-case suite into standing evaluation so
regressions surface before users do. A safety layer tuned only for
recall strangles the product it protects; the balance is explicit and
measured, never a side effect.
</Details>

<Details summary="Why watermark outputs if classifiers already block bad content?">
Different jobs on different timelines. Classifiers gate what leaves
the system *now*; watermarking and provenance make what does leave
**identifiable as AI-generated later** — for deepfake attribution,
platform enforcement, and regulatory compliance downstream, long
after the content has left your infrastructure. Blocking prevents;
provenance enables accountability for the (inevitable) content that
was allowed, or that slipped through. Complements, not redundancy.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- GenAI safety is a **defense-in-depth pipeline** — input moderation,
  hardened policy, output moderation, model-level alignment — because
  against an adversary with unlimited retries, any single layer
  loses; uncorrelated misses are the design goal.
- The objective has **two failure surfaces**: safety recall *and*
  over-refusal, calibrated per policy category — a system tuned only
  to catch harm silently kills the product it protects.
- It's an **arms race run as a loop**: continuous red-teaming, attack
  success rate as the robustness metric, and every successful attack
  recycled into training data.
- **Cheapness is a safety property** (a slow safety layer gets
  bypassed), and **provenance complements blocking** — one prevents,
  the other makes what's generated accountable downstream.
