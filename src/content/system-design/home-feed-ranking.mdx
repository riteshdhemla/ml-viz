# Design a Home-Feed Ranking System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Pinterest / Netflix"
  scale="300M+ users · billions of items · <200ms budget"
  difficulty="intermediate"
/>

A user opens the app. Within 200 milliseconds, the service must choose,
from **billions** of candidate items, the few dozen this particular
person is most likely to care about right now — and it must do this ten
thousand times per second, for hundreds of millions of users, while the
item catalog and everyone's interests shift under it continuously.

"Design the home feed" is the canonical ML system-design interview, and
the classic trap is reaching for a model in the first sentence. The
strong answer works through the **ML project loop** in order — data →
hypothesis space → objective → optimization → evaluation → feedback —
treating every choice as *which slot am I filling, and what breaks if I
skip it?* Read that way, the feed has one governing insight: **the
latency arithmetic, not any modeling preference, dictates the entire
architecture.** Everything else in this case unpacks the consequences of
that. The `SpineNav` strip above marks the stages this problem leans on
hardest.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
The recommender-systems vocabulary this case assumes:

- **Retrieval → ranking funnel** — the two-stage architecture where a
  cheap model narrows billions of items to ~1000 candidates and an
  expensive model ranks those. This case derives *why* it must exist.
- **Two-tower model** — a retrieval architecture with separate user and
  item encoders meeting only at a dot product, so item vectors can be
  precomputed and indexed.
- **ANN (approximate nearest neighbor) search** — finding the closest
  vectors to a query without comparing against all of them; HNSW is the
  standard graph-based index — see [HNSW](/wiki/hnsw) and
  [vector databases](/wiki/vector-databases).
- **Implicit vs. explicit feedback** — clicks, saves, dwell time, and
  hides (implicit, abundant, biased) versus star ratings (explicit,
  sparse). Feeds train almost entirely on implicit signals.
- **Position bias** — items shown higher get more clicks *because of
  where they sat*, so logged clicks conflate relevance with placement.
- **Training-serving skew** — features computed differently in the
  training pipeline vs. the serving path, so the model meets inputs in
  production it never saw in training; a feature store's reason to exist.
- **NDCG / Recall@k / AUC** — offline ranking metrics: how much of the
  known-relevant set the system surfaced, and how well-ordered the list is.
- **A/B testing & guardrail metrics** — online experiments comparing
  variants on the true objective, with secondary metrics that must not
  regress (latency, diversity, hide rate).
- **Inverse-propensity weighting (IPS)** — re-weighting logged feedback
  by how likely the old system was to show each item, to de-bias
  training and off-policy estimates.
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

Pin down scope with the interviewer before designing anything. The feed
is a ranked, infinite-scroll list of items — pins, videos, posts —
personalized per user and refreshed each session. The scale: 300M+
users, billions of items, heavily skewed popularity, and a constant
stream of brand-new users and items on both sides.

The subtle part is **what we optimize**. The naive answer — clicks — is
a trap worth naming out loud: click-through is easy to game with
clickbait, and a feed that maximizes clicks degrades into outrage and
thumbnails. The real objective is **long-term engagement** — return
visits, time well spent — which is hard to measure per-impression, so
the system optimizes trainable proxies (clicks, saves, dwell) while the
evaluation layer keeps watch that the proxies still track the goal.
That objective/proxy gap runs through the whole case.

Out of scope: ads ranking (a separate system with auction economics —
see the ad-CTR case), notifications, and search.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
Naming the objective as *long-term engagement* and immediately flagging
the proxy-metric risk (CTR → clickbait) is the single highest-signal
thing you can say in the first two minutes.
</Callout>

<ThinkFirst question="Billions of candidate items, a 200ms end-to-end budget. Before any modeling — what does the arithmetic alone force on the architecture?">
Divide the budget by the scale: at 10k QPS with 200ms per request, you
can afford to run an expensive model over a few *hundred* candidates per
request — not billions, not millions. So something much cheaper must
first shrink billions to hundreds. The arithmetic forces a **funnel** —
a cheap retrieval stage feeding an expensive ranking stage — before
you've discussed a single model.
</ThinkFirst>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence for the design |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | < 200 ms p99 end-to-end | Rules out scoring billions of items directly — forces a funnel |
| Throughput | ~10k feed requests/sec at peak | Ranker must be cheap per item; heavy work moves offline |
| Freshness | New items rankable within minutes | Streaming feature pipeline, not nightly batch |
| Candidate pool | Billions of items | Approximate nearest-neighbor retrieval, not brute force |
| Availability | 99.9% | Graceful fallback to a non-personalized feed |

The back-of-envelope from the ThinkFirst is worth writing on the
whiteboard explicitly, because every later section inherits from it:
a few hundred candidates through the heavy model per request is the
budget, and the funnel is what makes the budget achievable.

## 3. ML problem framing

The feed is not one model — it's a **multi-stage pipeline**, and each
stage is a different ML problem filling a different slot:

1. **Retrieval** *(hypothesis space)*: from billions of items, cheaply
   fetch ~1000 candidates plausibly relevant to this user — framed as
   **approximate nearest-neighbor search** in a learned embedding space.
2. **Ranking** *(hypothesis space + objective)*: score those ~1000 with
   an expensive model predicting engagement — framed as learning-to-rank
   / pointwise probability prediction.
3. **Re-ranking**: apply what the models don't know — diversity rules,
   freshness boosts, dedup, policy filters.

What was breaking before this shape? A single ranker over all items
blows the latency budget by orders of magnitude, and the cheap
alternative — a global popularity list — isn't personalized at all. The
funnel exists to make *personalized* scoring *tractable*: the retrieval
stage sacrifices some precision to buy the ranking stage the small
candidate set it needs.

<ThinkFirst question="Nobody rates their feed. Where do the training labels come from — and what's the catch?">
From **implicit feedback**: impressions, clicks, saves, dwell time,
hides. It's abundant — every session generates labels — but biased in a
structural way: users can only engage with what the *old* model chose to
show them. Train naively on those logs and the new model just relearns
yesterday's policy, confidently. Breaking the circle requires correcting
for the logging policy — inverse-propensity weighting, or reserving a
small randomized-exposure slice of traffic that sees unranked items.
</ThinkFirst>

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

Labels are the implicit signals just described, but they aren't
interchangeable — they form a **quality ladder**. A click might be an
accident or clickbait success; a long dwell means actual attention; a
save is a deliberate act of value; a hide is an explicit negative.
Weighting positives by engagement depth pushes the model toward the
long-term objective rather than raw clickiness. Impressions without
action serve as negatives — handled carefully, because an unclicked item
at position 40 may simply never have been seen (position bias again).

Features come in three groups: **user** (long-term interest embeddings
built from history, short-term session interests), **item** (content
embeddings, age, popularity stats), and **context** (time of day,
device, what the user has already seen this session). Two properties of
the feature pipeline matter as much as the features themselves. First,
**train/serve consistency**: the same code path must compute features
for training and serving — the feature store exists to enforce this —
or you get the classic production bug where offline metrics look great
and the live model underperforms mysteriously. Second, **freshness**:
velocity features (engagement in the last N minutes) must stream, or a
minutes-old viral item is invisible to the ranker.

<Callout type="warning" title="Position & popularity bias">
Logged feedback is biased by what the old model showed and *where* it
showed it. Correct for it — inverse-propensity weighting, or a small
randomized-exposure slice — or the model simply relearns its own past
behavior with extra confidence.
</Callout>

<ThinkFirst question="Retrieval has ~40ms to find 1000 relevant items among billions. What shape must the model take for that to even be possible?">
User and item features can't interact *inside* the model at query time —
that would mean running the model once per candidate, i.e. billions of
times. The only shape that works: encode user and item **separately**
into the same vector space, so item embeddings are precomputed and
indexed offline, and query-time work collapses to one user-tower forward
pass plus an ANN lookup. That constraint is exactly why the two-tower
architecture exists — and why cross-attention retrieval, however
accurate, is off the table at this stage.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective + optimization)*

**Retrieval — the two-tower model.** A user tower and an item tower each
map their inputs into the same $d$-dimensional space, and relevance is
just a dot product:

$$
s(u, i) = \mathbf{e}_u^\top \mathbf{e}_i
$$

The dot product is the entire point: because user and item only meet at
that final operation, all item embeddings can be computed offline and
indexed, exactly as the ThinkFirst derived. Pedagogically (and
historically) the two-tower model is a deep generalization of **matrix
factorization** — same dot-product structure, but with feature-driven
encoders replacing free per-ID vectors, which is what lets it embed
brand-new items from content alone:

<MatrixFactorizationViz />

**Ranking — a deep engagement model.** The ~1000 retrieved candidates
flow through a richer model — DLRM/DeepFM-style: sparse-feature
embeddings, explicit feature crosses, an MLP — free to use every
expensive interaction feature the two-tower stage had to forgo, because
it only runs on hundreds of items. Build the logistic-regression
baseline first: it proves the pipeline end-to-end, and it tells you how
much the depth is actually buying.

## 6. Serving & scaling

The 200ms budget, allocated:

| Stage | Budget | How |
|---|---|---|
| Feature fetch | ~30 ms | Feature store, cached user features |
| Retrieval (ANN) | ~40 ms | HNSW over precomputed item embeddings; multiple sources merged |
| Ranking | ~80 ms | Batch-score ~1000 candidates on GPU/optimized CPU |
| Re-rank + assemble | ~20 ms | Diversity, dedup, policy filters |

The unglamorous machinery that makes the table true: aggressive caching
of user embeddings and item metadata, item embeddings maintained by a
nightly batch job plus a streaming path for new items, and a **graceful
degradation** story — if the ranker times out, serve a popularity feed
rather than an error. A feed that's occasionally generic is fine; a feed
that's occasionally blank is an incident.

<WikiLink slug="vector-databases" title="Vector databases — serving the ANN index" />

<ThinkFirst question="Your new ranker lifts offline NDCG by 3%. Do you ship it?">
Not yet. Offline gains are computed on logged data the *old* model
produced — position bias, training-serving skew, and proxy-metric
mismatch can each manufacture a fake win, and the follow-ups section
has a real example of each. Offline metrics select which candidates
*deserve* an A/B test; the **A/B test on long-term engagement**, with
guardrails watching, is the only judge that ships models.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

The evaluation story is a hierarchy of increasingly expensive, increasingly
trustworthy signals. **Offline** ranking metrics — Recall@k for
retrieval, NDCG@k and AUC/logloss for the ranker — are cheap and run on
every experiment, but they inherit every bias in the logs; treat them as
necessary, never sufficient. **Counterfactual estimators** (IPS-based
off-policy evaluation) sit in the middle: they use the logged propensities
to estimate what a new policy *would* have done, cheaply screening
candidates before live traffic is spent. **Online A/B tests** are the
verdict — run on the real objective (long-term engagement, retention),
and always with **guardrail metrics**: dwell quality, hide rate, creator
diversity, latency. The guardrails are what catch the model that wins
CTR by quietly flooding the feed with clickbait — the proxy-gap failure
from section 1 made measurable.

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

Once live, the system becomes its own data source — production feedback
is tomorrow's training data — which makes the feedback stage a design
surface, not an afterthought. Three standing concerns. **Drift**: user
interests and the item inventory shift constantly, so feature and
prediction distributions are monitored and the ranker retrains daily or
continuously, with embeddings refreshed as new items arrive. **Cold
start**: new items enter retrieval via content-based embeddings (no
engagement history needed) plus a bounded exploration boost to gather
signal; new users get a popularity/onboarding feed until they accrue
history. **Loop integrity**: the randomized-exposure slice from section
4 stays on permanently — it's the system's only unbiased window on the
world outside its own recommendations.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why the funnel wins here |
|---|---|---|
| Two-stage funnel | One big ranker over all items | Latency budget makes full scoring impossible |
| Two-tower retrieval | Cross-attention retrieval | Towers allow precomputed item embeddings + ANN; cross-attention can't scale to billions |
| Implicit feedback | Explicit ratings | Implicit is abundant; explicit is sparse and biased in its own ways |
| Daily retrain | Fully online learning | Simpler, more stable; online learning adds infra + failure modes |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="How do you stop the feed from becoming a filter bubble?">
Make diversity an explicit objective rather than hoping for it: a
diversity term in re-ranking (determinantal point processes, or a
simple category/creator quota), plus reserved exploration slots that
show items outside the user's established interests. Critically, track
creator- and topic-diversity as guardrail metrics in every A/B test —
otherwise a pure-engagement win that collapses diversity ships before
anyone notices.
</Details>

<Details summary="A viral item is 10 minutes old — how does it get ranked?">
Three mechanisms, one per stage. Retrieval: the item gets a
content-based embedding at ingest, so the ANN index can surface it with
zero engagement history. Ranking: streaming velocity features
(engagement in the last few minutes) reach the ranker within minutes.
Re-ranking: an exploration boost buys it initial impressions so real
signal accumulates. Together they close the loop from upload to
rankable in minutes — which is what the freshness requirement in
section 2 was really asking for.
</Details>

<Details summary="Offline NDCG went up but the A/B test is flat. What happened?">
The usual suspects, in checking order: **feature parity** — training-
serving skew means the model sees different features online (check this
first; it's the most common). **Bias** — the offline metric rewarded
position/popularity patterns users don't actually value. **Objective
mismatch** — you optimized clicks, the A/B measured dwell or retention.
**Leakage** — the offline eval let future information into features.
This is why offline metrics nominate and A/B tests decide.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- **The latency budget is the architecture**: 200ms over billions of
  items forces the retrieval → ranking funnel before any modeling
  discussion begins.
- **Two-tower retrieval** exists because of a shape constraint — user
  and item must encode separately so item vectors can be precomputed and
  ANN-indexed.
- Labels are **biased implicit feedback**: weight them by engagement
  depth, correct for position and logging-policy bias, and keep a
  randomized slice as the unbiased window.
- **Optimize long-term engagement, not clicks** — and let A/B tests with
  guardrail metrics, not offline NDCG, decide what ships.
- The feed is a **loop**: today's recommendations generate tomorrow's
  training data, so drift monitoring, retraining, and cold-start
  handling are part of the design itself.
