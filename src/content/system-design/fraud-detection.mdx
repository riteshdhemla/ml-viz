# Design a Real-Time Fraud Detection System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Stripe / Nubank"
  scale="billions of tx/day · <100ms · fraud <0.5% · adversarial"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

Picture the moment this system lives in. A customer taps their card at a
checkout page. Somewhere between "Pay" and "Payment confirmed," a scoring
service has about 100 milliseconds to answer one question: *is this
transaction fraudulent?* If it says yes to a real customer, that customer is
embarrassed at the register and may never come back. If it says no to a
stolen card, the money is gone — and weeks later the real cardholder will
dispute the charge and the payment company eats the loss.

That single moment is why fraud detection is the ML interview where **the
data and the evaluation are the hard parts**, not the model. The modeling
itself is fairly ordinary binary classification. What makes the problem
genuinely difficult are two forces that most ML problems don't have. The
first is **extreme class imbalance**: fewer than 1 in 200 transactions is
fraud, so any naive approach drowns in the negatives. The second is an
**adaptive adversary**: unlike, say, a movie recommender, the thing you are
predicting actively studies your defenses and changes tactics the moment
you catch on. Together, these two forces mean the **data → evaluation →
feedback** stages of the ML loop carry almost all the weight — and that is
where this walkthrough spends its time.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
This case leans on domain vocabulary from payments and on a few ML
concepts. Here is the background knowledge it assumes, spelled out:

- **Chargeback** — the customer tells their *bank* "I didn't make this
  purchase," the bank reverses the charge, and the merchant/payment
  processor absorbs the loss plus a fee. Crucially, chargebacks arrive
  **weeks after** the transaction — which is why fraud labels are always
  late.
- **Step-up authentication ("challenge")** — instead of approving or
  declining outright, you can ask the customer for extra proof: a one-time
  code, a bank-app confirmation (3-D Secure). It adds friction but converts
  an uncertain decision into a confident one.
- **KYC (Know Your Customer)** — the identity-verification process banks run
  when opening accounts. Adjacent to fraud scoring, not part of it.
- **Precision, recall, and PR-AUC** — precision is "of the transactions we
  flagged, how many were actually fraud"; recall is "of all the fraud, how
  much did we catch." PR-AUC summarizes that tradeoff across thresholds and
  behaves much better than ROC-AUC when positives are rare — see
  [ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC](/wiki/roc-auc).
- **Feature store** — a service that computes and serves model features so
  that training and serving use *identical* values; the streaming variant
  updates features within seconds of each event.
- **Gradient-boosted decision trees (XGBoost)** — the workhorse model family
  for tabular data; an ensemble of small trees trained sequentially, each
  correcting the last.
- **Graph neural networks / message passing** — models that pass information
  along edges of a graph so each node's score reflects its neighborhood —
  see [graph-based fraud detection](/wiki/graph-fraud-detection).
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

Start by pinning down what the system actually decides. The goal is to score
every transaction for fraud risk *in real time* and then **act** on that
score. The action is not just approve-or-decline: there is a third option,
the **step-up challenge**, which asks the customer for extra verification.
That third option matters because it lets you convert medium-confidence
decisions into customer-verified ones instead of guessing.

Next, name the thing that shapes every downstream choice: **the two kinds of
error have wildly different costs, and they are not symmetric.** A missed
fraud (false negative) costs the transaction amount — a concrete, bounded
dollar loss. A false positive blocks a legitimate customer mid-purchase,
which costs the sale *and* erodes trust in a way that compounds: customers
whose cards get declined stop using them. Because the costs are asymmetric
and business-specific, **where you set the decision threshold is a business
decision, not a modeling one** — a point this case returns to repeatedly.

Finally, scope it. This case covers real-time transaction scoring and the
feedback loop that keeps the model current. Chargeback *handling*, KYC, and
account-takeover detection are adjacent systems — worth naming in an
interview to show you see the landscape, but out of scope here.

<Callout type="warning" title="Never optimize accuracy">
With fraud under 0.5% of volume, a model that predicts "never fraud" is
99.5% accurate and completely useless. Reaching for accuracy is the classic
interview red flag in any imbalanced problem. The right vocabulary is
precision and recall *at a chosen operating point*, PR-AUC for
threshold-free comparison, and — best of all in this domain —
**dollar-weighted cost**, since the business measures errors in money, not
in counts.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

Each requirement below forces a specific architectural consequence — that's
the point of writing them down. Read each row as "because we must hit
*this*, we are forced to build *that*."

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | < 100 ms inline with checkout | Precomputed/streaming features; a model that scores in microseconds |
| Volume | Billions of tx/day | Features computed by streaming pipelines, never per-request from scratch |
| Imbalance | Fraud < 0.5% | Class weighting, cost-sensitive thresholds, PR-AUC instead of accuracy |
| Adversarial | Fraud patterns shift weekly | Fast retraining + drift monitoring + a human review queue |
| Explainability | Reason codes for every decline | Feature attributions — often a *regulatory* requirement, not a nice-to-have |

Two of these deserve elaboration. The **latency budget** rules out anything
expensive at request time: you cannot run a large neural network or compute
aggregations over a customer's history while the checkout spinner turns.
Whatever the model needs must be either precomputed or maintained
incrementally by a streaming system, ready to fetch in single-digit
milliseconds. And the **explainability requirement** is not cosmetic — card
networks and regulators in many jurisdictions require a stated reason when
a transaction is declined, which quietly constrains the model family you
can put inline.

## 3. ML problem framing

Formally, this is **cost-sensitive binary classification on a highly
imbalanced, non-stationary stream**. Let's unpack each word of that,
because each one changes the design:

- *Binary classification*: fraud or not, from labeled history.
- *Cost-sensitive*: the loss function and the decision threshold must
  reflect that the two error types cost different amounts of money — the
  decision is not `argmax` over class probabilities but a **threshold**
  (in practice, two thresholds creating three bands: approve below the
  first, challenge in the middle, decline above the second), chosen to
  minimize expected dollar cost.
- *Imbalanced*: positives are rare, so training and evaluation both need
  imbalance-aware treatment.
- *Non-stationary stream*: the data distribution shifts constantly —
  partly organic (new merchants, seasonality) and partly adversarial
  (fraudsters probing for blind spots) — so the model is never "done."

Supervised learning on confirmed-fraud labels is the core of the system.
**Anomaly detection** plays a supporting role: it can flag *novel* patterns
that have no labels yet, buying time until the supervised model catches up.

It's worth asking the spine question — *what was breaking before this
system?* The pre-ML answer is static rules: "decline if amount > $X from a
new device," and so on. Rules have two fatal flaws as a primary defense.
They are brittle — each rule captures one narrow pattern and misses
everything adjacent — and they are transparent to the adversary, who simply
probes until they find the edges and then operates just inside them. A
plain, cost-blind classifier fixes the brittleness but ignores the
asymmetric costs and the imbalance. The system below fixes both.

<ThinkFirst question="Your label is “this transaction got a chargeback.” Before building features — what's wrong with that label?">
Three things, each needing its own countermeasure. It's **late** —
chargebacks arrive weeks after the transaction, so your most recent (and
most relevant) data is unlabeled. It's **noisy** — not all fraud gets
disputed, and not every dispute is fraud ("friendly fraud" is a customer
disputing a purchase they actually made). And it's **selection-biased** —
you only ever observe chargebacks on transactions you *approved*; declined
transactions generate no label at all. The countermeasures, respectively:
maturation windows before trusting a "not fraud" label, a human review
queue as a faster and cleaner label source, and a small randomized slice of
risky transactions that get approved anyway so the model keeps seeing that
region of the space.
</ThinkFirst>

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

**Labels first, because they are the weakest link.** The ground-truth label
is "this transaction was later confirmed as fraud" — mostly via
chargebacks. As the ThinkFirst above unpacked, that label is late, noisy,
and biased, and **label latency is a first-class design problem** here, not
a footnote: a system retrained daily on labels that arrive weeks late is
always training on last month's attack patterns.

**Features next — and one family dominates.** The most predictive fraud
features are **velocity features**: counts and sums over short recent
windows that capture *behavioral tempo*. Examples: `tx_count_5min` (how
many transactions this card attempted in the last five minutes),
`distinct_countries_24h` (a card used in three countries in a day is
suspicious), `device_age` (fraud disproportionately comes from
newly-seen devices), `merchant_decline_rate` (some merchants attract
fraud). Why do these dominate? Because stolen credentials get *used
differently* than legitimate ones — fast, repeatedly, and across accounts —
even when each individual transaction looks normal.

Velocity features carry an infrastructure price tag. They must be fresh to
the second (a burst of five transactions in five minutes *is* the signal —
yesterday's snapshot is useless), queryable in under ~10ms to fit the
latency budget, and — critically — **computed by the same pipeline for
training and for serving**. If the training job computes `tx_count_5min`
one way from logs and the serving path computes it slightly differently
from the stream, the model learns on features that don't exist in
production. That failure mode is called **train-serve skew**, and the
**streaming feature store** exists to prevent it.

**The third signal family is the graph.** Individual fraudulent
transactions are engineered to look clean. But fraud is run as a business,
and businesses reuse infrastructure: the same device, shipping address, or
card shows up behind many accounts. Build a **shared-entity graph** —
accounts, cards, devices, addresses as nodes; "used together" as edges —
and organized fraud rings become visible as dense clusters even when every
per-transaction feature looks innocent.

The visualization above shows the mechanism: **message passing** propagates
risk along edges, so an account two hops from a cluster of confirmed-fraud

In production, these graph scores are computed offline or near-line and
served as *precomputed features* — the graph model never runs inline.

<ThinkFirst question="Tabular features, a 100ms inline budget, and regulators want reason codes for every decline. What model family — and why not the deepest one you know?">
Gradient-boosted trees. On tabular velocity features they remain
state-of-the-art or within noise of it, they score in microseconds (leaving
the latency budget for feature fetches), and their feature attributions
supply the reason codes regulators require. Deep models still earn a place —
but as *feature producers*: entity embeddings and GNN risk scores computed
offline and fed into the tabular model as inputs. The deep model enriches
the features; the tree model makes the inline decision.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective)*

The model choice follows directly from the constraints just established.
The **baseline — and the production default across the industry — is
gradient-boosted trees** (XGBoost or similar) over velocity and account
features. Three properties make trees the incumbent: they are strong on
tabular data, they are fast enough to be irrelevant to the latency budget,
and their attributions satisfy the explainability requirement.

On top of that baseline, layer in the signals trees can't extract alone:
**entity embeddings** (learned dense representations of merchants, devices,
categories) and **GNN risk scores** from the entity graph, both computed
offline and consumed as features. The serving-time decision blends three
inputs: the tabular model's score, the precomputed graph score, and a
**rules layer** (whose role becomes clear in the next section).

The **objective** encodes the cost asymmetry. Rather than vanilla
cross-entropy, use class-weighted or focal loss — and set the weights from
the **business cost of each error type**, not mechanically from inverse
class frequency. A missed $5,000 fraud and a missed $5 fraud are not the
same event, and dollar-weighting the loss teaches the model to care about
the difference.

## 6. Serving & scaling

Walk the request path, because the whole architecture is legible from it.
A transaction arrives inline with checkout. The scorer fetches streaming
features from the feature store and the precomputed graph score (a few
milliseconds each, in parallel), runs the tree ensemble (microseconds),
maps the score through the threshold bands to an action — approve,
challenge, or decline — and emits reason codes alongside. Total: well
under 100ms, with the budget dominated by feature fetches, not the model.

Alongside the model sits a **rules layer**, and it earns its place for one
reason: **speed of response**. When a new attack pattern emerges at 2 a.m.,
a rule ("block this merchant-ID + BIN combination") deploys in minutes.
A model can't respond until labels accumulate and a retrain ships — hours
to days. Mature fraud systems are never "rules vs. ML"; they are ML for
the broad learned surface plus rules for known-bad patterns and rapid
incident response.

<ThinkFirst question="The model outputs a risk score. Where do you set the decision threshold — and why is 0.5 the wrong answer?">
0.5 silently assumes both error types cost the same, which section 1
established is false: a missed fraud costs the transaction value; a false
decline costs a customer relationship. Set thresholds where the **expected
dollar cost** curve is minimized — and in practice not one threshold but
two, creating approve / challenge / decline bands, because the step-up
challenge gives you a cheap middle option between the two expensive
mistakes. The model produces a score; the *thresholds* encode the business
decision.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

Offline evaluation starts with choosing metrics that survive the imbalance.
**PR-AUC is the headline metric, not ROC-AUC** — with 0.5% positives,
ROC-AUC can look excellent while the model is useless at any real operating
point, because the false-positive *rate* is diluted by the enormous
negative class. PR-AUC keeps the focus on the positive class, where the
problem actually lives. Alongside it, report precision and recall **at the
actual operating thresholds**, and — the metric the business genuinely
optimizes — **dollar-weighted cost curves**: expected fraud loss plus
expected false-decline loss as a function of threshold. That curve is what
turns "pick a threshold" from a guess into a calculation, and it's how the
approve/challenge/decline bands from section 6 get their numeric values.

<WikiLink slug="roc-auc" title="ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC — why PR-AUC for rare positives" />

Online, the loop is: **shadow first, then A/B**. A new model runs in shadow
mode — scoring live traffic without acting — so its score distribution and
disagreements with the incumbent can be inspected safely. Only then does it
take a small slice of real decisions. The online metrics to watch are
approval rate (is the new model quietly declining more good customers?),
realized fraud loss (with the caveat that this number matures over weeks,
as chargebacks trickle in), and false-positive complaints from support
channels.

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

In most ML systems, drift is an accident of changing user behavior. Here,
**drift is the adversary's strategy** — when fraudsters find a blind spot,
the input distribution shifts *by their design*, and it shifts fast.
That inverts the usual posture: drift monitoring on feature and score
distributions is not a hygiene item but a primary detection surface, and
an alert may literally mean "a new attack started this morning."

The feedback loop has three interlocking parts, each covering a different
timescale:

- **The human review queue** covers *hours*. Analysts review flagged and
  ambiguous transactions; their decisions become fresh, clean labels weeks
  before chargebacks would confirm the same cases — directly attacking the
  label-latency problem from section 4.
- **Fast retraining** covers *days*. Daily or continuous retraining on
  review-queue labels plus matured chargebacks keeps the model tracking the
  adversary. A monthly retrain cadence, fine elsewhere, loses this arms
  race.
- **Rules** cover *minutes*, as described in section 6 — the stopgap
  between attack and retrain.

One subtlety poisons retraining if ignored: **selection bias in the
labels**. You only observe outcomes on transactions you approved — declined
transactions never get the chance to become chargebacks, so the model never
learns whether its declines were right. Left uncorrected, the training set
increasingly reflects only the traffic past models let through. The
mitigations: a small, bounded **randomized-approval slice** in the risky
band (deliberately approving some transactions the model would decline, to
keep observing that region), plus reject-inference techniques that impute
likely outcomes for declined traffic.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

Every choice above beat a real alternative. The table summarizes the forks
and why this design takes the branch it does:

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| GBDT + graph + rules ensemble | Single end-to-end deep model | Interpretability (reason codes), microsecond inference, and rules react in minutes while retrains take days |
| Cost-sensitive threshold bands | Single 0.5 cutoff | Costs are asymmetric, and the step-up challenge creates a cheap middle action between the two expensive errors |
| Streaming feature store | Batch (daily) features | Velocity attacks happen in minutes; stale features literally cannot see them |
| Supervised + anomaly hybrid | Pure supervised | Anomaly detection flags novel patterns during the label-latency gap, before any chargeback exists |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Labels arrive weeks late as chargebacks. How do you train?">
Treat recent labels as censored: a transaction from last week with no
chargeback is not confirmed-good — the chargeback may simply not have
arrived yet. Impose a **maturation window** (only treat transactions as
negative once they're old enough that a chargeback would have appeared),
weight labels by confidence, and lean on the human review queue for faster,
cleaner labels on the ambiguous cases that matter most.
</Details>

<Details summary="Fraudsters found a new pattern overnight. Model or rules?">
Rules, immediately — they deploy in minutes and give exact coverage of the
known pattern while labels accumulate. The model catches up at the next
retrain and generalizes beyond the specific pattern. This is the
minutes/days division of labor from section 8: rules are the tourniquet,
the retrain is the treatment.
</Details>

<Details summary="You only see chargebacks on approved transactions. Isn't that biased?">
Yes — it's the selection-bias problem, and it compounds silently: each
model generation trains only on traffic previous generations approved.
Mitigate with a bounded randomized-approval slice in the riskiest band
(accepting a small, budgeted fraud loss as the price of unbiased data) and
reject-inference techniques that estimate outcomes for declined traffic
when retraining.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Fraud is **cost-sensitive classification** under **extreme imbalance**
  against an **adaptive adversary**. Accuracy and ROC-AUC are traps at 0.5%
  positives; the honest metrics are PR-AUC and expected dollar cost.
- **Velocity features** (behavioral tempo over minutes) and **graph
  signals** (shared-entity structure) carry the model; a streaming feature
  store keeps them fresh and prevents train-serve skew.
- The decision is **threshold bands, not argmax** — approve / challenge /
  decline, placed by the dollar-cost curve, because the two error types
  cost different amounts of money.
- The system wins by **closing the loop faster than the adversary moves**:
  rules respond in minutes, human review generates labels in hours,
  retraining ships in days — and a randomized-approval slice keeps the
  labels honest.
