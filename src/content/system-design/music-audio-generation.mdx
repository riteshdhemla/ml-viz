# Design a Music & Audio Generation System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="Generative AI System Design"
  company="Suno / ElevenLabs"
  scale="minutes-long coherent audio · streaming · copyright-sensitive"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

"An upbeat indie-folk song about leaving home, female vocals, with a
big chorus" — and ninety seconds later, a full three-minute track with
verses, a chorus that returns, and a bridge that resolves. Audio
generation shares the generative-AI playbook with images and text,
but three properties push the design in its own directions, and each
gets its own act in this case.

First, **the signal is enormous**: CD-quality audio runs tens of
thousands of samples per second, so a song is *millions* of timesteps
— no sequence model attends over that raw, and the enabling move of
the whole field is generating in a **compressed representation** (a
neural codec's tokens or a latent) instead of the waveform. Second,
**music has long-range structure**: unlike an image, where coherence
is local, a song's quality lives in its *form* — verses that return,
a chorus you recognize the second time, a key that resolves — and
models that nail ten seconds routinely fall apart at three minutes.
Third, **copyright and voice are uniquely fraught**: the training
data is the recorded-music industry's crown jewels, and the
modality's signature abuse — cloning a real person's voice — enables
fraud in a way no image quite matches.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
This case builds on the speech-audio and generative-models courses:

- **Audio representations** — waveforms, sample rates, and
  spectrograms; from speech-audio/01. The key number: 44.1kHz means
  44,100 samples per second per channel.
- **Neural audio codecs & RVQ** — learned compressors that turn a
  waveform into a short sequence of discrete tokens (residual vector
  quantization stacks several codebooks for fidelity) and decode
  tokens back to audio. The "tokenizer for sound."
- **Autoregressive transformers over tokens** — the LLM recipe
  applied to audio tokens (MusicGen/AudioLM-style): predict the next
  token, where tokens now encode sound.
- **Latent diffusion** — the alternative family: denoise in a
  compressed audio latent, then decode; same trick as image latent
  diffusion, pointed at sound.
- **Hierarchical (coarse→fine) generation** — generating a coarse
  structural sequence first, then filling in fine acoustic detail
  conditioned on it.
- **FAD & CLAP score** — Fréchet Audio Distance (realism of the audio
  distribution) and CLAP (prompt–audio alignment) — the audio
  analogues of FID and CLIPScore.
- **MOS** — mean opinion score, the standard human rating for voice
  naturalness.
- **Vocoder** — the final decoder from an intermediate representation
  to waveform.
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

The goal: generate music or audio — songs, sound effects, voices —
from a text prompt, optionally with lyrics or a reference melody,
**coherent over minutes**. That last phrase carries the quality bar:
a ten-second demo and a listenable three-minute song are different
products, and the gap between them is the structure problem.

The two hard axes to name up front: **temporal resolution** (the
16-million-sample arithmetic below) and **long-range structure**
(musical form). And the risk to name in the same breath: **copyright
and likeness** — generating in a named artist's style or voice is the
defining legal exposure, and it shapes data sourcing, prompt
filtering, and output monitoring throughout. Scope: the generation
pipeline — model, serving, safety.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
Raw waveforms are far too long to model directly — name the **neural
audio codec / tokenization** as the enabling move, and **long-range
musical structure** as the quality challenge. Those two points are
the audio-specific content of this interview.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | Minutes of coherent audio | Long-range modeling; hierarchical generation |
| Resolution | High sample rate | Model compressed tokens/latents, not raw samples |
| Latency | Streamable | Chunked/streaming synthesis |
| Copyright | No artist/voice cloning without consent | Style/voice filters, provenance |

<ThinkFirst question="A 3-minute song at 44.1kHz stereo — roughly how many samples is that, and what does the number rule out?">
180 seconds × 44,100 samples × 2 channels ≈ **16 million samples**.
No sequence model attends over 16 million timesteps — raw-waveform
generation is arithmetically off the table before any modeling
discussion starts. The enabling move: a **neural audio codec**
compresses the waveform into a short discrete-token or latent
sequence — hundreds of tokens per second instead of tens of thousands
of samples — the generative model works in that space, and a decoder
reconstructs the waveform. Same shape of trick as latent diffusion
for images: never model the raw signal.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. GenAI problem framing

With the codec established, two model families dominate, and they're
both "borrow a proven recipe and point it at audio tokens":

- **Tokenized-audio autoregressive** (MusicGen/AudioLM-style): the
  codec turns audio into discrete tokens; a transformer models the
  token sequence exactly as an LLM models text. Long context windows
  over compact tokens are what make minutes of structure reachable.
- **Latent diffusion on audio** (or on mel-spectrograms): denoise in
  the compressed audio latent, decode to waveform — the image
  playbook, transposed.

The pre-generative baseline: sample libraries and rule-based
composition tools — professional, but with no open-ended text
control. The step change is the same as in every generative modality:
an arbitrary natural-language request becomes novel output.

## 4. Data & training *(spine: data)*

Training happens in two stages with distinct data needs. First,
**train the codec** — the compressed representation everything else
lives in — on broad audio. Then train the **generative model** over
codec tokens, on music and audio *paired with text*: captions, genre
and instrument tags, tempo and mood metadata. That pairing rarely
exists naturally at scale, so captioning and tagging pipelines are
real subsystems here, the way caption quality was for text-to-image.

And the data decision that towers over the others in this modality:
**licensing**. Music rights-holders are concentrated, organized, and
litigious in a way image rights-holders are not — training-data
provenance, licensed catalogs, and artist opt-outs are existential
business questions, not compliance hygiene. The design should treat
"what are we allowed to train on" as a first-class constraint that
shapes what the model can do.

<ThinkFirst question="Your model produces gorgeous 10-second clips, but 3-minute songs meander and repeat. What's missing?">
**Long-range structure.** Local coherence — the next bar following
naturally from this one — is a different skill from musical *form*:
verses that return, a chorus that repeats recognizably, a key that
eventually resolves. A flat next-token model optimizes the former and
drifts on the latter; nothing pulls it back to material from two
minutes ago. The fix is **hierarchical generation** — model coarse
structural tokens first (the song's skeleton), then fill in fine
acoustic detail conditioned on them — plus long context and explicit
form conditioning: lyrics with section tags, verse/chorus markers.
Structure has to be represented to be preserved.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model & generation *(spine: hypothesis-space)*

The stack, bottom to top. The **codec** — RVQ-based — is the
foundation: waveform in, short token sequence out, and its fidelity
caps the whole system's audio quality the way the autoencoder caps
latent diffusion. The **generator** — autoregressive transformer or
latent diffusion — models the token/latent sequence conditioned on
text (plus lyrics or melody reference), generating **hierarchically**
coarse-to-fine per the ThinkFirst: structure tokens first, acoustic
detail second. The **decoder/vocoder** turns tokens or latents back
into waveform.

<DiffusionViz />

Whichever family you pick, the through-line is the one from the
arithmetic: **generate in a compressed representation, then decode**.
The families differ in sampling behavior and latency profile;
they agree on never touching raw signal.

## 6. Serving & scaling

Song generation is a GPU *job* — tens of seconds to minutes — so the
serving pattern is queue-and-notify rather than request-response.
The experience fix is **streaming**: generate and decode in chunks so
playback starts while the rest of the track is still being generated
— the perceived latency is time-to-first-audio, not time-to-complete
(the same insight as the voice-agent case, at a different timescale).
Outputs land in object storage behind a CDN; codec and vocoder stay
cached; generation requests batch.

One contrast worth naming: **voice synthesis (TTS) is a different
serving regime** — short utterances with a light vocoder can run
near-real-time, which is why voice products feel interactive while
song products feel like a render queue. Same stack, different
operating points.

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

Automated metrics mirror the image world's pair: **FAD** for
distributional realism, **CLAP score** for prompt–audio alignment —
useful as checkpoint screens, weak as taste. More than in any other
modality, **human evaluation dominates**: musicality and structure
resist automation almost completely, and voice naturalness has its
own standard (MOS). One eval this case uniquely needs: **explicit
structure checks at full length** — does a two-minute song hold
together, do choruses return, or does it meander? — because
per-clip metrics computed on short windows are structurally blind to
exactly the failure the ThinkFirst described.

<ThinkFirst question="What's the abuse case unique to audio generation — the one that's worse here than in any other modality?">
**Voice cloning.** A convincing synthetic voice enables fraud with a
directness no image matches: the fake distress call from a family
member, the fake CEO authorizing a wire transfer — attacks that
exploit trust in a *channel* (a voice on the phone) that predates any
notion of synthetic media. Audio deepfakes are cheap to make, hard
for humans to detect, and deployable in real time over a phone line.
That's why consent gates on voice references, voice-likeness
detection on outputs, and watermarking rank *ahead of* general
content filtering in this modality's safety ordering.
</ThinkFirst>

## 8. Safety & guardrails

The guardrails follow the ThinkFirst's ordering. **Voice cloning**
first: consent verification before any voice reference is used,
voice-likeness detection screening outputs against known and
protected voices, and watermarking throughout. **Style and
copyright** second: prompt filters on requests targeting named
artists, and output monitoring for melodic or verbatim regurgitation
of training tracks — the audio version of the memorization problem,
with the dedup-and-detect answer. **Provenance** underneath:
inaudible watermarks plus metadata marking generated audio as
generated, so downstream platforms and investigators can tell — which
matters more here than anywhere, precisely because human ears can't.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Tokenized/latent audio | Raw waveform modeling | 16M samples per song — intractable directly |
| Hierarchical coarse→fine | Flat single-scale generation | Captures long-range form *and* fine detail |
| Streaming chunked synthesis | Generate-then-serve whole | Playback starts in seconds, not minutes |
| Licensed data + opt-outs | Scrape everything | Copyright exposure is existential in music |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Why not model the raw audio waveform directly?">
Run the arithmetic: minutes of audio is millions of samples, far past
any tractable attention span — and even if you could, sample-level
prediction spends all its capacity on waveform minutiae while
learning nothing about musical structure. The codec solves both at
once: compression makes the sequence short enough to model, and
because codec tokens encode perceptually meaningful chunks of sound,
the generative model's capacity goes toward music rather than
waveform bookkeeping. The decoder handles reconstruction.
</Details>

<Details summary="A 10-second clip sounds great but a 3-minute song falls apart. Why?">
Because local coherence and musical form are different skills, and a
flat next-token model only has the first. Nothing in its objective
rewards a chorus returning or a key resolving — so songs meander and
repeat. The fixes: hierarchical generation (coarse structural
skeleton first, acoustic detail conditioned on it), long context over
compact tokens, and explicit form conditioning (section-tagged
lyrics). And evaluate at full length — short-clip metrics literally
cannot see this failure, which is how it ships.
</Details>

<Details summary="A user prompts 'a song in the voice of [famous artist].' What happens?">
Blocked at multiple layers: prompt filters catch the named-artist
voice request; voice-likeness detection screens outputs against
protected voices (catching what a rephrased prompt slipped through);
and watermarking marks whatever is generated for downstream
attribution. Unconsented voice and style cloning is the modality's
defining legal and safety risk — treated as a hard guardrail backed by
training-data licensing hygiene, not a tunable content preference.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- The enabling move of all audio generation is the **neural codec**:
  compress 16 million samples into hundreds of tokens per second,
  generate there, decode back — never model raw signal.
- **Long-range structure is the quality frontier**: local coherence
  is cheap, musical form needs hierarchical coarse-to-fine
  generation, form conditioning, and full-length evaluation.
- **Human ears are the judge**: FAD and CLAP screen checkpoints, but
  musicality, structure, and naturalness are human-rated — and
  structure must be evaluated at song length, not clip length.
- **Voice cloning is the modality's signature abuse** — consent
  gates, likeness detection, and watermarking outrank general content
  filtering — and **music licensing is existential**, shaping the
  training data before any model decision is made.
