# Design a Search & Ranking System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Airbnb / Amazon"
  scale="100M+ listings · 10k+ QPS · <300ms budget"
  difficulty="intermediate"
/>

A user types "beach house for 6 near lisbon" into a travel marketplace.
In under 300 milliseconds, the system must interpret that short,
ambiguous string — fix any typos, work out that "oceanfront villa,
sleeps 8" is a match even though it shares no words with the query —
find the few hundred best candidates among a hundred million listings,
and order them so that the results this user will actually *book* come
first. Then do it ten thousand times a second.

"Design search" sounds like an information-retrieval question — inverted
indexes, keyword matching. The reframe that makes this an ML interview:
a modern search system is an **ML ranking system with an IR front-end**.
The classic index machinery is still there, doing what it's good at, but
wrapped in ML at three points — understanding the query, retrieving by
*meaning* rather than just words, and ranking by predicted conversion
rather than lexical similarity. Structurally, the winning move is the
same one as the home-feed case: separate **retrieval** (cheap, cast a
wide net, high recall) from **ranking** (expensive, precise, runs on a
small candidate set). Walk it through the ML project loop.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
Search sits on decades of IR vocabulary plus modern embedding machinery:

- **Inverted index** — the classic search data structure: a map from
  each term to the list of documents containing it, making "find all
  docs with these words" fast at any scale.
- **BM25** — the standard lexical relevance formula scoring documents by
  term overlap with the query, weighting rare terms more and adjusting
  for document length — see [BM25](/wiki/bm25-ranking). "Lexical" means
  it matches *words*, not meanings.
- **Semantic / embedding retrieval** — encoding queries and items as
  vectors so that "beach house" and "oceanfront villa" land near each
  other; served by ANN indexes like [HNSW](/wiki/hnsw) in a
  [vector database](/wiki/vector-databases).
- **Learning to rank (LTR)** — training a model to order results.
  *Pointwise* scores items independently; *pairwise/listwise* train on
  preferences between items. **LambdaMART** is the classic GBDT-based
  listwise workhorse.
- **NDCG / MRR / Recall@k** — offline ranking metrics; NDCG rewards
  putting the most relevant items highest, with graded (not binary)
  relevance.
- **Position bias** — users click top results partly *because they're on
  top*, so click logs conflate relevance with placement;
  **inverse-propensity weighting (IPS)** is the standard correction.
- **Hard negatives** — training negatives chosen to be *difficult*
  (shown-but-skipped items) rather than random, so the model learns to
  resolve close calls.
- **Interleaving** — an online evaluation trick that merges two rankers'
  results into one list and attributes clicks, detecting quality
  differences with far less traffic than a split A/B test.
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

What's being searched is a catalog of listings or products — structured
fields (price, location, capacity), free text, and images — queried by
short, often misspelled, often ambiguous strings. That asymmetry (rich
structured items, messy three-word queries) is why *query understanding*
is a stage of its own.

The definition of "a good result" is where marketplaces diverge from web
search, and it's worth stating precisely: a good result is one that's
relevant **and converts** — gets booked, gets purchased. Search here is
judged on downstream conversion, not on lexical match quality, and that
choice of objective propagates into the labels (bookings, not just
clicks) and the evaluation (bookings-per-search, not just NDCG).
Personalization follows from the same logic: the same query means
different things to different users — budget, location, taste — so user
context is a ranking input.

Scope: query understanding, retrieval, and ranking. The crawl/indexing
infrastructure and ads placement are adjacent systems.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
State early that relevance labels are **implicit and biased** — you only
observe clicks and bookings on what you chose to show, in the order you
showed it. Position bias is the defining data problem of search ranking,
and naming it before being asked is the strongest signal in this
interview.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | < 300 ms p99 | Two-stage funnel; ANN + a bounded ranker |
| Catalog | 100M+ items | Inverted index + embedding index, never scans |
| Throughput | 10k+ QPS | Cheap retrieval; optimized ranker on ~1k candidates |
| Robustness | Typos, synonyms, multilingual | Query understanding + semantic retrieval |
| Freshness | New/updated items searchable in minutes | Incremental indexing |

<ThinkFirst question="You already have BM25 over an inverted index — a classic search engine. What can't it do, and where exactly does ML enter?">
Lexical matching has three blind spots. It can't bridge vocabulary —
"beach house" and "oceanfront villa" share zero terms, so BM25 scores
them as unrelated. It can't personalize — the index doesn't know who's
asking. And it doesn't know that relevance isn't the goal — conversion
is. ML enters in exactly three slots to fix those three gaps: a query
understanding stage, a *semantic* retrieval channel running alongside
BM25 (not replacing it), and a learned ranker on top. The classic
engine stays; ML wraps it.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

The pipeline has three ML stages, each answering the gap the ThinkFirst
identified:

1. **Query understanding** — spell-correction, tokenization,
   intent/entity detection ("near lisbon" → a geo filter), synonym
   expansion. Framed as lightweight tagging and classification models.
2. **Retrieval** *(hypothesis space)* — fetch ~1000 candidates through
   **hybrid** recall: BM25 over the inverted index *and* embedding ANN
   search, merged. Hybrid, because each channel catches what the other
   misses — lexical nails exact terms and IDs, semantic bridges
   vocabulary.
3. **Ranking** *(hypothesis space + objective)* — a **learning-to-rank**
   model scores each candidate on `P(book | query, item, user)`,
   bringing in everything retrieval couldn't afford: user context,
   rich query–item interactions, behavioral history.

Why this shape? The two failure modes it fixes are complementary: pure
lexical search misses meaning and can't personalize; a single expensive
ranker over the full catalog blows the latency budget a thousandfold.
The funnel keeps the strengths of both.

<ThinkFirst question="You're building the ranker's training set. Clicked items are the positives — what should the negatives be?">
Not random catalog items. Teaching the model to separate "beach house"
from a lawnmower is trivially easy and teaches it nothing about ranking.
Use **hard negatives** — items shown in the *same session* that the
user skipped — so the model learns to resolve exactly the close calls
it will face in production: ten plausible beach houses, one of which
gets clicked. And keep the caveat in view: the positives are
position-biased — the user clicked partly because you ranked it first —
so the labels need de-biasing before they can be trusted.
</ThinkFirst>

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

The labels form a graded ladder from cheap-and-weak to
scarce-and-strong: clicks (abundant, noisy, position-biased), long
dwell (attention), bookings/purchases (the real objective, but sparse).
Training targets the conversion end of the ladder, using the weaker
signals to densify. All of it needs **position-bias correction** —
inverse-propensity weighting by how likely each position was to be
examined, or an explicit click model — because uncorrected click data
teaches the model to reproduce the old ranking rather than improve it.

Features group by what they describe: the **query** (embedding, length,
detected intent), the **item** (text and image embeddings, price,
quality score, popularity), the **query–item pair** (BM25 score,
embedding similarity, historical CTR of this item for this query —
often the strongest features in the model), and the **user** (location,
history, price sensitivity). The pair features deserve the emphasis:
ranking is fundamentally about the *match*, and features that directly
measure it outperform any amount of one-sided description.

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective + optimization)*

**Retrieval** pairs the two channels. The inverted index handles the
lexical side untouched. The semantic side is a **two-tower model** — a
query tower and an item tower trained into a shared embedding space —
chosen for the same structural reason as in feed ranking: towers keep
query and item independent until a final dot product, so item
embeddings are precomputed and ANN-indexed, and query-time cost is one
encoder pass plus a lookup. Candidates from both channels merge and
dedup before ranking.

**Ranking** is learning-to-rank, built in deliberate steps. The
baseline is BM25-only — it measures what the whole ML stack must beat.
Step one up: a GBDT ranker (LambdaMART remains a production workhorse a
decade on) over the query–item features — strong, cheap, debuggable.
Step two, if the data supports it: a neural pairwise/listwise ranker
optimizing NDCG directly. Building in this order isn't conservatism —
each step measures the lift of the next, which is exactly what the
interviewer wants to see quantified.

## 6. Serving & scaling

The 300ms budget, allocated across the pipeline:

| Stage | Budget | How |
|---|---|---|
| Query understanding | ~20 ms | Cached, small models |
| Retrieval (BM25 ∪ ANN) | ~60 ms | Inverted index + HNSW, merged & deduped |
| Ranking | ~120 ms | GBDT/neural ranker on ~1000 candidates |
| Blend + business rules | ~30 ms | Diversity, availability, policy |

Around the table, the usual production armor: cache results for popular
queries (query traffic is heavy-tailed, so a small cache covers a large
share), precompute item embeddings at index time, and degrade
gracefully — if the ranker times out, BM25-ordered results are a far
better failure than an error page.

<ThinkFirst question="You have two candidate rankers and limited live traffic. How do you find the better one without burning weeks of A/B tests?">
**Interleaving**: merge both rankers' results into a single list and
attribute each click to the ranker that contributed the item. Because
every user sees both rankers simultaneously, the comparison is
within-user and far more statistically sensitive than a traffic-split
A/B — differences surface in days instead of weeks. The full funnel:
offline NDCG on de-biased labels filters the field, interleaving picks
the promising challenger, and a conventional A/B on bookings makes the
final call.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

The evaluation stack has four layers, ordered by cost and trust.
**Offline metrics** — NDCG@k, MRR, MAP on graded relevance for the
ranker; Recall@k for retrieval — run on every experiment but inherit the
biases of the logs they're computed from. A **human-judged gold set** —
raters scoring query–result relevance — covers what implicit signals
can't: tail queries with no click history, and quality dimensions
clicks don't capture. **Interleaving** provides the sensitive online
screen from the ThinkFirst. And the **A/B test on the business metric**
— bookings or purchases per search — is the verdict, with guardrails on
zero-result rate, latency, and query abandonment, each an early symptom
of a different failure (recall regression, performance regression,
relevance regression).

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

Search drift is seasonal and inventory-driven: the query mix shifts
("ski chalet" in December), items appear and vanish. The most useful
early-warning metrics are behavioral rather than model-based:
**zero-result rate** climbing means retrieval is missing something the
query mix now contains; **reformulation rate** climbing (users
immediately re-searching with different words) means results looked
wrong even when something was returned.

The ranker retrains frequently on fresh click logs — and every retrain
re-runs the de-biasing machinery, because this loop has the same
self-reinforcement risk as the feed: the model trains on clicks its own
ranking generated, and without IPS correction it converges to
reproducing its own past exposure decisions.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hybrid lexical + semantic | Pure semantic (embeddings only) | Lexical still wins on exact IDs/SKUs and rare terms; hybrid is robust |
| Two-stage funnel | One ranker over all items | Latency makes full scoring impossible |
| GBDT ranker | Deep listwise ranker first | GBDT is a strong, cheap, interpretable baseline; add depth only where it pays |
| IPS debiasing | Ignore position bias | Ignoring it makes the model relearn its own exposure decisions |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="A user searches an exact product SKU and gets fuzzy semantic matches. Fix?">
This is the canonical argument for keeping the lexical channel: pure
embedding retrieval blurs exact identifiers — a SKU is just an
arbitrary token to an encoder, and its nearest neighbors are *similar*
products, not *the* product. The hybrid design already contains the
fix: give the lexical exact-match channel the ability to
short-circuit — a high-confidence ID/SKU hit gets boosted or pinned
above the semantic results.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you handle a brand-new listing with no click history?">
Content carries it until behavior exists: text and image embeddings plus
structured attributes let the listing be retrieved and ranked on day
one, and an exploration boost buys the initial exposure that generates
its first behavioral signal. It's the search flavor of the cold-start
problem — same structure as in feeds, same answer: content features
first, bounded exploration to bootstrap the rest.
</Details>

<Details summary="Offline NDCG improves but bookings drop in the A/B. Why?">
The offline metric and the business metric came apart, and there are
three usual culprits: the NDCG labels reward clicks that don't convert
(clickbait titles and photos rank higher, bookings fall); position bias
inflated the offline gain; or train-serve feature skew means the live
model isn't the offline model. The structural fixes: optimize toward
the booking label rather than clicks, and interleave before trusting
any offline delta.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Modern search is **retrieval + learning-to-rank** — an ML system
  wrapped around a classic IR core, not string matching and not pure
  embeddings.
- **Hybrid retrieval beats either channel alone**: lexical keeps exact
  matches and rare terms, semantic bridges vocabulary; each covers the
  other's blind spot.
- The labels are **implicit and position-biased** — correct them with
  IPS or click models, and choose hard negatives from skipped-in-session
  items, or the ranker learns the wrong lesson from every click.
- Optimize and judge on **downstream conversion**: offline NDCG
  nominates, interleaving screens cheaply, and the A/B test on
  bookings-per-search decides.
