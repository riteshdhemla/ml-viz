# Design a Friend / Connection Recommendation System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="LinkedIn / Meta"
  scale="billion-node graph · link prediction · <200ms"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

Open LinkedIn or Facebook and you'll find a module suggesting people to
connect with — "People You May Know." Behind it sits one of the cleanest
graph ML problems in industry. The data is a social graph with a billion
nodes (users) and many billions of edges (connections), and the task is to
predict which edge will form *next*: given this user, who are they likely
to send an invite to — and, just as important, who is likely to say yes?

Two things make this different from the item recommenders elsewhere in
this course. First, **the structure of the graph is itself the dominant
signal**: who you know predicts who you'll know far better than any
profile attribute does. So graph-structural features and graph-aware
candidate generation carry the system, and the ML term for the task is
**link prediction**. Second, the outcome is **two-sided** — a
recommendation only succeeds if one person sends an invite *and the other
accepts*. A recommender that surfaces celebrities everyone wants to
connect with, but who accept no one, is failing, even though every
suggestion looks plausible. Walk it through the ML project loop with
those two ideas in front.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
This case draws on graph ML vocabulary plus standard recommender
machinery:

- **Link prediction** — the graph ML task of predicting which edges
  (connections) will appear next, given the current graph.
- **Second-degree neighborhood / "friends of friends"** — everyone
  connected to your connections but not (yet) to you. The empirical
  backbone of this problem: most new edges form inside it.
- **Adamic-Adar / common-neighbor scores** — classical link-prediction
  heuristics: count shared neighbors, weighting rare (low-degree) shared
  neighbors more, since sharing an obscure contact is more informative
  than sharing a popular one.
- **Node embeddings (node2vec / GNN)** — learned vectors placing
  structurally similar nodes close together, so "similar position in the
  graph" becomes measurable as vector distance.
- **GNNs and message passing** — neural networks that compute a node's
  representation by aggregating its neighbors' features, layer by layer —
  covered in the GNN course, and visualized below.
- **Candidate generation → ranking funnel** — the standard two-stage
  recommender architecture: something cheap proposes a few hundred
  plausible candidates, then an expensive model scores them.
- **ANN / vector search** — approximate nearest-neighbor lookup over
  embeddings, used for retrieval — see
  [vector databases](/wiki/vector-databases).
- **Temporal vs. random splits** — evaluating on *future* edges rather
  than randomly held-out ones; the reasoning is a centerpiece of the
  evaluation section.
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

The goal, stated carefully: recommend people the user is likely to
*connect with* — meaning they send an invite **and** it gets accepted.
Both halves matter. Optimizing only "will they send an invite" surfaces
famous and aspirational profiles that ignore the invite; the recommender
looks busy while producing no new edges. The objective is
`P(connect AND accept)`.

Where does the signal come from? Overwhelmingly from the graph itself:
mutual connections, shared schools and employers, shared groups,
co-location, interaction history. Profile-text similarity is a minor
player — two people with identical job titles in the same city usually
don't know each other, while two people with thirty mutual connections
almost certainly do.

This problem also carries **guardrails unusual for a recommender**,
because the recommendations are *about people*. Surfacing a connection
can reveal a sensitive relationship (a therapist's client list, an
estranged family member, someone's second account) — "accurate" and
"appropriate" are different tests. And popularity feedback loops have a
human cost here: a system that only recommends the already-connected
makes the network rich-get-richer.

Scope: candidate generation and ranking over the graph. The graph
storage and serving infrastructure is adjacent — acknowledge it, don't
design it.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
Frame it as **link prediction on a graph**, and state the objective as
`P(connect AND accept)` — naming the two-sided outcome is the
differentiator between candidates who see the social structure and those
porting a movie recommender.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Graph size | Billions of nodes/edges | Can't score all pairs — candidate generation first |
| Latency | < 200 ms | Precomputed embeddings + bounded candidate set |
| Freshness | New users/edges matter fast | Incremental graph + embedding updates |
| Sparsity | Most users have few edges | Cold-start handling, side features |

<ThinkFirst question="Scoring every user pair is quadratic in a billion nodes — a non-starter. What cheap structural heuristic generates most of the good candidates?">
**Friends of friends.** The overwhelming majority of new connections form
inside the second-degree neighborhood — people who share at least one
mutual connection with you — and that set is enumerable per user with a
cheap graph traversal. This one heuristic collapses the candidate space
from a billion to a few hundred, and everything fancier (embedding
retrieval, shared-attribute candidates) merely supplements it for the
cases it misses.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

Like every large recommender, the system is a **funnel** — but here both
stages are shaped by the graph:

1. **Candidate generation** *(hypothesis space)*: cheaply propose
   plausible connections. The workhorse is the second-degree
   neighborhood from the ThinkFirst; shared-attribute sources (same
   employer, same school, imported contacts) and embedding-based
   retrieval fill in candidates the graph traversal can't see — most
   importantly for sparsely-connected users.
2. **Ranking**: score each candidate on `P(connect & accept)` using
   graph features plus profile and activity features.

Why this shape and not something simpler? Scoring all pairs is quadratic
in a billion nodes — arithmetic ends that conversation. And the
pre-graph alternative, ranking by profile similarity, ignores the
structure that actually predicts connection: people connect to people
*near them in the graph*, not to people who resemble them on paper.

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

Labels fall out of the product's own event stream, in usefully graded
tiers. An **accepted invite** is the true positive — both sides of the
two-sided outcome happened. An invite that was **ignored or declined**
is a *hard negative*, and an especially valuable one: the sender found
the suggestion plausible, but the two-sided outcome failed, which is
exactly the distinction the objective cares about. Impressions with no
action are weak negatives — maybe a bad suggestion, maybe just unseen.

Feature-wise, the hierarchy of value is stark. **Mutual-connection
count** is the single strongest feature. Around it: refined
common-neighbor scores like Adamic-Adar (which discount popular shared
neighbors), shared communities, and graph distance. Then **learned node
embeddings** — node2vec-style or GNN-produced — which serve double duty:
as ranking features, and as the keys for embedding-based candidate
retrieval via ANN search.

<WikiLink slug="vector-databases" title="Serving node embeddings for candidate retrieval" />

<ThinkFirst question="Before any GNN — what's the single strongest feature, and what baseline would you build first?">
**Mutual-connection count.** By itself it's an embarrassingly strong
predictor of connection, and a GBDT over it plus a handful of classical
graph features (Adamic-Adar, shared communities, path distance) is the
baseline every neural model must beat. That baseline isn't a formality:
if your GNN doesn't clear it, the GNN isn't extracting graph structure —
it's expensive overhead. Interviewers reward candidates who commit to
this ordering.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective)*

The baseline, per the ThinkFirst: logistic regression or GBDT over the
classical graph features. It is genuinely hard to beat, cheap to serve,
and interpretable — which matters when a recommendation needs explaining
("12 mutual connections").

The step up is a **graph neural network** — GraphSAGE or GCN — which
learns each node's embedding by aggregating its neighborhood's features,
layer by layer. An edge's score becomes a function of its two endpoint
embeddings. What the GNN buys over the hand-built features is
*higher-order structure*: patterns like "these two users sit in the same
tightly-knit community three hops wide" that no pairwise count captures.
It also naturally folds in node attributes (industry, school), letting
structure and content reinforce each other.

<MessagePassingViz />

The visualization shows the core mechanism: each node's representation
is built from its neighbors', so two users embedded near each other
share graph context — which is precisely the meaning of "you may know
them."

The **objective** ties back to section 1: binary classification (or
pairwise ranking) on acceptance, with the ignored-invite hard negatives
doing the work of teaching the model the difference between "plausible"
and "will actually connect."

## 6. Serving & scaling

The compute splits cleanly along the batch/online line. **Offline**, a
periodic graph job computes node embeddings and materializes each user's
candidate list (their second-degree neighborhood plus
embedding-retrieved and shared-attribute candidates). **Online**, a
request fetches the precomputed candidates, ranks them with fresh
features, applies guardrail filters — blocked users, sensitive-relation
suppression — and returns the top-k, comfortably inside 200ms because
nothing expensive happens at request time. Between batch runs,
incremental updates fold new edges and new users into candidates and
embeddings so the system doesn't go stale in the gaps.

<ThinkFirst question="You hold out 10% of edges at random for the test set. Why are your metrics about to look better than they should?">
Because edges aren't independent across time. A random holdout lets the
model train on edges that formed *after* the test edges — including
other edges of the very same node pairs' neighborhoods — so the
surrounding graph context effectively hands the model the answer.
The honest protocol is a **temporal split**: train on the graph as it
existed at time T, predict which edges form after T. Which is also
exactly the situation the deployed model faces.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

Offline, the ranking metrics are standard — AUC, Recall@k, MAP — but the
*protocol* is the point: they're computed on held-out **future** edges
under the temporal split just described. A random split inflates every
number and, worse, inflates them non-uniformly, so it can reverse model
comparisons.

Online, the metric hierarchy mirrors the two-sided objective: invite
send rate tells you suggestions look plausible; **acceptance rate** tells
you they were right; and the long-term measures — network growth,
engagement of the resulting connections — tell you the new edges were
worth creating. Complaint and block rates ride along as guardrails,
because this recommender's failure modes include making people
uncomfortable, not just being ignored.

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

The graph never stops changing — every accepted invite this system
generates *changes its own input*. That makes staleness the baseline
operational concern: embeddings and candidate lists must refresh on a
cadence matched to edge velocity, or the system recommends against last
month's graph.

The subtler risks are the feedback loops. Popular, well-connected users
attract more impressions, gain more connections, and become more
recommendable — rich-get-richer by construction. Counter it by
optimizing acceptance (popular nodes accept less), capping exposure of
high-degree nodes, and monitoring recommendation diversity. And keep
standing watch on the sensitive-reveal guardrail: complaint signals are
the observable symptom of recommendations that were accurate but should
never have been shown.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate gen + rank | Score all pairs | All-pairs is quadratic — impossible at a billion nodes |
| GNN embeddings | Profile-only similarity | Graph structure is the dominant signal; profiles are weak |
| Temporal eval split | Random split | Random splits leak future edges → inflated, distorted metrics |
| `P(connect & accept)` | `P(similar)` or `P(invite)` | An ignored invite is a failed recommendation; only the two-sided outcome counts |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="A brand-new user has no connections. What do you recommend?">
The graph is silent, so fall back on everything that isn't the graph:
imported contacts (the strongest cold-start signal by far), shared email
domain, employer, school, co-location, and early content interactions —
plus popular-in-their-context suggestions as filler. Every early
connection they make rapidly unlocks the second-degree machinery; the
cold-start phase is short but decisive for retention, since a user with
zero connections has no reason to return.
</Details>

<Details summary="The system keeps recommending already-popular people. Why, and fix?">
It's a feedback loop: high-degree nodes appear in more second-degree
neighborhoods, get more impressions, gain more edges, and appear in
more neighborhoods still. Break it at three points: optimize
*acceptance* rather than invite volume (celebrities don't accept), cap
per-node exposure so no one dominates impression share, and add
diversity terms to the re-rank. Monitor degree-distribution of
recommendations so the loop can't quietly re-form.
</Details>

<Details summary="Why is a random train/test split wrong here?">
It leaks the future through the graph. Edges cluster in time and space:
when a user joins a workplace, a burst of related edges forms together.
A random split scatters that burst across train and test, so the model
"predicts" test edges it has effectively already seen the context of.
Train on the graph up to time T and predict edges after T — matching
both statistical honesty and the deployed reality.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Friend recommendation is **link prediction on a graph**, run as a
  candidate-generation → ranking funnel because all-pairs scoring is
  arithmetically impossible.
- **The graph is the signal**: mutual connections and their refinements
  dominate, GNN embeddings add higher-order structure, and profile
  similarity alone is weak.
- The objective is **two-sided** — `P(connect & accept)` — and the
  evaluation must respect time: temporal splits, acceptance rate, and
  long-term network health.
- The system feeds back into itself — it creates the edges it later
  trains on — so **popularity loops, staleness, and sensitive reveals**
  are standing operational concerns, not edge cases.
