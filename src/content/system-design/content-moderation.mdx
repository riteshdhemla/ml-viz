# Design a Content Moderation System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Meta / TikTok"
  scale="billions of posts/day · multimodal · policy-driven · appeals"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

Content moderation is a **multimodal classification** system wrapped in a
**human-in-the-loop policy machine**. The ML is only half the answer — the
interview reward is designing the loop between models, human reviewers, policy
thresholds, and appeals, so it stays accurate *and* fair as policy and adversaries
evolve. Walk it through **data → evaluation → feedback**. The reference systems
here are Meta's integrity stack, whose progress is published quarterly in the
**Community Standards Enforcement Report**, and OpenAI's moderation pipeline.

## 1. Clarify the problem

- **Goal:** detect and act on policy-violating content (violence, hate, spam,
  CSAM, self-harm, misinformation) across text, image, video, and audio — at
  upload time and after.
- **Actions:** remove, age-gate, demote/reduce reach, or route to human review.
  Not binary — a graded response by confidence and severity.
- **Asymmetry varies by category:** missing CSAM is catastrophic (high recall at
  any cost); over-removing satire harms free expression. **Thresholds are per-policy.**
- **Scope:** the detection + review pipeline. Adjacent: coordinated-behavior /
  network detection (mention).

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
"There is no single threshold — each policy category has its own precision/recall
target set by the cost of each error." Naming this shows you understand moderation
is policy-driven, not a one-number classifier.
</Callout>

<ThinkFirst question="A violating post that 10 people saw and one that 10 million saw count the same in “items removed.” How should the harm actually be measured, and how does that reshape the pipeline?">
Harm scales with **exposure**, so the pipeline can't treat all items equally:
upload-time gating must be instant and cheap for everything, but deeper scans
and scarce human attention should be spent in proportion to predicted reach and
severity. This is also why the field's north-star metric is defined over
*views*, not posts — a design decision you'll meet again in evaluation.
</ThinkFirst>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | Billions of items/day | Cheap first-pass filter; humans only on the margin |
| Modality | Text, image, video, audio | Multimodal encoders; video is expensive |
| Latency | Fast for upload-time gating | Tiered: instant filter → async deep scan |
| Adversarial | Evasion (leetspeak, cropping) | Robust features + continual retraining |
| Fairness / auditability | Explainable, appealable | Reason codes, human review, appeals |

<ThinkFirst question="Human review can't scale to billions of items, and full automation is unacceptable for high-stakes calls. What's the resolution?">
Route by **confidence**: auto-act where the model is very sure, auto-allow
where it's very sure the other way, and spend scarce human attention only on
the uncertain middle — ranked by severity and reach. The design question isn't
"model or human" but *where the boundary between them sits, per policy
category*. Meta's proactive-detection rate — the share of actioned content
flagged by systems before any user report — went from 23.6% in 2017 to roughly
95% for hate speech, which is this boundary moving over five years.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

**Multi-label, multimodal classification** with per-category thresholds, feeding
a **triage** decision: auto-act (very high confidence), auto-allow (very low), or
**route to human review** (the uncertain middle). Humans are the label source and
the safety net — the system is designed around *where the model is unsure*.

*What was breaking before?* Keyword blocklists miss context and are trivially
evaded; pure human review can't scale to billions of items. The lineage runs
from crowdsourced toxicity classifiers — Jigsaw's Perspective work began with
100k human-labeled Wikipedia comments
([Wulczyn et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08914)) — to today's multimodal,
policy-conditioned models.

<ThinkFirst question="Labels come from human reviewers at real cost per item. You can't label everything — where do you spend the budget?">
On the items where a label changes the most: content the model is **uncertain**
about, and content where the model **disagrees with humans** — that's active
learning. Random labeling mostly buys you confident duplicates of what the
model already knows — OpenAI's production moderation pipeline leans on active
learning precisely because violating content is rare in random samples. And
because policy labels are subjective, part of the budget goes to labeling the
*same* items multiple times to measure inter-annotator agreement.
</ThinkFirst>

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

- **Labels:** human-reviewer decisions against a policy taxonomy — expensive,
  subjective, and evolving as policy changes. **Inter-annotator agreement** and
  clear guidelines are part of the data system; OpenAI's moderation paper
  treats taxonomy design, labeling instructions, and label quality control as
  the core engineering, ahead of modeling
  ([Markov et al., AAAI 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.03274)).
- **Multimodal features:** text encoders, image/video encoders, and
  **vision-language** models for image+caption context (a benign image with a
  harmful caption, or vice-versa).
- **Active learning:** label the items the model is most *uncertain* or
  *disagrees with humans* on — the highest-value labels per dollar, and the
  only affordable way to capture rare-event categories.

<WikiLink slug="roc-auc" title="Precision/recall & operating points" />

<ThinkFirst question="You hold a database of millions of known CSAM and terror images. Why is a classifier the wrong first tool for them — and why won't an ordinary cryptographic hash work either?">
For *known* content you don't want generalization, you want **exact recall at
near-zero false positives** — a lookup, not a prediction. But MD5/SHA break on
a one-pixel change, so you need **perceptual hashing**: Meta's open-sourced PDQ
(images) and TMK+PDQF (videos) produce compact signatures that survive
re-encoding, resizing, and light edits, and can be shared across companies via
GIFCT without sharing the content itself. Classifiers are for *novel* content;
hashes are for *known* content.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective)*

- **First-pass filter:** hash-match databases — perceptual hashes like Meta's
  **PDQ / TMK+PDQF** for known CSAM/terror/violent media
  ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2019/08/open-source-photo-video-matching/)) —
  plus cheap keyword/embedding models catch the known and obvious at scale.
- **Deep classifiers:** fine-tuned multimodal transformers per policy area.
  Efficiency research is load-bearing here: Meta credits the **Linformer**
  (linear-attention) architecture with making large language models cheap
  enough to run on billions of posts, and its **RIO** (Reinforced Integrity
  Optimizer) framework trains classifiers against live production metrics
  rather than a static offline dataset
  ([Meta AI](https://ai.meta.com/blog/how-ai-is-getting-better-at-detecting-hate-speech/)).
- **Objective:** per-category weighting; high-recall regimes for catastrophic
  categories, precision-favoring for expression-sensitive ones.

<ThinkFirst question="A new harmful-content policy launches Monday. Your usual pipeline needs tens of thousands of labels and months of iteration. What breaks, and what's the modern fix?">
The bottleneck is label accumulation: by the time the classifier is trained,
the harm has evolved or peaked. The fix is **few-/zero-shot policy
conditioning**: Meta's Few-Shot Learner starts from a large multimodal,
multilingual pretrained model and takes action on new violation types from the
policy *text* plus a handful of examples — adapting in **weeks instead of
months**, across 100+ languages. The trade: lower peak accuracy than a mature
supervised classifier, so it's a bridge while labels accrue, not a
replacement.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Serving & the human loop

Tiered pipeline: (1) instant hash/keyword + light model gate at upload; (2) async
deep multimodal scan; (3) **human review queue** for the uncertain band, ranked
by severity × reach × uncertainty; (4) **appeals** route back through senior
review. Reviewer decisions feed labels back to training. For brand-new harm
types, a few-shot policy-conditioned model (Meta's **Few-Shot Learner**) covers
the gap before a dedicated classifier exists
([Meta AI](https://ai.meta.com/blog/harmful-content-can-evolve-quickly-our-new-ai-system-adapts-to-tackle-it/)).

<ThinkFirst question="What's the north-star metric — and why is “amount of violating content removed” the wrong one?">
Removal counts reward volume, not impact: taking down a million zero-view spam
posts matters less than one viral violating video. The right target is
**prevalence** — the fraction of *views* that land on violating content that
slipped through — because it weights misses by the harm they actually caused.
Meta reports it per category: hate-speech prevalence on Facebook was down to
about 0.02%, i.e. ~2 views per 10,000, by mid-2022.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

- **Prevalence:** the north-star — violating **views per 10,000 views**,
  estimated by human review of sampled content. Meta reported hate-speech
  prevalence falling from ~0.10% in 2020 to **0.02% (2 in 10,000 views)** by
  Q2 2022
  ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2022/08/community-standards-enforcement-report-q2-2022/)).
- **Proactive rate:** share of actioned content caught before any user report —
  a recall proxy at the system level.
- **Per-category precision/recall** at the policy's operating point; track
  recall hard on catastrophic categories. Note the metrics trade off publicly:
  Meta's hate-speech proactive rate *dropped* from 95.6% to 90.2% in Q3 2022
  when it retuned for precision using appeals data — a deliberate move along
  the curve, not a regression.
- **Fairness:** error rates across languages, demographics, and content types —
  over-enforcement on specific groups is a failure even at good aggregate metrics.
- **Human audits** on sampled auto-decisions to catch silent drift.

<ThinkFirst question="Prevalence is estimated by humans reviewing a sample of viewed content. What are the two ways this estimate silently degrades — and what does each one force you to build?">
First, **reviewer drift**: the humans defining ground truth disagree with each
other or shift with policy rewrites, so you need ongoing inter-annotator
measurement and re-calibration sets. Second, **evasion outpacing the sample**:
adversarial content the reviewers don't recognize (new coded language,
re-edited media) reads as compliant, so prevalence undercounts exactly the
newest harms. That forces adversarial red-teaming and evasion-rate tracking as
their own monitoring stream — which is the next section.
</ThinkFirst>

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

- **Adversarial drift:** evasion tactics evolve (obfuscated text, cropped/edited
  media); monitor evasion rates and retrain continually — Meta's RIO exists
  precisely to keep classifiers optimizing against *live* prevalence instead of
  a frozen dataset.
- **Policy changes:** when policy shifts, labels and thresholds must be
  re-versioned — a governance problem as much as an ML one; few-shot models
  bridge the label gap for brand-new policies.
- **Appeals as signal:** overturned removals are precision errors; Meta
  explicitly used past-appeal data to retune its hate-speech classifiers for
  accuracy. Reinstated-after-appeal rates feed both metrics and retraining.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Per-category thresholds | One global threshold | Error costs differ wildly by policy |
| Tiered filter → deep scan → human | All-human or all-model | Only tiering scales to billions with acceptable accuracy |
| Active learning | Random labeling | Violating content is rare; random samples waste label budget |
| Perceptual hash-match + ML | ML only | Known-bad content is caught instantly, exactly, and shareably |
| Few-shot policy conditioning | Wait for labels | Weeks-not-months response to new harm types |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="A benign image gets a harmful caption. How do you catch it?">
Cross-modal reasoning: a vision-language model that scores the image *and* text
*together*, not two independent unimodal classifiers. Context — the caption
recontextualizing the image — is exactly what unimodal pipelines miss, and it's
why Meta's few-shot integrity models are trained multimodally.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you keep up with adversaries obfuscating text (l33tspeak, homoglyphs)?">
Normalize/augment training data with obfuscation transforms, use subword/character
robust encoders, and monitor evasion rate as a live metric so new tactics trigger
targeted labeling and retraining. It's a continual arms race, not a one-time fix.
</Details>

<Details summary="Aggregate precision/recall look great, but a community complains of over-removal. What's wrong?">
Sliced metrics hide it. Break precision/recall down by language, region, and
content type — over-enforcement concentrated in one slice is a fairness failure
even with strong global numbers. Add the slice to your dashboards and
retraining objectives.
</Details>

<Details summary="Your proactive-detection rate fell this quarter. Is the system worse?">
Not necessarily — it depends what moved it. Meta's rate fell ~5 points in 2022
because they deliberately traded recall for precision after appeals showed
over-enforcement. A single rate can't distinguish "catching less" from
"removing less wrongly"; always read proactive rate jointly with prevalence and
appeal-overturn rate.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Moderation is **multimodal classification** + a **human-in-the-loop policy
  machine**; design the loop, not just the classifier.
- **Per-policy thresholds** and **prevalence** (violating views per 10,000)
  are the right targets — never one global accuracy number; proactive rate and
  precision trade off deliberately.
- **Perceptual hashing for known content, classifiers for novel content,
  few-shot models for new policies** — three tools, three time horizons.
- **Active learning, appeals, and fairness slicing** keep it accurate and just as
  adversaries and policy evolve.
