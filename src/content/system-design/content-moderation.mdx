# Design a Content Moderation System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Meta / TikTok"
  scale="billions of posts/day · multimodal · policy-driven · appeals"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

Every day, billions of posts — text, images, video, audio — flow onto a
large platform. Buried in that stream is content that violates policy:
spam, hate speech, graphic violence, self-harm content, child sexual abuse
material (CSAM). The platform must find it and act on it, at a scale where
even a 0.01% error rate means hundreds of thousands of mistakes a day, and
where the two kinds of mistake are both public failures: miss harmful
content and it goes viral; over-remove and you're censoring legitimate
speech.

The instinct is to frame this as "build a harmful-content classifier." That
framing misses what actually makes the system work. Content moderation is a
**multimodal classification system wrapped in a human-in-the-loop policy
machine** — and the machine around the model is the harder design. The
model produces scores; but *policy* decides what counts as violating,
*thresholds tuned per policy* decide what happens at each score, *human
reviewers* handle what the model can't decide and produce the labels the
model learns from, and *appeals* correct the mistakes and feed back
measurement. The interview reward is designing that loop — which is why
this walkthrough leans on the **data → evaluation → feedback** stages of
the ML loop.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
The vocabulary this case leans on, spelled out:

- **Policy taxonomy** — the platform's rulebook: a tree of violation
  categories (violence, hate, spam, CSAM, self-harm, misinformation…),
  each with its own definition, severity, and enforcement rules. Labels
  only mean something relative to this taxonomy.
- **CSAM** — child sexual abuse material; the category where a miss is
  catastrophic and legally reportable, which is why it gets dedicated
  machinery (hash matching) rather than just a classifier.
- **Precision / recall and operating points** — precision: of what we
  removed, how much actually violated; recall: of what violated, how much
  we caught. A threshold picks one point on that tradeoff — see
  [precision/recall & operating points](/wiki/roc-auc).
- **Perceptual hashing** — fingerprints (e.g. PhotoDNA-style) that match
  *known* bad images/videos even after resizing or small edits — exact,
  instant lookup against a database, no ML judgment involved.
- **Vision-language models** — encoders that score an image and its text
  *jointly*, so meaning that only exists in the combination (a benign
  photo + a menacing caption) is visible.
- **Active learning** — choosing which items to spend the labeling budget
  on based on where the model is uncertain, rather than sampling randomly.
- **Inter-annotator agreement** — how often two human reviewers give the
  same item the same label; low agreement means the *policy definition* is
  ambiguous, and no model can beat the noise floor of its own labels.
- **Prevalence** — trust-and-safety's north-star metric: the fraction of
  *views* that land on violating content. Defined here because it's the
  centerpiece of the evaluation section.
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

The goal: detect policy-violating content across all modalities, both at
upload time and retroactively (policies change; old content gets
re-evaluated), and **act** on it. The actions form a ladder, not a
binary: remove outright, age-gate, demote (reduce distribution without
removal), or route to a human reviewer. Which rung is appropriate depends
on both the model's confidence and the category's severity.

The most important thing to say early: **the cost asymmetry between error
types is different for every policy category, so there is no single
threshold.** For CSAM, a miss is catastrophic and the system runs at
maximum recall, accepting false positives freely. For satire that resembles
hate speech, over-removal is the expensive error — suppressing legitimate
expression at scale is its own harm — so the threshold favors precision.
One classifier score, many per-policy operating points.

Scope: the detection and review pipeline for individual pieces of content.
Coordinated-behavior detection (bot networks, influence operations) is an
adjacent system operating on account graphs rather than content — name it,
set it aside.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
"There is no single threshold — each policy category has its own
precision/recall target set by the cost of each error type." Naming this in
the first minutes shows you understand moderation is policy-driven, not a
one-number classifier.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | Billions of items/day | Cheap first-pass filter; human attention only on the margin |
| Modality | Text, image, video, audio | Multimodal encoders; video is by far the most expensive |
| Latency | Fast gating at upload | Tiered: instant filter first, deeper async scan after |
| Adversarial | Deliberate evasion (leetspeak, cropping, re-encoding) | Robust features + continual retraining |
| Fairness / auditability | Decisions explainable and appealable | Reason codes, human review, an appeals path |

The volume row is the one that forces the architecture. Human review
capacity is maybe hundreds of thousands of items a day; the stream is
billions. So humans cannot be *in* the main path — the design question
becomes where to spend the scarce human attention, which the next
ThinkFirst poses directly.

<ThinkFirst question="Human review can't scale to billions of items, and full automation is unacceptable for high-stakes calls. What's the resolution?">
Route by **confidence**. Where the model is very sure content violates,
auto-act. Where it's very sure content is fine, auto-allow. Spend human
attention only on the uncertain middle — and rank that queue by severity
and potential reach, so reviewers see the highest-stakes ambiguous items
first. The design question is never "model or human"; it's *where the
boundary between them sits, separately for each policy category*.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

Formally: **multi-label, multimodal classification** — multi-label because
one post can violate several policies at once, multimodal because meaning
can live in any modality or in their combination. But the classifier's
output doesn't directly become an action; it feeds a **triage decision**
with three outcomes per category: auto-act at very high confidence,
auto-allow at very low, and route to human review in between. The system is
deliberately designed *around where the model is unsure* — humans are
simultaneously the safety net for those cases and the source of every
training label.

What was breaking before this design? Keyword blocklists — the first
generation — have no sense of context (they flag reclaimed slurs and
medical discussion, miss coded language) and are trivially evaded by
obfuscation. Pure human review — the other pole — is accurate but cannot
scale past a tiny fraction of the stream. The tiered model-plus-human
machine is the only architecture that gets both scale and judgment.

<ThinkFirst question="Labels come from human reviewers at real cost per item. You can't label everything — where do you spend the budget?">
On items where a label changes the most: content the model is **uncertain**
about, and content where the model **disagrees** with existing human
decisions — that's active learning. Random sampling mostly buys confident
duplicates of what the model already knows. And because policy judgments
are genuinely subjective, part of the budget goes to labeling the *same*
items with multiple reviewers — measuring inter-annotator agreement tells
you whether your problem is the model or an ambiguous policy definition.
</ThinkFirst>

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

Labels here are unusual in three ways, and each shapes the data system.
They are **expensive** — every label is minutes of a trained human's time,
which is what makes the active-learning strategy above necessary rather
than nice. They are **subjective** — "is this satire or hate speech?" gets
honest disagreement, so labeling guidelines, reviewer training, and
inter-annotator agreement tracking are part of the data infrastructure,
not HR overhead. And they are **non-stationary in a unique way**: when
policy changes, existing labels don't just age — they become *wrong under
the new definition*, and label sets must be versioned against the policy
version they were collected under.

On the feature side, the system needs text encoders, image and video
encoders, and — critically — **vision-language models** that score
modalities *jointly*. The reason is that violation often lives in the
combination: an innocuous photo with a caption that turns it into a threat,
or a slur-laden image macro whose text component is embedded in pixels.
Two independent unimodal classifiers each see half the meaning and both
pass it.

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective)*

The model layer is a hierarchy matched to cost. At the front, **the cheap
and the exact**: perceptual-hash lookups against databases of known
violating content (industry-shared CSAM and terror-content hash sets) catch
re-uploads instantly with essentially perfect precision — no ML judgment
needed for content already adjudicated. Alongside, lightweight text models
handle the obvious at line rate.

Behind that filter sit the **deep classifiers**: multimodal transformers
fine-tuned per policy area, with vision-language models covering the
cross-modal cases. Per-category heads share a common backbone — policies
differ, but the underlying representations transfer.

The **objective** encodes the per-category asymmetry from section 1:
category-specific class weights and thresholds put catastrophic-miss
categories (CSAM, imminent-harm) in high-recall regimes, and
expression-sensitive categories (satire-adjacent hate speech,
misinformation) in precision-favoring ones.

## 6. Serving & the human loop

Follow one uploaded post through the tiers. **Tier 1, at upload,
synchronous**: hash lookups and light models gate the clearly-known-bad
before it's ever visible. **Tier 2, seconds-to-minutes, async**: the deep
multimodal scan scores everything against every policy category. **Tier 3,
the human queue**: items in the uncertain band land in review, ranked by
severity × reach × uncertainty — a borderline post going viral outranks a
borderline post nobody sees. **Tier 4, appeals**: users contest decisions,
and appeals route to senior reviewers whose decisions are treated as the
highest-quality labels in the system.

The loop closes here: every reviewer decision — queue or appeal — flows
back into the training set. The humans aren't just handling edge cases;
they are continuously teaching the model where its boundary should be.

<ThinkFirst question="What's the north-star metric — and why is “amount of violating content removed” the wrong one?">
Removal counts reward volume, not impact: a million zero-view spam posts
removed counts a million, while one violating video that reached ten
million people counts one. The honest target is **prevalence** — the
fraction of *views* that land on violating content that slipped through —
because it weights every miss by the harm it actually caused. Prevalence is
also harder to game: you can inflate removal counts by over-removing;
you can only improve prevalence by actually protecting users.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

Offline evaluation is per-category by construction: precision and recall
at each policy's operating point, with recall watched hardest on the
catastrophic categories. But the headline metric is the one from the
ThinkFirst: **prevalence**, measured by human-auditing a random sample of
*viewed* content. That sampling detail matters — auditing what the model
flagged only measures precision; auditing what users actually saw measures
what got through.

The dimension unique to this domain is **fairness measurement**. Aggregate
precision/recall can be excellent while errors concentrate on one language,
dialect, or community — a model over-removing posts in one language is a
real-world harm and a policy failure even when the global dashboard is
green. Error rates must be sliced by language, region, and content type as
first-class metrics. Finally, continuous **human audits of sampled
auto-decisions** guard against the failure mode where the model drifts and
nobody notices because the confident decisions never reach a human.

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

Three feedback channels, each catching a different failure. **Adversarial
drift**: evaders iterate — obfuscated text, cropped and filtered media,
re-encoded video that dodges hashes — so evasion rate is monitored as a
live metric, and new tactics trigger targeted labeling and retraining
rather than waiting for scheduled cycles. **Policy change**: when the
rulebook shifts, thresholds and label sets must be re-versioned together —
this is governance machinery as much as ML, because training on labels
collected under the old policy silently enforces the old policy.
**Appeals as measurement**: every overturned removal is a confirmed
precision error, so the overturn rate per category is a free, continuous,
adversarially-surfaced precision metric — users appeal exactly the mistakes
that matter to them — feeding both dashboards and retraining.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Per-category thresholds | One global threshold | Error costs differ wildly by policy — CSAM recall vs. satire precision |
| Tiered filter → deep scan → human | All-human or all-model | Only tiering gets both billions-scale and human judgment |
| Active learning | Random labeling | Uncertain/disagreement items are worth far more per labeling dollar |
| Hash-match + ML | ML only | Known-bad content deserves exact, instant matching, not probabilistic scoring |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="A benign image gets a harmful caption. How do you catch it?">
With cross-modal reasoning: a vision-language model that scores the image
*and* text together. Two independent unimodal classifiers each pass their
half — the image is genuinely benign, the caption alone is ambiguous — and
the violation, which exists only in the combination, sails through. This
recontextualization case is the standard argument for joint multimodal
models in moderation.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you keep up with adversaries obfuscating text (l33tspeak, homoglyphs)?">
Three layers: normalize and augment training data with the obfuscation
transforms you've seen (so the model learns them), use character- and
subword-robust encoders (so novel variants degrade gracefully instead of
failing), and monitor evasion rate as a live metric so new tactics trigger
targeted labeling and retraining. It's a continual arms race — the design
goal is a fast loop, not a permanent fix.
</Details>

<Details summary="Aggregate precision/recall look great, but a community complains of over-removal. What's wrong?">
The aggregate is hiding a concentrated slice failure. Break error rates
down by language, region, and content type — over-enforcement focused on
one community is a fairness failure even with strong global numbers, and
it's invisible until you slice. The fix is structural: add the slice to
standing dashboards and to the training objective, so the regression can't
recur silently.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Moderation is **multimodal classification wrapped in a human-in-the-loop
  policy machine** — the loop between model, thresholds, reviewers, and
  appeals is the design, not an accessory to the classifier.
- **There is no single threshold.** Each policy category gets its own
  operating point, set by the cost of each error type — maximum recall for
  catastrophic categories, precision-favoring for expression-sensitive ones.
- The honest north star is **prevalence** — violating *views* that slipped
  through — not removal counts, which reward volume over impact.
- **Active learning, appeals, and fairness slicing** are what keep the
  system accurate and just as adversaries evade, policy evolves, and errors
  try to concentrate where no one is looking.
