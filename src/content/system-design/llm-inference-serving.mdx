# Design an LLM Inference Serving Platform

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="Generative AI System Design"
  company="OpenAI / Together AI"
  scale="thousands of req/s · streaming tokens · GPU-bound · strict SLOs"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

This is the generative-AI **systems/infra** interview: not *what* the model
generates but *how you serve it* efficiently. LLM inference is **autoregressive**
(one token at a time), **memory-bound during decode**, and runs on **scarce,
expensive GPUs**, so the entire design is about squeezing throughput out of GPUs
without blowing latency SLOs. It's pure serving — there's no training loop, so
no SpineNav strip.


## S: System requirements

**Functional.**

- **Goal:** serve LLM completions to many concurrent users — high **throughput**
  (tokens/sec/GPU, i.e. cost) with low **latency**, streaming as tokens are
  produced.
- **Two latency numbers, not one:** **TTFT** (time-to-first-token — how long the
  user stares at nothing) and **TPOT** (time-per-output-token — the streaming
  rate). Interactive chat wants TTFT in the low hundreds of ms and TPOT faster
  than reading speed (~30–50ms/token); batch summarization tolerates seconds.
- **Two-phase workload:** **prefill** (process the whole prompt in parallel,
  compute-bound) then **decode** (generate tokens one at a time,
  memory-bandwidth-bound) — the two phases have opposite performance profiles
  ([Pope et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05102)).
- **Scope:** the serving system. Model training/fine-tuning is out of scope.

**Non-functional.**

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Throughput | Max tokens/sec/GPU (cost) | Continuous batching, big effective batch |
| Latency | TTFT p99 in hundreds of ms; steady TPOT | Prioritize prefill; cap batch to bound tail |
| Memory | KV cache grows with context × batch | PagedAttention; paging, prefix sharing |
| Concurrency | Thousands of streams | Async scheduler; token streaming |
| Cost | GPUs dominate | Quantization, multi-tenancy, autoscaling |

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
Distinguish **prefill vs. decode** and name decode as **memory-bandwidth-bound**:
each step reloads all weights plus the KV cache to produce one token. That single
insight explains continuous batching, KV-cache management, and why throughput and
latency trade off — the core of the whole answer.
</Callout>

<ThinkFirst question="A 13B model in FP16 occupies 26GB of an A100's 40GB. A request arrives with a 2,000-token context. Roughly how much memory does that one request's KV cache need — and what does the arithmetic say about your maximum batch size?">
Per token you cache a key and a value vector per layer: 2 × 40 layers × 5,120
dims × 2 bytes ≈ **800KB per token**. At 2,000 tokens that's ~**1.6GB for one
request**. With ~14GB left after weights, naive per-request allocation caps you
at fewer than ten concurrent sequences — the GPU's compute sits idle because
**memory, not FLOPs, limits the batch**. Every major serving technique attacks
this number.
</ThinkFirst>

## C: Choose the model

In a serving design the model's weights are an input, but its **serving form**
is a choice, and each choice moves the memory arithmetic above:

- **Precision.** 4-bit weight-only quantization (GPTQ/AWQ) shrinks weights
  ~4× and speeds up bandwidth-bound decode ~3× over FP16; AWQ's trick is
  protecting the ~1% of weight channels with the largest activations, which
  preserves quality without mixed precision
  ([Lin et al., MLSys 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00978)). FP8 KV-cache
  quantization doubles cache capacity — i.e. batch size.
- **Partitioning.** Large models are **sharded**: tensor parallelism splits
  each layer's matmuls across GPUs (needs NVLink-class interconnect, cuts
  latency), pipeline parallelism splits layers into stages (cheaper
  interconnect, adds bubbles). The right partitioning depends on batch size and
  latency target — at low batch you shard for weight-bandwidth, at high batch
  for compute — a tradeoff Google mapped analytically for 500B+ models,
  reaching a Pareto frontier of cost vs. latency
  ([Pope et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05102)).
- **A draft model.** A small model from the same family, for speculative
  decoding (section L).
- **Model tiers.** A quantized or smaller replica to absorb overflow when the
  full model's pool is saturated.

## A: Acquire & prepare data

A serving system's "data" is the workload and the checks it must pass:

- **Traffic traces:** prompt- and output-length distributions, arrival
  patterns, and the diurnal curve. Batch sizing, chunk sizes, and autoscaling
  are tuned against these, not against averages.
- **Prefix statistics:** how much of the traffic shares a system prompt or
  few-shot header, which sizes the prefix-cache win.
- **Calibration sets:** a few hundred representative prompts for AWQ/GPTQ
  calibration, and for measuring the draft model's acceptance rate.
- **Correctness suites:** perplexity and task evals that quantized and
  speculative paths must pass against the FP16 reference.

<ThinkFirst question="You batch 32 requests together for throughput. One generates 2,000 tokens; the rest finish in 100. What happens to the GPU — and what's the fix?">
With naive static batching, the 31 finished slots sit idle while the long
request grinds on — the batch runs at the speed of its slowest member, and
newly-arrived requests queue behind it. The fix is scheduling at the **token
level**: every decoding step, finished sequences leave the batch and queued
requests join. That's continuous batching, and it's the single biggest
throughput win in LLM serving.
</ThinkFirst>

## L: Leverage the model *(the core techniques)*

### Batching, memory, and decoding

**Continuous (in-flight) batching.** Orca introduced **iteration-level
scheduling**: the scheduler invokes the engine for *one decode step* at a time,
admitting and evicting sequences between steps, keeping the GPU saturated. On
GPT-3 175B this delivered a **36.9× throughput gain over FasterTransformer at
the same latency**
([Yu et al., OSDI 2022](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi22/presentation/yu)).

<WikiLink slug="continuous-batching" title="Continuous batching — iteration-level scheduling" />

**KV cache + PagedAttention.** Each token attends to all previous tokens' keys/
values; caching them avoids recomputation, but the cache is huge and grows
unpredictably. **PagedAttention** manages it like OS virtual memory — fixed-size
non-contiguous blocks with a per-sequence block table — eliminating internal and
external fragmentation and enabling copy-on-write sharing. vLLM's paged cache
raised throughput **2–4× over FasterTransformer and Orca** at equal latency,
with the edge growing for long sequences
([Kwon et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180)).

<WikiLink slug="paged-attention" title="PagedAttention — paged KV-cache memory" />

**Speculative decoding.** A cheap draft model proposes several tokens; the big
model verifies them in a single parallel pass, accepting a prefix of them via
rejection sampling. Output distribution is **provably identical** to the target
model's, and wall-clock decoding speeds up **2–3×** when the draft's acceptance
rate is high ([Leviathan et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)) —
which it is for predictable spans (code, boilerplate) and less so for creative
prose.

<WikiLink slug="speculative-decoding" title="Speculative decoding — draft-and-verify" />

Inside the kernel, **FlashAttention** computes exact attention with tiling so
the softmax never materializes in HBM — an IO-aware kernel that makes long-prompt
prefill far cheaper ([Dao et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14135)).

<LatencyCriticalPathViz />

<ThinkFirst question="Bigger batches always raise tokens/sec/GPU. So why not run the biggest batch that fits — and what metric captures the real objective?">
Because throughput and tail latency are a **frontier**: a huge batch means new
requests wait behind long prefills and every stream's TPOT degrades. Raw
tokens/sec can rise while more requests *miss their SLO*. The honest metric is
**goodput**: requests served within both the TTFT and TPOT targets, per GPU —
and batch size is chosen as an operating point on the frontier, not maximized.
A system optimized for goodput can look "slower" on a throughput benchmark and
be strictly better in production.
</ThinkFirst>

### Evaluation & SLOs

- **Throughput:** tokens/sec/GPU (the cost metric) at a target concurrency.
- **Latency SLOs:** p50/p99 **TTFT** and **TPOT** measured separately — they
  fail for different reasons (queueing + prefill vs. batch contention).
- **Goodput:** requests/sec/GPU meeting *both* SLOs — the metric DistServe
  optimizes for and the one that should drive capacity planning
  ([Zhong et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09670)).
- **Correctness:** quantized/speculative paths must match the reference model —
  speculative decoding exactly (by construction), quantization within a measured
  quality budget (perplexity + task evals, not vibes).

<ThinkFirst question="Every request to your API starts with the same 2,000-token system prompt. What's the free win, and what mechanism makes it nearly free?">
**Prefix caching**: the KV cache for a shared prefix is byte-identical across
requests, so compute it once and let every request's block table point at the
same physical pages — PagedAttention's copy-on-write makes the sharing safe.
Prefill cost for the shared portion drops to ~zero, cutting TTFT and freeing
compute — one of the rare optimizations with no quality tradeoff at all.
</ThinkFirst>

## E: Estimate resources

The KV-cache arithmetic is the whole capacity story. The vLLM authors measured
that contiguous per-request allocation (sized for max length, fragmented
between requests) wastes **60–80% of KV memory** in prior systems, versus under
4% with paging ([Kwon et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180)). Batch
size, hence throughput, is downstream of that waste.

For the 13B FP16 model on a 40GB A100 (1.56 TB/s of HBM bandwidth):

| Quantity | Arithmetic | Result |
|---|---|---|
| KV per token | 2 × 40 layers × 5,120 dims × 2 bytes | ~800 KB |
| KV per 2K-token request | 2,000 × 800 KB | ~1.6 GB |
| Room for KV | 40 GB − 26 GB weights | ~14 GB ≈ 8 such requests if nothing is wasted |
| Decode floor | 26 GB of weights read per step ÷ 1.56 TB/s | ~17 ms per step ≈ 60 tokens/s for a lone stream |
| Batching | 8 streams share each weight read | ~8× the tokens per step for almost the same time |
| Shared prefix | one 2K-token system prompt cached once | ~1.6 GB saved per concurrent request that shares it |
| 4-bit weights | 26 GB → ~6.5 GB | ~33 GB freed for KV: several times the batch |

Read down the table and the design writes itself: every technique in L and D
either packs more sequences into the KV budget (paging, prefix sharing, FP8 KV,
4-bit weights) or amortizes one weight read over more tokens (batching,
speculative decoding).

<ThinkFirst question="A 3,000-token prompt's prefill and thirty in-flight decode streams share one GPU. The prefill takes ~200ms of solid compute. What do the thirty streaming users experience, and what are your scheduling options?">
Their streams **stall**: every decode step waits behind the monolithic prefill,
so inter-token latency spikes from ~30ms to ~230ms — visible stutter in thirty
UIs to serve one new user. Options: **chunk the prefill** into pieces
interleaved with decode steps (bounded TPOT, slightly worse TTFT), or
**disaggregate**: run prefill and decode on separate GPU pools and ship the KV
cache across, so the phases never contend.
</ThinkFirst>

## D: Design the system

### Serving architecture

Request → **router/scheduler** → GPU worker pool. The scheduler does continuous
batching, interleaves or separates prefill/decode, manages KV-cache pages, and
streams tokens back over SSE/gRPC. A queue with **priority tiers** protects
interactive traffic; an autoscaler adds GPU replicas on queue depth.

```
client ──► API edge (auth, rate limits, quotas) ──► router (priority tiers, prefix-aware placement)
       ──► prefill pool ──(KV cache transfer)──► decode pool      [disaggregated]
           or one colocated pool with chunked prefill              [colocated]
       ──► token stream (SSE / gRPC) ──► client
autoscaler ◄── queue depth, KV occupancy, diurnal forecast
```

### Optimizations & tradeoffs

- **Prefix caching:** share KV pages for common system prompts and few-shot
  headers across requests.
- **Chunked prefill:** split long prompts into chunks interleaved with decode
  steps to protect inter-token latency.
- **Disaggregated prefill/decode:** DistServe assigns the phases to separate GPU
  pools, each with its own parallelism plan, transferring KV caches between
  them — up to **7.4× more requests (or 12.6× tighter SLO)** served within
  latency targets versus colocated serving
  ([Zhong et al., OSDI 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09670)).
- **Quantization:** the 4-bit weights and FP8 KV cache chosen in section C.

<WikiLink slug="paged-attention" title="Memory management is the throughput ceiling" />

<ThinkFirst question="Traffic is near-zero at 4am and spikes 10× at 9am. Loading a 70B model onto a fresh GPU node takes several minutes. How do you autoscale without either burning idle GPUs all night or queueing users all morning?">
You can't scale reactively on request latency — by the time p99 degrades, a
multi-minute cold start guarantees a bad half hour. Scale **predictively** on
queue depth and diurnal forecasts, keep a warm pool with weights pre-loaded
(or stream weights from local NVMe), and admit degraded modes: route overflow
to a quantized replica or a smaller model tier. Cold-start time is a
first-class design constraint, not an ops detail.
</ThinkFirst>

### Operations & guardrails

- **Autoscaling & multi-tenancy:** pack tenants/models per GPU pool, scale on
  queue depth and KV-cache occupancy, pre-warm replicas ahead of forecast load.
- **Observability:** per-request TTFT/TPOT, tokens in/out, GPU utilization,
  KV-cache occupancy, preemption/eviction counts, speculative acceptance rate —
  the dashboard that finds bottlenecks.
- **Fairness/limits:** per-tenant rate limits and token quotas so one user can't
  starve others; abuse and prompt-injection handling at the API edge.

## Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous batching | Static batching | Avoids head-of-line blocking; saturates the GPU |
| PagedAttention | Contiguous KV cache | 60–80% memory waste → under 4%; higher concurrency |
| Speculative decoding | Plain decoding | 2–3× lower latency, provably identical outputs |
| 4-bit quantization | Full precision | ~3× decode speedup for a small, measurable quality cost |
| Disaggregated prefill/decode | Colocated phases | No interference; per-phase parallelism; costs KV transfer |

## Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Why is decode memory-bound while prefill is compute-bound?">
Prefill processes all prompt tokens in one pass — big matmuls, high arithmetic
intensity, compute-bound. Decode produces one token per step, so each step
streams the full weights plus the growing KV cache from HBM for a tiny amount
of compute — arithmetic intensity is ~1 FLOP/byte, far below the GPU's
compute/bandwidth ratio. Bandwidth, not FLOPs, is the ceiling, which is why
batching many decode streams (amortizing the weight reads) is the key
throughput lever, and why weight quantization speeds decode up almost linearly.
</Details>

<Details summary="Throughput is great but p99 time-to-first-token is terrible. Why?">
Big batches maximize throughput but long prompts' prefill blocks the batch,
delaying first tokens for everyone behind them in the queue. Fixes: chunked
prefill (interleave with decodes), priority scheduling for short interactive
prompts, capping batch size, or disaggregating prefill onto a separate pool
(the DistServe result). Throughput and tail latency are a frontier — pick the
operating point per SLO and report goodput, not tokens/sec.
</Details>

<Details summary="A 70B model won't fit on one GPU. How do you serve it?">
Shard it: tensor parallelism splits each layer across GPUs (lowest latency,
needs NVLink-class bandwidth since it all-reduces every layer), pipeline
parallelism splits layers into stages (tolerates slower interconnect, adds
pipeline bubbles). Pope et al. show the optimal partitioning shifts with batch
size and latency target. Combine with 4-bit quantization — 70B weights drop
from ~140GB to ~35GB, turning a 4-GPU problem into a 1–2-GPU one.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- LLM serving is **memory-bound during decode** — do the KV-cache arithmetic
  first; it explains why batch size, not compute, is the ceiling.
- **Continuous batching + PagedAttention** are the throughput foundations
  (36.9× and 2–4× measured gains); **speculative decoding + quantization** cut
  latency and cost with bounded quality impact.
- Serve to **goodput** (requests within TTFT *and* TPOT SLOs) on the
  throughput↔latency frontier; shard large models with tensor/pipeline
  parallelism matched to the interconnect.
