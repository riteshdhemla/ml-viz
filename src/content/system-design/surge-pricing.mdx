# Design a Dynamic / Surge Pricing System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Uber / Lyft"
  scale="per-region/minute pricing · supply–demand balance · fairness"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

Surge pricing is the interview where the **objective and optimization** stages of
the loop dominate — the ML predictions (demand, supply, elasticity) feed a
**decision** that acts back on the market it's predicting. That feedback (a high
price suppresses demand and attracts supply) makes it a control problem, not a
plain prediction problem. The production references are Uber's surge system and
Lyft's **PrimeTime**, and the best natural experiment in the literature is the
night Uber's surge briefly *broke*.

## 1. Clarify the problem

- **Goal:** set a price multiplier per region/time that **balances supply and
  demand** — enough drivers for the riders who want trips — while maximizing a
  long-term objective (completed trips, marketplace health, revenue).
- **Why not a fixed price?** The evidence is unusually clean. When surge worked
  after a sold-out Ariana Grande show at Madison Square Garden (March 2015),
  the multiplier floated between 1.2–1.8x and average wait times held around
  **2.6 minutes** with completion rates near 100%. On New Year's Eve 2014–15, a
  bug disabled surge for **26 minutes**: ETAs tripled to ~8 minutes and the
  share of unfulfilled requests jumped to ~**25%**
  ([Hall, Kendrick & Nosko](https://andrewchen.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/effects_of_ubers_surge_pricing.pdf)).
- **Constraints:** fairness (no exploitative spikes), stability (no wild
  oscillation), transparency/regulation.
- **Scope:** the pricing engine. Matching/dispatch consumes prices (adjacent).

<Callout type="warning" title="The feedback trap">
Price *changes the data*. A model trained on "high price → low demand" can't
conclude "raise prices to reduce demand" naively — that's correlation. You need
**causal** elasticity estimation, or you'll chase your own tail.
</Callout>

<ThinkFirst question="Price is one lever to close a supply-demand gap. Name two others — and why does price usually win at the minutes timescale?">
The other levers are **repositioning supply** (driver incentives, heatmaps,
dispatch radius) and **rationing demand** (longer waits, queueing). But
repositioning takes tens of minutes and incentives leak money, while rationing
by wait time degrades everyone's experience and — worse — collapses matching
efficiency. Price acts on *both sides simultaneously and immediately*: it
sorts riders by willingness to pay and signals drivers toward the gap. The
others are complements on slower timescales, not substitutes.
</ThinkFirst>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Cadence | Update per region every ~minute | Fast forecasting + optimization loop |
| Objective | Long-term marketplace health | RL / optimization, not myopic revenue |
| Stability | No oscillation, bounded changes | Smoothing, rate limits, hysteresis |
| Fairness | No exploitative or discriminatory pricing | Caps, constraints, audits |

<ThinkFirst question="What predictions does the price decision need — and which of them can never be learned from logged data alone?">
Three: demand per region/time, supply and how it responds, and **elasticity** —
how both react to a price change. The first two are forecasting problems logged
data supports. Elasticity is **causal**: historically, prices were high
*because* demand was high, so the observational fit gets the sign of the
relationship wrong. Only deliberate price experiments can estimate it.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

Three ML sub-problems feed one **optimization**:

1. **Demand forecasting** — expected ride requests per region/time.
2. **Supply forecasting** — available drivers, and how supply responds to price.
3. **Elasticity estimation** *(causal)* — how demand and supply respond to price.

Then **optimize** the price multiplier to balance the market against the
long-term objective — framed as constrained optimization or a **reinforcement
learning** / bandit control problem, because today's price affects tomorrow's
supply. Lyft describes PrimeTime exactly this way: successive iterations of a
real-time controller whose job is to keep a volatile marketplace in balance,
not to maximize instantaneous revenue
([Lyft Engineering](https://eng.lyft.com/dynamic-pricing-to-sustain-marketplace-balance-1d23a8d1be90)).

*What was breaking before?* Static or purely reactive pricing overshoots,
oscillates, and can't anticipate predictable peaks. And undersupply doesn't
just mean waiting — Castillo, Knoepfle & Weyl show it triggers the **"wild
goose chase"**: with too few free drivers, the dispatcher matches riders to
faraway cars, drivers burn their time on long pickups, effective supply falls
further, and throughput collapses. Surge exists to keep the market out of that
regime, which is why it's a reliability mechanism before it's a revenue one
([EC 2017](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3033274.3085098)).

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

- **Labels/signals:** historical requests, completions, cancellations, driver
  positions, prices offered and outcomes — including *app opens without
  request*, the cleanest demand-suppression signal you have.
- **Elasticity data:** the hard part — you need **experimental variation** (price
  experiments / geo holdouts) to estimate causal response, since observational
  price–demand correlation is confounded by the conditions that caused the price.
- **Features:** region, time, events, weather, current supply/demand imbalance,
  recent conversion at each price.

<WikiLink slug="uplift-modeling" title="Uplift modeling — estimating causal response to a treatment" />

<ThinkFirst question="Your optimizer recomputes each region's price every minute. What failure mode does the naive version hit almost immediately?">
Oscillation. Price rises → demand dips and supply floods in → the imbalance
flips → price crashes → repeat. The optimizer is a **controller** acting on the
system it measures, so it needs control-systems hygiene: rate limits,
hysteresis, smoothed inputs, and an objective defined over a horizon rather
than the instantaneous imbalance.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model & optimization *(spine: objective + optimization)*

- **Forecasting:** time-series / GBDT models for demand and supply per region;
  Lyft leans on contextual bandits and RL where multiple objectives interact.
- **Elasticity:** causal models estimated from pricing experiments (the treatment
  is the price change).
- **Optimizer:** given forecasts + elasticity, choose the multiplier maximizing
  expected long-term objective subject to fairness/stability constraints —
  constrained optimization, or an RL policy where the state is market imbalance
  and the action is the price.
- **The driver side has its own theory:** Garg & Nazerzadeh prove that the
  classic *multiplicative* surge is not incentive-compatible for drivers when
  surge varies over time — it overpays long trips and tempts drivers to
  decline short surged trips — and that **additive** surge (a flat per-trip
  bonus) is closer to incentive-compatible; Uber's surge moved in this
  direction ([Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2021.4058)).
- **Smoothing:** rate-limit changes and add hysteresis so prices don't flap.

<ThinkFirst question="During a storm your optimizer's unconstrained solution is a 4.9x multiplier in one neighborhood. Ship it?">
No — and not only for PR reasons. Extreme multipliers exploit exactly the
riders with the fewest alternatives, invite regulatory caps written *for* you
rather than by you (post-Sandy, Uber agreed to cap surge during emergencies),
and the trust damage suppresses long-run demand more than the spike earns.
Bound the action space: hard caps, jurisdiction-aware emergency rules, and let
the imbalance clear partly through wait times. The optimizer proposes; the
constraint layer disposes.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Serving & scaling

Per region every minute: pull live supply/demand, forecast the short horizon,
apply the optimizer + constraints + smoothing, publish the multiplier. Bound the
action space and cap maximum surge for fairness and PR safety. Publish
*explainable* prices — riders see the multiplier (or an upfront price) before
requesting, which is itself part of the mechanism: the MSG data shows riders
sorting themselves by willingness to pay when the price is visible upfront.

<ThinkFirst question="Why can't you evaluate a pricing policy offline the way you'd evaluate a ranker — and what breaks standard A/B tests too?">
Offline replay assumes the world would have behaved the same under your new
prices — but the market *reacts* to price, so logged outcomes don't transfer.
And standard A/B assumes independent units, while riders and drivers in
treatment regions spill into control regions (drivers literally drive to the
higher-paying cell). The fix is **switchback** experiments: toggle the policy
over time within a region and compare time-region units — the design DoorDash
uses for exactly this class of marketplace interference
([DoorDash Engineering](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/switchback-tests-and-randomized-experimentation-under-network-effects-at-doordash/)).
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

- **Offline is weak here** — you can't replay a market that reacts to your prices.
  Rely on **geo/time-based experiments** (switchbacks) as the primary
  evaluation, randomizing time-region units rather than individual requests,
  and analyze with cluster-robust errors (naive analysis of switchbacks
  inflates false positives).
- **Metrics:** completion rate, wait times, reliability, driver earnings, revenue,
  and **fairness** metrics — balanced, since optimizing one alone degrades the
  marketplace. The NYE outage numbers (ETAs 3x, 25% unfulfilled) are the
  canonical picture of what "reliability" means here.

<ThinkFirst question="The switchback says your new policy wins on completion rate. Two months after full launch, driver hours are quietly declining. What did the experiment miss?">
Horizon. Switchback windows are hours long, so they capture immediate matching
effects but not **slow supply-side adaptation**: drivers learn the new earnings
distribution over weeks and reduce hours or churn. This is the
incentive-compatibility problem Garg & Nazerzadeh formalize — a policy can
clear the market today while eroding the supply base that clears it tomorrow.
You need long-running holdout regions and driver-cohort retention metrics on
top of switchbacks.
</ThinkFirst>

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

- **Closed-loop control:** monitor for oscillation, over/under-supply, and
  regions stuck at cap. The price acts on the market, so instability is a live
  risk.
- **Slow-loop supply health:** track driver earnings distributions, acceptance
  rates on surged trips, and cohort retention — the metrics that catch
  incentive problems switchbacks can't see.
- **Regime shifts:** events and weather change elasticity; keep experiments
  running to refresh causal estimates. The loop closes hard into **data**.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Causal elasticity (experiments) | Observational price–demand fit | Observational is confounded → wrong sign, chases its own tail |
| RL / long-term objective | Myopic revenue maximization | Short-term greed drives away supply and riders |
| Additive driver surge | Multiplicative surge | Multiplicative isn't incentive-compatible; drivers game trip selection |
| Smoothed, capped prices | Unconstrained optimizer | Oscillation and exploitative spikes destroy trust |
| Switchback experiments | Standard A/B | Market interference breaks unit-independent A/B assumptions |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Why can't you just fit demand vs. price from historical data?">
Confounding. Prices were high *because* demand was high (rush hour, events), so
the naive fit sees "high price ↔ high demand" and gets elasticity backwards. You
need experimental price variation (geo/switchback tests) to estimate the causal
effect of price on demand.
</Details>

<Details summary="Prices are oscillating every minute. How do you stabilize?">
Add hysteresis and rate limits (bounded change per interval), smooth the demand/
supply signals, and design the objective over a horizon so the controller isn't
chasing instantaneous noise. Oscillation is a control-systems failure, not a
forecasting one.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you A/B test pricing when one region's price affects neighbors?">
Standard A/B assumes independent units, which breaks under market interference.
Use **switchback** experiments (toggle the treatment on/off over time in a region)
or region-level randomization with buffer zones, randomize time-region units, and
analyze with cluster-robust standard errors, as DoorDash documents for its
dispatch experiments.
</Details>

<Details summary="Surge is unpopular. Why not just cap it at 1.0 and eat the shortage?">
Because the alternative isn't "same service, lower price" — it's the wild goose
chase: undersupply makes matches longer, which wastes driver hours, which
deepens undersupply. The NYE natural experiment quantifies it: 26 minutes
without surge took unfulfilled requests to ~25%. The honest framing is that
surge trades some riders priced out against the marketplace staying functional
for the rest — then defend the caps and fairness constraints you'd add.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Surge pricing is a **control problem**: predictions feed an **optimizer** whose
  action changes the market it predicts — and whose absence causes measurable
  collapse (ETAs 3x, 25% unfulfilled in Uber's 26-minute outage).
- Elasticity must be **causal** — estimated from price experiments, not
  observational correlation, or the system chases its own tail.
- Evaluate with **switchback experiments** for short-run effects and long-running
  holdouts for supply-side adaptation; multiplicative vs. **additive surge** is
  an incentive-design question, not a cosmetic one.
- **Smooth, cap, and constrain**: the optimizer proposes, the fairness and
  stability layer disposes.
