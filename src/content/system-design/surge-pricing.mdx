# Design a Dynamic / Surge Pricing System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Uber / Lyft"
  scale="per-region/minute pricing · supply–demand balance · fairness"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

It's 11 p.m. on New Year's Eve. In one downtown zone, five thousand
people want rides and two hundred drivers are online. A fixed price
can't fix this — at the normal fare, wait times explode, riders can't
get cars at any reliability, and drivers across town have no reason to
come. So the marketplace moves the one lever it has: **price**. A higher
multiplier trims demand to the riders who value the trip most and pulls
in supply from adjacent zones — and the system must pick that
multiplier, per zone, every minute, all night.

Here's what makes this the most conceptually distinct case in the ML
track: **the system's output changes the world that generates its
inputs.** A ranker predicts clicks; the clicks don't restructure the
catalog. A pricing engine predicts demand — and then sets a price that
*suppresses* that demand and *attracts* new supply, immediately
invalidating the conditions it predicted from. That feedback makes
surge pricing a **control problem, not a prediction problem**, and it's
why the **objective and optimization** stages of the ML loop dominate
this case. It also smuggles in a genuine causal-inference trap that
sinks the naive design, which the first ThinkFirst below sets up.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
This case reaches into causal inference and control theory as much as
ML. What it assumes:

- **Price elasticity** — how much demand (or supply) changes when price
  changes. The number the pricing decision fundamentally needs.
- **Confounding / correlation ≠ causation** — a third variable driving
  both of two correlated quantities. Here: rush hour drives both high
  prices *and* high demand, so historical data shows them positively
  correlated — the causal effect, backwards. Covered in the causal
  inference course.
- **Treatment effects & experiments** — estimating causal effects by
  deliberately varying the treatment (here: price) rather than fitting
  observational data — see [uplift modeling](/wiki/uplift-modeling).
- **Control-systems vocabulary** — a *controller* acts on a system based
  on measurements of it; *oscillation* is the classic instability where
  the controller overshoots and the system flip-flops; *hysteresis*
  (different thresholds for moving up vs. down) and *rate limits* are
  standard dampers.
- **Reinforcement learning framing** — a *policy* mapping states to
  actions, optimizing cumulative long-horizon reward; relevant because
  today's price affects tomorrow's driver supply.
- **Switchback experiments** — an experiment design that toggles a
  treatment on and off *over time* within the same region, used when
  spillover between units breaks standard A/B assumptions. Explained
  fully in the evaluation section.
- **Marketplace terms** — *completion rate* (requests that end in a
  ride), *reliability* (can a rider get a car when they ask), *market
  clearing* (price at which supply meets demand).
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

The goal: set a price multiplier per region per time-slice that
**balances supply and demand** — enough drivers for the riders who want
trips — in service of a *long-term* objective: completed trips,
marketplace reliability, sustainable revenue. The long-term qualifier
is doing real work. A myopic revenue-maximizer would happily spike
prices at every demand peak; the price-gouging headlines, rider churn,
and regulatory attention that follow destroy more value than the spike
captured.

Why is dynamic pricing needed at all? Because at peak, demand far
exceeds supply, and *something* must allocate the scarce cars. Without
a price signal, that something is the queue — wait times balloon and
reliability collapses for everyone. Price is the lever that both clears
the market now and changes supply later, since multipliers are visible
to drivers deciding whether to go online.

The constraints are unusually prominent for an ML system, and they're
design inputs, not afterthoughts: **fairness** (no exploitative spikes
during emergencies — this has ended up in legislatures), **stability**
(prices that whipsaw minute-to-minute destroy trust on both sides), and
**transparency** for regulators. Scope: the pricing engine.
Matching/dispatch consumes prices and is adjacent.

<Callout type="warning" title="The feedback trap">
Price *changes the data*. A model fit on historical logs sees "high
price ↔ high demand" — because surge was triggered *by* demand peaks —
and concludes that raising prices raises demand. That's confounded
correlation with the causal arrow backwards. Elasticity must be
estimated **causally**, from deliberate experiments, or the system
chases its own tail.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Cadence | Update per region every ~minute | Fast forecasting + optimization loop |
| Objective | Long-term marketplace health | RL / horizon optimization, not myopic revenue |
| Stability | No oscillation, bounded changes | Smoothing, rate limits, hysteresis |
| Fairness | No exploitative or discriminatory pricing | Caps, constraints, audits |

<ThinkFirst question="What predictions does the price decision need — and which of them can never be learned from logged data alone?">
Three: demand per region/time, supply and its dynamics, and
**elasticity** — how demand and supply *respond to a price change*.
The first two are forecasting problems that logged data supports
perfectly well. Elasticity is different in kind: it's a **causal**
quantity, and the logs are confounded — historically, prices were high
*because* demand was high, so the observational price–demand fit gets
the sign of the relationship wrong. No amount of logged data fixes
this. Only deliberate price experiments — actually varying price
independent of conditions — can estimate it.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

The framing that keeps the design honest: **three ML sub-problems
feeding one optimization**, with a bright line between the predictive
and the causal.

1. **Demand forecasting** — expected ride requests per region and
   time-slice, from history plus events, weather, and time features.
   Standard supervised forecasting.
2. **Supply forecasting** — available drivers and their dynamics (how
   fast supply flows between zones). Also standard.
3. **Elasticity estimation** — the causal response of demand and supply
   to price, estimable only from experimental variation, per the
   ThinkFirst.

The **optimizer** then chooses each region's multiplier to maximize the
expected long-term objective given the forecasts and elasticities,
subject to the fairness and stability constraints. Two valid framings:
constrained optimization solved each cycle, or a reinforcement-learning
policy — state = market imbalance, action = price — which is the
natural fit precisely because today's price affects tomorrow's supply,
making the problem sequential rather than one-shot.

What was breaking before? Static pricing simply fails to clear the
market at peak. Purely *reactive* pricing — a hand-tuned rule mapping
current imbalance to a multiplier — overshoots, oscillates, and can't
act ahead of the predictable peaks (concert endings, rush hour, storms)
that forecasting sees coming.

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

The observational data is rich and mostly unproblematic: requests,
completions, cancellations (a direct demand-suppression signal — riders
who saw the price and declined), driver positions and session
decisions, and the full history of prices offered with their outcomes.
Features for the forecasters: region, time, event calendars, weather,
the current supply/demand imbalance, and recent conversion at each
offered price.

The hard-won data is the **experimental variation** that elasticity
requires: deliberate price perturbations — randomized small
multiplier changes, geo holdouts — run continuously as a standing cost
of doing business. Continuously, because elasticity itself is not a
constant: riders' price sensitivity at 2 a.m. in the rain differs from
noon on a Tuesday, so causal estimates go stale and must keep being
refreshed. Budgeting permanent experiment traffic is the data-design
decision that separates this system from a pure forecaster.

<WikiLink slug="uplift-modeling" title="Uplift modeling — estimating causal response to a treatment" />

<ThinkFirst question="Your optimizer recomputes each region's price every minute. What failure mode does the naive version hit almost immediately?">
**Oscillation.** Price rises → demand dips and supply floods in → the
imbalance flips to oversupply → the optimizer crashes the price →
drivers leave, demand returns → the imbalance flips back → repeat, every
few minutes. The root cause: the optimizer is a **controller** acting on
the very system it measures, with delays in the response. Controllers
need control-systems hygiene — rate limits on price changes, hysteresis,
smoothed input signals, and an objective defined over a horizon rather
than the instantaneous imbalance — none of which a pure-prediction
mindset would think to add.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model & optimization *(spine: objective + optimization)*

Each sub-problem gets the boring-but-right tool. The demand and supply
**forecasters** are per-region time-series models — GBDTs over lag and
calendar features are the workhorse. The **elasticity models** are
causal-effect estimators fit on the experimental data, where the
treatment is the price change and the response is demand/supply shift —
uplift-model machinery, possibly heterogeneous by region and context
since sensitivity genuinely varies.

The **optimizer** consumes all three and picks the multiplier
maximizing the long-horizon objective under the constraint set — caps,
bounded change rates, fairness rules. Whether it's implemented as
explicit constrained optimization or a learned RL policy, the essential
property is the same: it reasons over a *horizon*, because the sequential
structure (tonight's gouging is next week's driver shortage) is the
problem's defining feature.

And wrapped around the optimizer, the **stabilizers** from the
ThinkFirst: rate limits, hysteresis, signal smoothing. They're not
polish; they're what makes a minute-cadence controller deployable.

## 6. Serving & scaling

The serving loop is compact: per region per minute, pull live supply
and demand, forecast the short horizon, run the optimizer with its
constraints and smoothing, publish the multiplier to the pricing and
driver-facing systems. No single step is exotic — the load is modest by
ML-serving standards — but the loop's *safety envelope* is: the action
space is bounded, maximum surge is capped, and every published price is
audit-logged, because each output is a real price charged to a real
person, and both regulators and the press will eventually read the
logs.

<ThinkFirst question="Why can't you evaluate a pricing policy offline the way you'd evaluate a ranker — and what breaks standard A/B tests too?">
Offline replay assumes the world would have behaved the same under your
new prices — but the entire premise of this system is that the market
*reacts* to price, so logged outcomes don't transfer to counterfactual
prices. And the standard A/B fix breaks too: A/B assumes independent
units, while treatment and control regions share one market — riders
walk across a zone boundary to the cheaper price, drivers migrate
toward the surging zone. The interference contaminates both arms. The
answer is **switchback experiments**: toggle the policy on and off over
time within the same region, and compare the periods.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

Take the ThinkFirst's conclusion seriously: this is the rare ML system
where **offline evaluation is structurally weak**, so live experiments
— switchbacks and carefully-buffered geo tests — are the *primary*
evaluation, not a confirmation step.

The metric set must be a balanced panel, because the marketplace has
three constituencies and optimizing any single number degrades another:
riders (completion rate, wait times, reliability), drivers (earnings,
utilization), and the platform (revenue, plus fairness metrics —
frequency and duration at cap, price distribution across neighborhoods
— because a pricing policy that's profitable and discriminatory is a
failed policy). An evaluation that reports one aggregate number is
hiding whichever constituency it just sacrificed.

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

Operating a controller means monitoring for *control* failures, not
just model drift: oscillation signatures (price variance per region),
persistent over- or under-supply (the controller systematically missing
equilibrium), and regions stuck at the cap (demand the price lever
can't clear — a signal the supply side, not the price, needs
attention).

The feedback stage also owns the standing experiments from section 4:
elasticity estimates decay as seasons, competitors, and rider
expectations shift, so the experiment pipeline never turns off — the
loop closes hard into **data**, permanently. A pricing system that
stopped experimenting is a pricing system whose causal model is
quietly aging into fiction.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Causal elasticity (experiments) | Observational price–demand fit | Observational is confounded → wrong sign → the system chases its own tail |
| RL / long-horizon objective | Myopic revenue maximization | Short-term greed drives away drivers and riders — the sequential structure is the problem |
| Smoothed, capped prices | Unconstrained optimizer | Oscillation and exploitative spikes destroy trust and invite regulation |
| Switchback experiments | Standard A/B | Market interference breaks unit-independence; switchbacks compare a region with itself |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Why can't you just fit demand vs. price from historical data?">
Because the historical price was *set in response to* demand — rush
hour and events caused both the high demand and the high price. The
naive fit therefore shows price and demand rising together and gets
elasticity's sign wrong: it would conclude that raising prices raises
demand. The confounder (underlying demand conditions) can only be
broken by experimental price variation — geo or switchback tests where
price moves independently of conditions.
</Details>

<Details summary="Prices are oscillating every minute. How do you stabilize?">
Treat it as the control-systems failure it is, not a forecasting bug.
Add hysteresis (a higher threshold to raise than to lower), rate-limit
the change per interval, smooth the input signals so the controller
isn't reacting to noise, and define the objective over a horizon so
minute-to-minute imbalance flickers don't translate into price
flickers. Forecast accuracy improvements alone won't fix a loop that's
structurally unstable.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you A/B test pricing when one region's price affects neighbors?">
Standard A/B assumes treatment and control don't interact — false in a
marketplace, where riders and drivers arbitrage across zone borders.
Two designs survive the interference: **switchback experiments**
(toggle the policy over time within a region, so each region is its own
control) and region-level randomization with geographic buffer zones
between arms. In both cases, analyze with the interference explicitly
modeled rather than assumed away.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Surge pricing is a **control problem**: the system's output (price)
  acts back on the market that generates its inputs, which breaks the
  pure-prediction mindset in three separate places — estimation,
  optimization, and evaluation.
- **Elasticity is causal, not observational.** Logged data has the sign
  backwards because price was set in response to demand; only standing
  price experiments can estimate the true response.
- The optimizer is a **controller** and needs control hygiene —
  hysteresis, rate limits, horizon objectives — plus hard caps and
  fairness constraints, because its outputs are real prices charged to
  real people.
- Evaluate with **switchback experiments** on a balanced
  rider/driver/platform metric panel — offline replay can't simulate a
  market that reacts to you, and standard A/B leaks across zone
  boundaries.
