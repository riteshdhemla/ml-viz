# Design a Dynamic / Surge Pricing System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Uber / Lyft"
  scale="per-region/minute pricing · supply–demand balance · fairness"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

Surge pricing is the interview where the **objective and optimization** stages of
the loop dominate — the ML predictions (demand, supply, elasticity) feed a
**decision** that acts back on the market it's predicting. That feedback (a high
price suppresses demand and attracts supply) makes it a control problem, not a
plain prediction problem.

## 1. Clarify the problem

- **Goal:** set a price multiplier per region/time that **balances supply and
  demand** — enough drivers for the riders who want trips — while maximizing a
  long-term objective (completed trips, marketplace health, revenue).
- **Why not a fixed price?** At peak, demand far exceeds supply; without pricing,
  wait times explode and reliability collapses. Price is the lever that clears the
  market and pulls in supply.
- **Constraints:** fairness (no exploitative spikes), stability (no wild
  oscillation), transparency/regulation.
- **Scope:** the pricing engine. Matching/dispatch consumes prices (adjacent).

<Callout type="warning" title="The feedback trap">
Price *changes the data*. A model trained on "high price → low demand" can't
conclude "raise prices to reduce demand" naively — that's correlation. You need
**causal** elasticity estimation, or you'll chase your own tail.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Cadence | Update per region every ~minute | Fast forecasting + optimization loop |
| Objective | Long-term marketplace health | RL / optimization, not myopic revenue |
| Stability | No oscillation, bounded changes | Smoothing, rate limits, hysteresis |
| Fairness | No exploitative or discriminatory pricing | Caps, constraints, audits |

<ThinkFirst question="What predictions does the price decision need — and which of them can never be learned from logged data alone?">
Three: demand per region/time, supply and how it responds, and **elasticity** —
how both react to a price change. The first two are forecasting problems logged
data supports. Elasticity is **causal**: historically, prices were high
*because* demand was high, so the observational fit gets the sign of the
relationship wrong. Only deliberate price experiments can estimate it.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

Three ML sub-problems feed one **optimization**:

1. **Demand forecasting** — expected ride requests per region/time.
2. **Supply forecasting** — available drivers, and how supply responds to price.
3. **Elasticity estimation** *(causal)* — how demand and supply respond to price.

Then **optimize** the price multiplier to balance the market against the
long-term objective — framed as constrained optimization or a **reinforcement
learning** / bandit control problem, because today's price affects tomorrow's
supply.

*What was breaking before?* Static or purely reactive pricing overshoots,
oscillates, and can't anticipate predictable peaks (events, weather, rush hour).

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

- **Labels/signals:** historical requests, completions, cancellations, driver
  positions, prices offered and outcomes.
- **Elasticity data:** the hard part — you need **experimental variation** (price
  experiments / geo holdouts) to estimate causal response, since observational
  price–demand correlation is confounded by the conditions that caused the price.
- **Features:** region, time, events, weather, current supply/demand imbalance,
  recent conversion at each price.

<WikiLink slug="uplift-modeling" title="Uplift modeling — estimating causal response to a treatment" />

<ThinkFirst question="Your optimizer recomputes each region's price every minute. What failure mode does the naive version hit almost immediately?">
Oscillation. Price rises → demand dips and supply floods in → the imbalance
flips → price crashes → repeat. The optimizer is a **controller** acting on the
system it measures, so it needs control-systems hygiene: rate limits,
hysteresis, smoothed inputs, and an objective defined over a horizon rather
than the instantaneous imbalance.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model & optimization *(spine: objective + optimization)*

- **Forecasting:** time-series / GBDT models for demand and supply per region.
- **Elasticity:** causal models estimated from pricing experiments (the treatment
  is the price change).
- **Optimizer:** given forecasts + elasticity, choose the multiplier maximizing
  expected long-term objective subject to fairness/stability constraints —
  constrained optimization, or an RL policy where the state is market imbalance
  and the action is the price.
- **Smoothing:** rate-limit changes and add hysteresis so prices don't flap.

## 6. Serving & scaling

Per region every minute: pull live supply/demand, forecast the short horizon,
apply the optimizer + constraints + smoothing, publish the multiplier. Bound the
action space and cap maximum surge for fairness and PR safety.

<ThinkFirst question="Why can't you evaluate a pricing policy offline the way you'd evaluate a ranker — and what breaks standard A/B tests too?">
Offline replay assumes the world would have behaved the same under your new
prices — but the market *reacts* to price, so logged outcomes don't transfer.
And standard A/B assumes independent units, while riders and drivers in
treatment regions spill into control regions. The fix is **switchback**
experiments: toggle the policy over time within a region and compare periods.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

- **Offline is weak here** — you can't replay a market that reacts to your prices.
  Rely on **geo/time-based experiments** (switchback tests) as the primary
  evaluation.
- **Metrics:** completion rate, wait times, reliability, driver earnings, revenue,
  and **fairness** metrics — balanced, since optimizing one alone degrades the
  marketplace.

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

- **Closed-loop control:** monitor for oscillation, over/under-supply, and
  regions stuck at cap. The price acts on the market, so instability is a live
  risk.
- **Regime shifts:** events and weather change elasticity; keep experiments
  running to refresh causal estimates. The loop closes hard into **data**.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Causal elasticity (experiments) | Observational price–demand fit | Observational is confounded → wrong sign, chases its own tail |
| RL / long-term objective | Myopic revenue maximization | Short-term greed drives away supply and riders |
| Smoothed, capped prices | Unconstrained optimizer | Oscillation and exploitative spikes destroy trust |
| Switchback experiments | Standard A/B | Market interference breaks unit-independent A/B assumptions |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Why can't you just fit demand vs. price from historical data?">
Confounding. Prices were high *because* demand was high (rush hour, events), so
the naive fit sees "high price ↔ high demand" and gets elasticity backwards. You
need experimental price variation (geo/switchback tests) to estimate the causal
effect of price on demand.
</Details>

<Details summary="Prices are oscillating every minute. How do you stabilize?">
Add hysteresis and rate limits (bounded change per interval), smooth the demand/
supply signals, and design the objective over a horizon so the controller isn't
chasing instantaneous noise. Oscillation is a control-systems failure, not a
forecasting one.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you A/B test pricing when one region's price affects neighbors?">
Standard A/B assumes independent units, which breaks under market interference.
Use **switchback** experiments (toggle the treatment on/off over time in a region)
or region-level randomization with buffer zones, and analyze with interference in
mind.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Surge pricing is a **control problem**: predictions feed an **optimizer** whose
  action changes the market it predicts.
- Elasticity must be **causal** — estimated from price experiments, not
  observational correlation, or the system chases its own tail.
- Evaluate with **switchback experiments**, optimize a **long-term** marketplace
  objective, and **smooth/cap** for stability and fairness.
