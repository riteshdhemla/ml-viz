# Design a Real-Time Voice Agent

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="Agentic System Design"
  company="OpenAI Realtime / Retell"
  scale="<800ms turn latency · streaming ASR/TTS · barge-in"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

Call a dentist's office and an AI receptionist answers. You say "I need
to move my Thursday cleaning to next week," and — this is the crucial
part — it starts replying about half a second after you stop talking.
It checks the calendar mid-sentence, offers two slots, and when you
interrupt it halfway through the second option ("the first one's
fine"), it stops talking instantly and books it.

In a text chat, a two-second pause before the reply is invisible. In
spoken conversation, humans feel a pause beyond roughly 500–800ms as
awkward, and beyond two seconds as broken — the caller starts saying
"hello? are you there?" That single fact about human conversation is
the entire design: **latency is the architecture.** The agent
machinery inside — tools, guardrails, context — is the same as the
support agent's; the differentiator is a real-time pipeline where
listening, thinking, and speaking **stream and overlap** instead of
taking turns. Walk it through the agentic project loop with the
latency budget in hand.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
Voice adds a layer of speech infrastructure on top of the standard
agent stack:

- **ASR (automatic speech recognition)** — speech-to-text; *streaming*
  ASR emits partial transcripts while the speaker is still talking.
  **WER** (word error rate) is its accuracy metric. Covered in the
  speech-audio course.
- **TTS (text-to-speech)** — synthesis of spoken audio; *streaming*
  TTS starts producing audio from the first sentence of text without
  waiting for the rest.
- **VAD (voice activity detection) & endpointing** — detecting when
  someone is speaking, and deciding when their turn has *ended* (that
  silence = done, not just a pause for breath). Endpointing errors are
  why systems cut people off or wait awkwardly.
- **Barge-in** — the user speaking while the agent is talking; natural
  conversation is full of it, so the agent must stop and listen
  mid-sentence.
- **Time-to-first-token / time-to-first-audio** — how quickly the LLM
  emits its first token, and how quickly the caller hears the first
  sound; the perceived-latency metrics.
- **Speculative decoding, continuous batching, PagedAttention** — LLM
  serving optimizations for latency and concurrency — see
  [continuous batching](/wiki/continuous-batching) and
  [PagedAttention](/wiki/paged-attention).
- **Telephony (SIP/PSTN)** — the phone-network plumbing: jitter,
  packet loss, and echo are real audio-path concerns, not just
  networking trivia.
- **Slots** — dialogue-system term for the structured fields a
  conversation fills in (date, address, payment amount); used below in
  the confirmation policy.
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the task *(spine: task)*

The goal: hold a natural spoken conversation that accomplishes
something — book an appointment, answer a question, take an order —
calling tools mid-conversation as needed.

The bar is **responsiveness**, and it decomposes into two
requirements. *Sub-second turn latency*: the gap between the caller
finishing and the agent starting to speak. And *natural turn-taking*:
knowing when the caller is done (endpointing), not talking over them,
and handling **barge-in** — because real callers interrupt constantly,
and an agent that plows on through an interruption feels more broken
than one that mishears.

Structurally, the task is a modality bridge: audio in → text
reasoning → audio out, with every stage streamed. Scope: the real-time
voice pipeline and its agent loop; the tool-use and guardrail patterns
are inherited from the support-agent case and only their
voice-specific twists are covered here.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
"Latency is the constraint that shapes everything — I'll stream and
overlap ASR, LLM, and TTS rather than run them in sequence." Leading
with the latency budget signals you've built voice, not just chat with
a microphone.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & constraints

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Turn latency | < 800 ms perceived | Stream + overlap ASR/LLM/TTS; speak first tokens early |
| Turn-taking | Detect end-of-speech, allow barge-in | VAD, endpointing, interruptible TTS |
| Robustness | Noise, accents, disfluency | Strong streaming ASR; tolerate partial transcripts |
| Scale | Many concurrent calls | Efficient serving; telephony integration |

<ThinkFirst question="Add up a sequential pipeline: full ASR (~300ms after end of speech) → LLM response (~1–2s) → TTS synthesis (~300ms). Does it fit the 800ms budget — and if not, where does the time come back from?">
It doesn't — the sequence is 2–3× over budget before you've added
network. And no component is individually slow; the *architecture* is
slow, because each stage idles while the previous one finishes. The
time comes back from **overlap**: ASR streams partial transcripts
while the user is still talking; the LLM starts reasoning on the
near-final transcript before the turn formally ends; TTS begins
speaking the first sentence while the rest of the response is still
generating. No stage waits — the pipeline is concurrent by design, and
that concurrency *is* the answer to the latency budget.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. Context & tools *(spine: context)*

The pipeline has four components: streaming **ASR** turning speech to
text, the **LLM** doing the reasoning and tool calls, streaming
**TTS** turning response text back to audio, and the
**VAD/endpointer** deciding turn boundaries — the small component with
outsized impact, since a 300ms-too-eager endpointer cuts callers off
and a 500ms-too-patient one adds half a second to every single turn.
(The emerging alternative collapses the cascade into one end-to-end
speech-to-speech model — lower latency and more natural prosody,
traded against the debuggability and control of separate stages; the
tradeoffs table returns to this.)

The **tools** are exactly the typed function calls of any text agent —
`check_calendar`, `book_appointment` — invoked mid-conversation. The
**context** is conversation history plus task state, kept deliberately
compact: in voice, every extra token of context is prefill time, and
prefill time is pause.

## 4. Orchestration *(spine: orchestration)*

The loop is **listen → understand → act/speak** — but the ThinkFirst's
overlap principle applies to every arrow. While the caller speaks, ASR
emits partials and the endpointer watches for the turn's end. The LLM
starts on the near-final transcript and **streams tokens**; TTS starts
speaking the first sentence before the rest exists. When a tool call
happens mid-turn, the agent covers the dead air conversationally —
"let me check that for you" — because two seconds of silence on a
phone line reads as a dropped call.

**Barge-in** is a first-class path through the loop: the instant VAD
detects the caller speaking over the agent, TTS stops and flushes its
queued audio, and the system returns to listening — folding the
interruption into context, since what the caller interrupted *with*
("the first one's fine") is usually the most important utterance of
the call.

<ThinkFirst question="Which single number best predicts whether the agent feels responsive — total response time, or something else?">
**Time-to-first-audio.** Callers perceive the pause before the agent
*starts* speaking, not the duration of the full answer: a response
that begins in 400ms and talks for ten seconds feels snappy, while one
that starts after two seconds feels broken no matter how brief.
That's also *why* the overlap architecture works — streaming doesn't
reduce total compute at all; it moves the first audible syllable
earlier, which is the only place the caller is measuring. Optimize
and monitor the start, not the total.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

The metric stack mirrors the design's priorities. **Latency first**:
p50/p95 turn latency and time-to-first-audio, per the ThinkFirst — the
primary product metric, watched per pipeline stage. **ASR quality**:
word error rate on *domain* audio — real callers with accents,
background noise, and jargon, not clean benchmark speech — because
every downstream behavior sits on the transcript. **Task success**:
did the call accomplish its goal (booking completed, question
answered), evaluated on transcripts like any agent outcome metric.
And **conversation quality** — clean interruption handling, natural
turn-taking, never talking over the caller — which resists automation
and leans on human rating of sampled calls.

<ThinkFirst question="ASR might mis-hear a payment amount or an address. Confirm every detail with the user, then?">
No — and the reason is the same latency currency as everything else:
each confirmation costs a full conversational turn, and an agent that
confirms everything feels like a phone tree from 2005. The policy is
**selective confirmation**: confirm only high-stakes, hard-to-reverse
slots — payment amounts, identity, addresses — and use ASR confidence
as the trigger, so a clearly-heard date flows through while a
mumbled street address gets "I heard 742 Evergreen — is that right?"
Safety spent uniformly is latency wasted; spend it exactly where an
error hurts.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Guardrails & safety *(spine: guardrails)*

The action-safety layer is inherited wholesale from the tool-using
agent playbook: permissioned tools, human-in-the-loop for risky
actions, injection defense applied to *transcribed* content (a caller
can speak an injection attempt as easily as type one).

The voice-specific additions: **graceful ASR failure** — the selective
confirmation policy above, plus tools designed to tolerate correction
("actually, make that 4pm") — and **disclosure**: the agent identifies
itself as an AI, which is both an ethical norm and, in a growing
number of jurisdictions, a legal requirement for automated calls.

The through-line from the ThinkFirst is worth restating as a
principle: in voice, **latency and safety trade against each other
turn by turn** — every safeguard that adds a turn must justify itself
against the conversational cost.

## 7. Operations *(spine: operations)*

**Serving** is where the concurrency requirement bites: many
simultaneous calls, each needing low-latency inference. The stack:
continuous batching to keep GPU utilization high across concurrent
streams, PagedAttention for memory efficiency with many active
contexts, speculative decoding to cut time-to-first-token, and
optimized ASR/TTS models sized for real-time.

**Tracing** logs audio, transcripts, per-stage latencies, and tool
calls — and the per-stage latency breakdown is the operational tool:
when turn latency regresses, the trace says whether the endpointer,
the LLM, or TTS ate the budget, which are three different fixes.

**Telephony** is the unglamorous last mile: SIP/PSTN integration, and
audio-path realities — jitter, packet loss, echo cancellation — that
can destroy an otherwise-perfect pipeline, because the best endpointer
in the world can't recover a turn boundary from choppy audio.

## 8. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Streamed, overlapped pipeline | Sequential ASR→LLM→TTS | Sequential blows the latency budget 2–3× |
| Cascaded ASR+LLM+TTS | End-to-end speech-to-speech | Cascade is debuggable and controllable; e2e is lower-latency and more natural but opaque |
| Speak first tokens early | Wait for full response | Time-to-first-audio dominates perceived latency |
| Confirm only high-stakes slots | Confirm everything | Every confirmation costs a slow turn; spend safety where errors hurt |

## 9. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="The user interrupts mid-sentence. What happens?">
The barge-in path fires: VAD detects incoming speech while TTS is
playing, TTS halts immediately and flushes its queued audio, and the
agent returns to listening — treating what the caller said as the new
input, in the context of what the agent had been saying when
interrupted. This must be a first-class path because interruption is
how humans converse; an agent that finishes its paragraph over the
caller's voice is the single most unnatural failure a voice system
can produce.
</Details>

<Details summary="Where does the 800ms go, and how do you cut it?">
The budget splits across: endpointing delay (deciding the caller is
done) + ASR finalization, LLM time-to-first-token, TTS
time-to-first-audio, and network. Each has its own cut: stream
partial transcripts and start LLM reasoning before end-of-speech is
certain (reclaims the endpointing wait), speculative decoding for
time-to-first-token, sentence-by-sentence TTS for time-to-first-audio.
The meta-answer: trace per-stage latency and attack the actual
bottleneck — teams routinely optimize the LLM when the endpointer is
eating 400ms.
</Details>

<Details summary="ASR mis-hears a critical detail (an address, a date). How do you handle it?">
With the selective-confirmation policy: high-stakes slots get an
explicit read-back ("I heard 742 Evergreen — correct?"), triggered by
ASR confidence so clear audio flows through unimpeded, and the tools
are designed to accept corrections gracefully. The line being defended:
never act on a low-confidence transcript for an irreversible action.
Targeted confirmation buys that safety for one turn of latency on
exactly the slots that warrant it — instead of taxing every turn of
every call.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- For a voice agent, **latency is the architecture**: the 800ms human
  tolerance for conversational pauses forces streaming and overlap
  across ASR, LLM, and TTS — no stage waits for another.
- The perceived-latency metric is **time-to-first-audio**: streaming
  moves the first syllable earlier, which is the only moment the
  caller measures.
- **Turn-taking is the interaction model**: endpointing decides when
  to speak, barge-in decides when to shut up, and both are first-class
  paths, not edge cases.
- Reuse the standard **tool and guardrail patterns**, but budget
  safety in turns: **confirm only high-stakes, low-confidence slots**,
  and serve concurrency with continuous batching and fast-first-token
  optimizations.
