# Design a Demand Forecasting System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Amazon / Instacart"
  scale="millions of series · multi-horizon · hierarchical · probabilistic"
  difficulty="intermediate"
/>

A retailer has to decide, tonight, how many units of every product to send
to every store — and the same forecast, aggregated up, decides how much the
regional warehouse orders from suppliers weeks in advance, and how many
staff each store schedules. Order too little and shelves go empty
(stockouts: lost sales and angry customers). Order too much and capital
rots in warehouses (holding cost, spoilage). The forecast is where all of
those decisions get their numbers.

This is the canonical **time-series at scale** interview, and the phrase
"at scale" changes the problem in three ways that a textbook
fit-an-ARIMA-to-one-series exercise never touches. First, there isn't one
series — there are *millions* (every item × every store), most of them
sparse and noisy. Second, the forecasts must be **coherent across a
hierarchy**: the per-store forecasts have to sum to the regional forecast,
because different people act on each level and contradictory numbers mean
conflicting decisions. Third, the output must be **probabilistic** — a
distribution, not a number — because the downstream decision is about risk:
you don't stock the *average* demand, you stock enough to hit a target
probability of not running out. This walkthrough follows the ML project
loop with those three pressures in view.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
Forecasting has its own vocabulary, drawn from both statistics and supply
chains. What this case assumes:

- **SKU** — stock-keeping unit; retail's word for "a distinct product."
  The finest grain of the forecast is SKU × store.
- **Service level / P90** — "stock to the P90" means holding enough
  inventory to cover demand in 90% of scenarios. Quantiles of the demand
  distribution (P50 = median, P90, …) are the currency of inventory
  decisions.
- **Stockout & censored demand** — when inventory hits zero, sales stop
  but *demand doesn't*. Recorded sales during a stockout are a floor on
  true demand, not a measurement of it — statisticians call such data
  "censored." This distinction drives a whole subsection below.
- **ARIMA / ETS / seasonal-naive** — the classical single-series methods:
  ARIMA models a series from its own lags, ETS from trend + seasonality
  components, and seasonal-naive simply predicts "same as this time last
  cycle" — an embarrassingly strong baseline.
- **Global vs. local models** — a *local* model is fit to one series; a
  *global* model is trained once across all series, learning shared
  patterns.
- **Covariates** — side inputs the forecast conditions on: price,
  promotions, holidays, weather. Some (a promo calendar) are known for the
  *future*, which is what makes them so useful.
- **Quantile (pinball) loss** — the training loss that makes a model
  predict a chosen quantile instead of the mean; needed to output the
  distribution that service levels demand.
- **Walk-forward validation** — backtesting that always trains on the past
  and tests on the future, rolling the split forward — see
  [walk-forward validation](/wiki/walk-forward-validation).
- **Reconciliation (MinT, top-down, bottom-up)** — post-processing that
  adjusts forecasts at every level of the hierarchy so they add up — see
  [hierarchical forecasting](/wiki/hierarchical-forecasting).
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

The goal: forecast demand for every item at every location, over horizons
from tomorrow (store replenishment) out to next quarter (supplier orders,
capacity planning), feeding ordering, staffing, and logistics systems.

Why must the output be probabilistic? Because the consumer of the forecast
is making a *risk* decision. The inventory optimizer doesn't ask "what's
expected demand?" — it asks "how much must I stock to have a 90% chance of
not running out?" That's a quantile question, and answering it requires the
model to produce a distribution. The point forecast is almost a by-product;
**the distribution is the product**.

Why must forecasts reconcile? Because the hierarchy — SKU → category →
store → region → total — has a different decision-maker at every level.
If the store forecasts sum to 12,000 units but the regional forecast the
warehouse planner sees says 10,000, the two of them are now planning
against contradictory futures. Coherence isn't aesthetic; it's what lets an
organization act on the numbers.

Scope: the forecasting system itself. The inventory optimizer that turns
quantiles into order quantities is the adjacent system — name the
interface (it reads quantiles), stay out of its internals.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
"We forecast a distribution, not a number, and reconcile across the
hierarchy." That one sentence — probabilistic + hierarchical — signals
production forecasting experience rather than a one-series ARIMA exercise.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Series count | Millions (item × location) | Global models, not one model per series |
| Horizons | Multi-horizon (1 day … 1 quarter) | Direct multi-horizon or recursive forecasting strategy |
| Coherence | Forecasts reconcile across the hierarchy | Explicit reconciliation step (MinT / top-down) |
| Output | Quantiles / full distribution | Quantile loss or probabilistic models |
| Events | Holidays, promotions, weather | Covariate features, including future-known ones |

<ThinkFirst question="Millions of item × store series. Would you fit one model per series? What breaks — and what's the alternative?">
Per-series models break three separate ways. Operationally, fitting and
maintaining millions of models doesn't scale. Statistically, most series
are too sparse to support their own model — a slow-moving SKU sells a few
units a week, and a *new* SKU has no history at all. And structurally,
independently-fit forecasts won't sum coherently across the hierarchy.
The modern answer is one **global** model trained across all series —
which shares statistical strength, so the sparse and new items borrow
patterns from similar ones — followed by a reconciliation step for
coherence.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

Putting the pieces together: **probabilistic, multi-horizon, hierarchical
time-series forecasting**, solved with a **global model** across all
series plus **reconciliation** for coherence.

What was breaking before this framing? The classical approach — one ARIMA
or ETS per series — fails exactly along the three axes from the ThinkFirst:
scale, sparsity, and coherence. And the pre-statistical approach — moving
averages and buyer intuition — is blind to precisely the things that move
demand hardest: promotions, seasonality, and events. Each element of the
modern framing repairs a specific one of these failures, which is worth
saying explicitly in an interview rather than presenting the stack as
received wisdom.

<ThinkFirst question="Your training target is historical sales. When is “sales” not “demand” — and what does the model learn if you ignore the difference?">
Whenever you **stocked out**. Sales flatline at the inventory you had, not
at what customers wanted — the observation is censored. Train on raw sales
and the model dutifully learns the suppressed number as true demand,
forecasts low, causes the optimizer to order less — which manufactures the
next stockout. It's a self-reinforcing under-forecasting spiral, and it's
invisible in the training metrics because the model fits the (censored)
data perfectly well. Censored periods must be masked or demand-corrected
before training.
</ThinkFirst>

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

The target is historical *demand* — and the previous ThinkFirst just
established why that is not the same column as historical *sales*. The
data pipeline needs stockout flags joined against the sales history, so
censored periods can be masked out of the loss or replaced with imputed
demand estimates. This correction is the single most consequential data
decision in the system, because errors here feed back into the very
inventory decisions that generate the next round of training data.

Covariates split into two kinds, and the distinction matters at forecast
time. *Past-observed* covariates (weather, competitor activity) are known
only historically. *Future-known* covariates — price, the promotion
calendar, holidays, day-of-week — are known for the forecast horizon too,
and they're the most valuable inputs the model has: a promo that
triples sales is only predictable if the model is told the promo is coming.

For **cold-start items** — new SKUs with zero history — the global model
is the safety net: attribute features (category, brand, price point) let
the new item inherit the demand patterns of similar existing items until
it accumulates history of its own.

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective)*

Always start with the humbling baseline: **seasonal-naive** ("predict what
happened this time last week/year") and classical per-series ARIMA/ETS on
the dense series. These set the bar every sophisticated model must beat —
and in forecasting, embarrassingly often the fancy model beats them by
less than its complexity costs. Quoting this fact in an interview is a
credibility signal.

The production model is **global**, and comes in two main flavors.
Gradient-boosted trees over lag features and calendar features are the
pragmatic default: tabularize the series (lags, rolling stats, covariates)
and let the trees learn cross-series structure. Deep sequence models —
DeepAR, N-BEATS, Temporal Fusion Transformer — earn their extra cost when
there are many series with shared patterns and rich covariates to exploit.
Either way, the **objective is quantile (pinball) loss** at several target
quantiles, because section 1 established that the distribution is the
product — a model trained only on squared error hands the inventory
optimizer a mean it cannot use.

The final stage is **reconciliation**: the base forecasts at each level of
the hierarchy get adjusted (MinT optimally, or simpler top-down/bottom-up
schemes) so every level sums consistently.

## 6. Serving & scaling

Unusually for this course's case studies, the serving story is **batch**.
Forecasts regenerate on a schedule — nightly for short horizons, weekly
for long — and land as quantile tables in a store the inventory optimizer
reads. Nothing here needs millisecond latency; the engineering pressure is
*throughput* (regenerating millions of series' forecasts inside the batch
window) and *coherence*, not request latency. The one dynamic requirement:
when a major future-known covariate changes — the promo calendar shifts, a
weather event is forecast — affected series need out-of-cycle recomputes.

<ThinkFirst question="How would you construct the train/test split — and why does the split you'd use for tabular ML overstate accuracy here?">
A random split leaks the future. With time series, a randomly-held-out
Tuesday is surrounded by training data from Monday and Wednesday — the
model has effectively seen the answer's neighborhood, so seasonality and
trend are given away and accuracy is overstated, sometimes wildly. The
honest protocol is **walk-forward (rolling-origin) backtesting**: train on
everything up to time T, forecast beyond T, roll T forward, repeat. And
because the outputs are quantiles, scoring needs pinball loss and coverage
checks, not just point-error metrics.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

Evaluation happens on three levels, from statistical to economic. For
**point accuracy**, use scale-free metrics — WMAPE or MASE — because raw
MAPE explodes on slow-moving items where actual demand is near zero, and
you need to compare error across series of wildly different volumes. For
the **distribution**, pinball loss scores each quantile, and *coverage*
checks honesty: the P90 forecast should exceed actual demand in 90% of
cases — no more (wasteful) and no less (undershooting the service level).
All of it under walk-forward backtesting, per the ThinkFirst above.

The third level is the one the business actually feels: **service level
achieved versus inventory cost**. The forecast is a means to an ordering
decision — a model that improves WMAPE by 2% but doesn't move stockout
rate or holding cost hasn't improved anything the company measures.
Evaluation should reach at least one step down the decision chain.

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

Demand is non-stationary on every timescale — trends drift, competitors
move, and occasionally the world breaks the model entirely (a pandemic, a
supply shock). Monitoring therefore tracks error *by segment* (category,
region, horizon), because regime changes rarely announce themselves
globally; they show up as one category's error quietly doubling.
Scheduled retraining absorbs slow drift; segment alarms trigger
investigation for fast breaks.

And the loop-closing subtlety this system uniquely owns: the
**forecast-stockout feedback cycle** from section 3. This system's outputs
cause the inventory decisions that generate its next training data. The
stockout-aware demand correction isn't a one-time data-cleaning step — it
is a permanent part of the feedback loop, standing between the model and
the under-forecasting spiral.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Global model across series | One model per series | Scales to millions, shares strength, handles cold-start |
| Probabilistic (quantiles) | Point forecast | Inventory decisions are service-level questions — quantile questions |
| Reconciliation step | Independent per-level forecasts | Incoherent totals mean different teams plan against different futures |
| Walk-forward backtest | Random train/test split | Random splits leak the future and overstate accuracy |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Store forecasts don't sum to the regional forecast. Problem?">
Yes — a real one, not a cosmetic one. Regional buyers and store managers
are now acting on contradictory numbers: the warehouse stocks for 10,000
units while the stores collectively plan for 12,000. Apply reconciliation —
MinT optimally combines information from all levels — so the
SKU → store → region → total hierarchy is coherent and everyone plans
against the same future.
</Details>

<Details summary="A big promotion is coming — how does the model know?">
Because the promo calendar is a **future-known covariate**: past
promotions and their observed lifts are in the training data, and the
upcoming calendar is supplied as an input at forecast time, so the model
conditions on it. Without promo features, the model has seen promo spikes
only as unexplained noise — so it partially smooths them into baseline
(overforecasting normal weeks) while still missing the spike itself.
</Details>

<Details summary="You stocked out last week — won't the model learn low demand?">
Only if you feed it raw sales. A stockout **censors** demand — you sold
exactly what you had, which says nothing about what customers wanted. Use
stockout-aware demand estimates or mask the censored periods from the
loss; otherwise the model learns downward-biased demand, the optimizer
under-orders, and you've automated the next stockout.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Demand forecasting at scale means **millions of series, multiple
  horizons, a hierarchy, and probabilistic outputs** — and each of those
  four words eliminates the naive one-series approach for a different
  reason.
- **The distribution is the product.** Downstream decisions are
  service-level (quantile) questions, so train with quantile loss and
  verify coverage, not just point error.
- A **global model** shares strength across series and rescues sparse and
  new items; **reconciliation** makes the hierarchy's numbers add up so the
  organization can act on them.
- The two correctness essentials are **walk-forward backtesting** (random
  splits leak the future) and **censored-demand handling** (raw sales
  during stockouts teach the model to cause more stockouts).
