# Design a Demand Forecasting System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Amazon / Instacart"
  scale="millions of series · multi-horizon · hierarchical · probabilistic"
  difficulty="intermediate"
/>

Demand forecasting powers inventory, staffing, and supply-chain decisions. It's
the canonical **time-series at scale** interview: not one series but *millions*
(item × store × time), at multiple horizons, where the forecasts must be
**coherent** across a hierarchy and **probabilistic** because the downstream
decision is about risk. The reference system is Amazon's, which generates
demand forecasts for **over 400 million items daily** with a single unified
deep model
([Amazon Science](https://www.amazon.science/latest-news/the-history-of-amazons-forecasting-algorithm)).
Walk it through the **ML project loop**.

## 1. Clarify the problem

- **Goal:** forecast demand per item/location over multiple horizons (next day →
  next quarter) to drive ordering, staffing, and logistics.
- **Why probabilistic:** you don't stock the mean — you stock to a service level
  (e.g. P90) to trade off stockouts vs. holding cost. The *distribution* is the
  product; Amazon's system has output **quantiles**, not points, since its
  random-forest era.
- **Hierarchy:** SKU → category → store → region → total must **reconcile** —
  the store forecasts should sum to the regional one.
- **Scope:** the forecasting system. The inventory optimizer consumes forecasts
  (adjacent).

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
"We forecast a distribution, not a number, and reconcile across the hierarchy."
Naming probabilistic + hierarchical forecasting immediately signals you've done
this in production, not just fit an ARIMA to one series.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Series count | Millions (item × location) | Global models, not one model per series |
| Horizons | Multi-horizon (1d … 1q) | Direct multi-horizon or recursive strategy |
| Coherence | Forecasts reconcile across hierarchy | Reconciliation step (MinT / top-down) |
| Output | Quantiles / full distribution | Quantile loss or probabilistic models |
| Events | Holidays, promos, weather | Event features / covariates |

<ThinkFirst question="Millions of item × store series. Would you fit one model per series? What breaks — and what's the alternative?">
Per-series models break three ways: the fitting itself doesn't scale, sparse
and new items have too little history to fit anything, and independent
forecasts won't sum coherently across the hierarchy. The modern answer is one
**global** model trained across all series — sharing statistical strength, so a
new SKU borrows from similar ones — plus a reconciliation step for coherence.
This is exactly the DeepAR insight: train a single autoregressive network on
the whole panel of related series.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

**Probabilistic, multi-horizon, hierarchical time-series forecasting.** The modern
answer is a **global model** trained across *all* series (sharing statistical
strength, handling cold-start items) rather than a local model per series, plus a
**reconciliation** step to make the hierarchy coherent. Amazon's published
lineage traces this arc: quantile random forests (SQRF) with hand-engineered
features → **DeepAR** and **MQ-RNN/MQ-CNN** → a unified transformer-era model —
"a decade-plus-long journey," not one breakthrough.

*What was breaking before?* One ARIMA per series doesn't scale to millions, can't
borrow strength for sparse/new items, and produces incoherent totals; simple
moving averages ignore promotions, seasonality, and events; and the
feature-engineered SQRF still needed humans to hand-craft features per product
line.

<ThinkFirst question="Your training target is historical sales. When is “sales” not “demand” — and what does the model learn if you ignore the difference?">
Whenever you **stocked out**: sales flatline at inventory, not at what
customers wanted. Train on raw sales and the model learns suppressed demand as
true demand, forecasts low, orders less, and manufactures the next stockout.
Censored periods must be masked or demand-corrected before training. At
Instacart the same problem appears one level down: predicting real-time
*availability* of 200M+ grocery items is its own ML system, because observed
sales say nothing when the shelf was empty.
</ThinkFirst>

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

- **Target:** historical demand (careful: *sales* ≠ *demand* when you stock out —
  censored demand is a real bias). Instacart runs a dedicated **availability
  model** (XGBoost over item, time, and shopper-scan features) to estimate what
  was actually on shelves
  ([Instacart Engineering](https://tech.instacart.com/predicting-real-time-availability-of-200-million-grocery-items-in-us-canada-stores-61f43a16eafe)).
- **Covariates:** price, promotions, holidays, weather, day-of-week/seasonality,
  product metadata, and cross-series signals. Future-known covariates (promo
  calendar, holidays) are what separate a forecaster from an extrapolator.
- **Cold-start items:** new SKUs with no history lean on the global model +
  attribute-based similarity — MQ-RNN was designed explicitly to handle
  cold-starts and planned event spikes.

<ThinkFirst question="The M5 competition (42,840 Walmart series) was won overwhelmingly by LightGBM-based models, yet Amazon runs deep models. When does deep actually win — and what baseline must survive in your system regardless?">
GBDT on lag/calendar features wins when series are few enough to feature-
engineer and covariates are tabular — it's cheap, robust, and was the M5
winners' workhorse. Deep global models earn their keep at **extreme scale and
heterogeneity**: millions of series, cold-starts, distributional targets, and
raw covariates you'd rather learn than hand-craft (DeepAR reported ~15%
accuracy gains over prior methods across benchmarks). Regardless: keep
seasonal-naive and ETS baselines running forever — in forecasting, fancy
models embarrassingly often fail to beat them, and you need to know when.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective)*

- **Baseline:** classical (ARIMA/ETS) per series and seasonal-naive — the
  benchmark every fancy model must beat (often it doesn't, by much).
- **Production:** a **global** model. The two canonical deep designs are both
  Amazon research: **DeepAR**, an autoregressive RNN trained on many related
  series that outputs a full predictive distribution
  ([Salinas et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)), and **MQ-RNN/MQ-CNN**,
  a seq2seq that emits **all horizons × all quantiles directly** in one shot,
  trained with quantile (pinball) loss
  ([Wen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.11053)). GBDT on lag/calendar
  features — the M5-winning recipe
  ([Makridakis et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169207021001874)) —
  is the strong tabular alternative.
- **Reconciliation:** MinT / top-down / bottom-up to make hierarchical forecasts
  coherent after the base models run.

Classical models like ARIMA remain the baseline and a component; the win from
deep global models is sharing strength across millions of series and covariates.

<ThinkFirst question="Forecasts for 400 million items — do you need a real-time serving stack like the fraud or ETA systems? What is the binding constraint instead?">
No — demand forecasts feed *planning* decisions (orders, staffing) made on
daily/weekly cadences, so this is a **batch** system: the binding constraints
are throughput (scoring millions of series nightly), **coherence** across the
hierarchy, and backfill correctness when covariates change. Spending your
complexity budget on a sub-100ms serving path here is solving the wrong
problem — a classic interview differentiator between this case and ETA.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Serving & scaling

Mostly **batch**: retrain and generate forecasts on a schedule (nightly/weekly),
write quantiles to a store the inventory optimizer reads. Real-time isn't the
constraint — *scale* (millions of series) and *coherence* are. Backfill and
recompute when big covariates (a promo calendar) change. Direct multi-horizon
architectures (MQ-RNN-style) help operationally too: one forward pass per
series emits every horizon and quantile, instead of error-accumulating
recursive rollouts.

<ThinkFirst question="How would you construct the train/test split — and why does the split you'd use for tabular ML overstate accuracy here?">
A random split leaks the future: the model trains on observations *after* the
ones it's tested on, so seasonality and trends are effectively given away.
Time series demands **walk-forward** (rolling-origin) backtesting — train up to
time T, forecast beyond it, roll forward, repeat, and probabilistic outputs
need pinball loss and coverage checks, not just MAPE. The M5 competition's
companion **uncertainty track** scored exactly this: quantile forecasts under
pinball loss.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

- **Point + probabilistic:** WMAPE / MASE for point accuracy; **pinball
  (quantile) loss** and coverage for the distribution.
- **Backtesting:** rolling-origin / **walk-forward** validation — never a random
  split; time leakage is the classic bug.
- **Business:** service level achieved vs. inventory cost — the forecast is a
  means, not the end.

<WikiLink slug="walk-forward-validation" title="Walk-forward validation for time series" />

<ThinkFirst question="Backtested P90 coverage was perfect at launch. Three months in, stockouts are up although the dashboards look fine. How might the system be eating its own tail?">
Through the **censoring loop**: the model under-forecasts somewhere → the
optimizer orders less → the item stocks out → recorded sales are censored at
the low inventory → the next training run sees "low demand" confirmed → it
forecasts lower still. Coverage metrics computed on *sales* can't see it,
because the labels themselves are corrupted. The fix is upstream: stockout-
aware demand correction (or masking censored periods) before data re-enters
training — the feedback stage closing back into data.
</ThinkFirst>

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

- **Drift & regime change:** demand shifts with trends, competitors, and shocks;
  monitor error by segment (new items, promoted items, high-velocity SKUs) and
  retrain on schedule.
- **Censored-demand correction:** feed stockout-aware demand back so the model
  doesn't learn suppressed sales as true demand — the loop above is the
  classic silent failure of production forecasters.
- **Human overrides:** planners will override forecasts for events the model
  can't know; log overrides and score them against actuals, or the model gets
  blamed for human edits (and vice versa).

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Global model across series | One model per series | Scales to millions; shares strength; handles new items |
| Probabilistic (quantiles) | Point forecast | Inventory decisions need service-level quantiles |
| Direct multi-horizon (MQ-style) | Recursive one-step rollout | No error accumulation; one pass emits all horizons/quantiles |
| GBDT global (M5 recipe) | Deep global (DeepAR-style) | GBDT wins at moderate scale; deep wins at extreme scale/heterogeneity |
| Reconciliation | Independent level forecasts | Otherwise totals don't add up: decisions conflict |
| Walk-forward backtest | Random train/test split | Random splits leak the future and overstate accuracy |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Store forecasts don't sum to the regional forecast. Problem?">
Yes — incoherence breaks downstream planning (regional buyers and store managers
act on conflicting numbers). Apply reconciliation (MinT optimally combines the
levels) so forecasts are coherent across the SKU→store→region→total hierarchy —
coherence was a scored requirement of the M5 setup, where the 30,490
bottom-level series had to aggregate consistently upward.
</Details>

<Details summary="A big promotion is coming — how does the model know?">
It's a covariate: past promotions with their lifts are in the training data, and
the upcoming promo calendar is a future-known input the model conditions on.
Without promo features the model treats the spike as noise and under-forecasts —
MQ-RNN's design calls out exactly this "planned event spike" case.
</Details>

<Details summary="You stocked out last week — won't the model learn low demand?">
Only if you feed it raw sales. Stockouts *censor* true demand (you sold what you
had, not what customers wanted). Use stockout-aware demand estimates or mask
censored periods, or the model learns a downward-biased demand and under-orders —
and the bias compounds through the ordering loop.
</Details>

<Details summary="Would you use a transformer for this?">
Maybe — Amazon's current unified model is transformer-based, and attention
handles long seasonal context and cross-series structure well. But the honest
ordering is: seasonal-naive → ETS/ARIMA → global GBDT → deep global model,
promoting only when backtests justify the added cost. M5's lesson is humbling:
the frontier at 40k series was boosted trees, not attention.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Demand forecasting is **hierarchical, multi-horizon, probabilistic time-series
  at scale** — a **global** model (DeepAR/MQ-RNN/global GBDT) beats per-series
  models on millions of series; Amazon forecasts 400M+ items daily this way.
- Forecast a **distribution** and **reconcile** the hierarchy; the downstream
  decision is about service level and risk, not a point number.
- **Walk-forward backtesting** and **censored-demand** handling are the
  correctness essentials — the stockout-censoring feedback loop is the
  system's signature silent failure.
- Keep naive and classical baselines alive forever; M5 says the fancy model
  must earn its place.
