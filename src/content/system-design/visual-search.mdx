# Design a Visual Search System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Pinterest Lens / Amazon"
  scale="billions of images · <300ms · embedding ANN"
  difficulty="intermediate"
/>

Someone sees a couch they love in a friend's living room, points their
phone camera at it, and taps "search." Three hundred milliseconds later
they're looking at shoppable listings for that couch — or ones just like
it — pulled from a catalog of a billion product images. No text was
typed; there's nothing to type. The photo *is* the query.

That one fact reorganizes the entire problem. Text search can lean on
words: indexes over terms, decades of lexical machinery. With an image
query, there is no vocabulary to match on — the only way to compare a
messy real-world photo against a catalog is to convert both into
something comparable. So the system reduces to **representation
learning plus nearest-neighbor retrieval**: train an encoder that maps
every image to a vector such that "visually similar" becomes "nearby in
vector space," then make searching that space fast at billion scale.
Every design decision in this case serves one of those two halves — and
as we'll see, the first half (the embedding) is where the quality lives,
while the second (the index) is where the engineering lives.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
The concepts this case builds on:

- **Embeddings / representation learning** — training a network to map
  inputs to vectors whose geometry is meaningful, so distance in the
  vector space measures similarity of the inputs.
- **Metric learning & contrastive/triplet losses** — training
  objectives that explicitly shape that geometry: pull "positive" pairs
  (same/similar items) together, push "negative" pairs apart.
- **Hard negatives** — negatives that are *difficult* — visually close
  but wrong — as opposed to random negatives that are trivially
  distinguishable. A recurring theme below.
- **Self-supervised pretraining (SimCLR / DINO)** — generating training
  pairs without labels by augmenting an image (crop, recolor) and
  treating the two views as a positive pair.
- **ANN (approximate nearest neighbor) search** — finding near vectors
  without comparing against all of them; **HNSW** (graph-based) and
  **IVF** (inverted lists) are the standard index families — see
  [HNSW](/wiki/hnsw) and [vector databases](/wiki/vector-databases).
- **Product quantization (PQ)** — compressing vectors ~30–60× by
  encoding them as codebook indices, trading a little recall for
  fitting billions of vectors in memory.
- **Object detection** — localizing objects in an image with bounding
  boxes; used here to crop the query object out of a cluttered photo.
- **CLIP-style joint image–text spaces** — encoders trained so images
  and text land in one shared embedding space, making cross-modal
  queries ("this dress, but red") possible.
- **Recall@k / mAP** — retrieval metrics: did the right items appear in
  the top k, and how well-ordered were they.
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

The goal: given a query photo — usually a messy real-world one, with
bad lighting, odd angles, and other objects in frame — return visually
similar catalog items, ideally shoppable ones.

The clarifying question that shapes everything downstream: **similar in
what sense?** "Same category" (any couch) is too coarse to be useful.
"Exact same product" (this SKU) is one valid target, with its own label
source. Usually the product wants something in between: *fine-grained
style similarity* — the same kind of mid-century leather couch, not
just any couch. Pinning this down first matters because the definition
of similarity **is** the label definition, and the label definition
drives the loss, the mining strategy, and the evaluation. Skip this
step and you'll train a beautiful embedding for the wrong notion of
similar.

One practical wrinkle to surface early: real photos contain multiple
objects, so the pipeline is usually **detect-then-search** — find the
objects, let the user pick (or take the most salient), and run the
search on the crop. Scope: the embedding, retrieval, and re-ranking
stack; the object detector is reused from an adjacent system.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
The core question is "what does *similar* mean, and how do I get labels
for it?" Exact-match and visually-similar are different objectives with
different mining strategies — pin this down before choosing a loss, and
say so out loud.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | < 300 ms | Precomputed embeddings + ANN, never pairwise comparison |
| Catalog | Billions of images | HNSW / IVF-PQ index, quantized embeddings |
| Robustness | Real-world photos (lighting, angle, clutter) | Heavy augmentation; detect-then-crop |
| Freshness | New products searchable in minutes | Incremental embedding + index updates |

<ThinkFirst question="There's no text query — the photo is the query. What does the whole system reduce to, and what single artifact caps its quality?">
It reduces to **learning a distance**: map every image to a vector such
that "similar" means "nearby," and nearest-neighbor search does the
rest. Which means the encoder is the whole game — retrieval quality is
*bounded* by embedding quality. If two similar couches land far apart
in the space, no index, no re-ranker, and no amount of serving
engineering can ever bring them back together. Everything downstream
can only lose information the embedding captured; nothing downstream
can recover information it didn't.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

Formally: **metric/representation learning** to train the encoder, then
**approximate nearest-neighbor retrieval** over the embedded catalog,
then a re-ranking pass on the shortlist. The ThinkFirst's conclusion is
the framing's center of gravity: the model *is* the embedding, and the
rest is (important) plumbing.

What was breaking before? The tag-based alternative — classify images
into categories, match on tags — can't express fine-grained similarity:
"chair" is a tag; "this specific mid-century silhouette in cognac
leather" is not, and never will be, because taxonomies can't keep up
with visual nuance. And the brute-force alternative — compare the query
against catalog images pairwise at query time — is computationally dead
on arrival at a billion items. Embedding + ANN is what makes
fine-grained visual similarity *both* expressible and searchable.

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

Where do labeled pairs for metric learning come from? Three sources,
conveniently ordered from cheap to precious. **Same-product photos** —
a catalog item photographed from different angles, in different
lighting — are free positives with perfect labels, and they teach
invariance to viewpoint. **User co-engagement** — items clicked or
saved together in visual-search sessions — captures the human sense of
"similar style" that no rule expresses, at the cost of noise.
**Human similarity judgments** are the gold standard for evaluation
sets and for the ambiguous middle, at per-label cost.

When labels run short, **self-supervision** fills in: augmenting an
image (crop, color jitter, rotation) and treating the two views as a
positive pair teaches robustness to exactly the photographic nuisances
— lighting, angle, framing — that separate messy query photos from
clean catalog shots.

The third data decision is going **multimodal**: training image and
text encoders into a shared space (CLIP-style) so a photo query and a
text refinement can be combined. This isn't free — it needs paired
image–text data — but it unlocks the product's best follow-on feature,
as the visualization below shows.

<ThinkFirst question="For contrastive training, which examples matter more — the positives or the negatives?">
The negatives — specifically the **hard** ones. Random negatives (a
shoe vs. a sofa) are pushed apart within the first epoch and teach
nothing afterward; the loss goes flat while the embedding stays coarse.
The model's final quality comes almost entirely from learning to
separate items that are *visually close but wrong* — the same chair
shape in a different style, the near-identical sneaker from another
brand. Hard-negative mining — actively finding these confusable pairs
and feeding them to the loss — is where the metric actually gets
learned.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective)*

The **encoder** is a CNN or ViT backbone, and the recipe is: start from
pretrained weights (supervised or self-supervised — SimCLR/DINO-style),
then fine-tune with a **contrastive or triplet loss** on the pair data
from section 4. The fine-tuning matters more than the backbone choice:
a generic pretrained encoder knows what a couch is, but only in-domain
metric learning teaches it which couches are *the same style* — the
fine-grained distinction the product lives on.

The **objective** is the geometric one the framing promised: pull
positive pairs together, push negatives apart — with the ThinkFirst's
caveat that the *selection* of negatives, more than the loss formula,
determines final quality.

The **re-ranker** is the accuracy escape valve: once ANN returns a
top-k shortlist, a heavier model — even cross-attention over
query+candidate pairs, unaffordable at catalog scale but cheap over 100
items — refines the order, and business signals (price, availability,
seller quality) enter here rather than polluting the similarity space.

<CLIPSpaceViz />

The shared image–text space visualized above is what lets a photo query
and catalog images meet in one geometry — and it's what makes "find
this, but in blue" a vector operation rather than a new product.

<ThinkFirst question="A billion 512-dimensional float32 embeddings — roughly how much memory is that, and what does the number force?">
512 dims × 4 bytes × 10⁹ vectors ≈ **2 TB** — far too much for the RAM
of a serving node, before any index structure is added on top. The
arithmetic forces compression: billion-scale ANN pairs a graph or
inverted-list index with **product quantization**, shrinking each
vector ~30–60× at a small recall cost — and then claws the accuracy
back by re-ranking the shortlist with exact, uncompressed distances.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Serving & scaling

The offline/online split is clean. **Offline**: embed the entire
catalog, quantize with PQ, build the ANN index — recomputed on a
schedule and updated incrementally as inventory changes, so a product
added this morning is searchable by this afternoon. **Online**, the
query path is: detect the object → crop → embed the query once → ANN
search over the quantized index → exact re-rank of the shortlist →
availability and business filters — all inside 300ms, with the encoder
forward pass and the ANN lookup as the two dominant costs.

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

Offline, retrieval metrics — Recall@k, precision@k, mAP — are computed
against similarity ground truth, which is exactly why the "what does
similar mean" decision from section 1 had to come first: the ground
truth *is* that definition, operationalized. Alongside the retrieval
numbers, embedding-quality probes (does the space cluster products
sensibly? do known style groups stay together?) catch representation
regressions before they reach the index.

Online, the metrics are commercial: click-through and purchase rate on
visual-search results, and zero/low-result rate as the recall
early-warning. The third leg is **human side-by-side judgments** —
raters comparing result sets — because both offline labels and clicks
have blind spots: the offline label may not match what users actually
want (the follow-ups dig into this), and clicks can't evaluate the
results users scrolled past.

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

Both sides of the system drift. The *catalog* side shifts with seasonal
inventory and new product styles; the *query* side shifts with camera
hardware and photo trends. Both erode a frozen embedding, so
re-embedding and periodic encoder retraining are scheduled operations,
not emergencies.

The feedback loop is the pleasant kind: engagement on returned results
— clicks, saves, purchases — becomes fresh co-engagement positive
pairs, so the embedding's training data grows richer in exactly the
regions users care about. The loop closes into **data**, and unlike the
adversarial cases in this course, nothing here is trying to poison it.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Metric-learned embedding + ANN | Classification + tag match | Tags are too coarse for fine-grained visual similarity |
| Detect-then-crop-then-search | Search the whole photo | Cluttered photos need the object isolated first |
| Quantized ANN (PQ) | Exact NN | Exact search is infeasible at billions of vectors — the 2 TB arithmetic |
| Contrastive fine-tune | Frozen pretrained encoder | Generic encoders know categories; only in-domain metric learning knows styles |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="The query photo has a couch, a rug, and a lamp. What do you return?">
Detect first, search second. Run the object detector, let the user tap
the object they meant (defaulting to the most salient), and embed only
that crop. Embedding the whole cluttered scene averages three objects
into one vector that's near none of them — the retrieval returns vague
"living room" results instead of that couch. Detect-then-search is why
the detector sits at the front of the pipeline.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you support 'find this dress but in red'?">
This is the payoff of the shared vision-language space: embed the
photo, then compose it with a text delta ("red") in the same space —
or condition the query on the attribute — and search as usual. In a
pure image space this feature would require a separate attribute
system; in a CLIP-style joint space it's arithmetic on vectors you
already have.
</Details>

<Details summary="Recall@k is high offline but users don't click. Why?">
Three places the offline win can be a lie, in checking order. The
**similarity label** may not match intent — visually-similar isn't the
same as want-to-buy, and an embedding perfectly optimized for the
offline definition can still retrieve unbuyable lookalikes. The
**detection/crop** may be wrong — perfect retrieval on the wrong
object. Or **post-retrieval filters** (availability, price) may be
gutting the result set after the metric was computed. The structural
fixes: validate the similarity label against real engagement, and let
the re-ranker carry the business signals.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Visual search is **representation learning + ANN retrieval**, and the
  embedding caps everything: no downstream stage can recover similarity
  the encoder failed to encode.
- **Define "similar" first** — it's the label definition, the loss
  target, and the evaluation ground truth all at once — then mine
  **hard negatives**, because that's where the metric is actually
  learned.
- Messy real photos are handled by **detect-then-crop** and
  augmentation-heavy training; billion-scale serving is handled by
  **quantized ANN with exact re-ranking** — the 2 TB arithmetic leaves
  no alternative.
- A **shared image–text space** turns cross-modal refinement ("this but
  in blue") into vector arithmetic — the highest-leverage extension the
  architecture offers.
