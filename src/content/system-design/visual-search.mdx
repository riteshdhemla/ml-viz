# Design a Visual Search System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="Pinterest Lens / Amazon"
  scale="billions of images · <300ms · embedding ANN"
  difficulty="intermediate"
/>

Visual search ("point your camera at a couch, find ones to buy") is a
**representation-learning + nearest-neighbor retrieval** problem. There's no text
query — the *image is the query* — so the whole system hinges on learning an
embedding where visually/semantically similar items are close, then searching it
fast. Pinterest, eBay, and Alibaba have all published their production systems —
Pinterest's 2015 paper is famous for showing a **small team with open-source
tools and commodity cloud infra** could build one, and that visual search
measurably lifted engagement in live A/Bs
([Jing et al., KDD 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07647)). Walk it through
the **ML project loop**.

## 1. Clarify the problem

- **Goal:** given a query image (often a messy real-world photo), return visually
  similar catalog items, ideally shoppable.
- **Similarity of what?** Usually *fine-grained* product similarity (same style of
  shoe), not just category — the label definition drives everything.
- **Detect-then-search:** a photo may contain several objects; often detect the
  object of interest first, then search on the crop.
- **Scope:** embedding + retrieval + re-rank. Adjacent: the detection model,
  which we reuse.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
The core question is "what does *similar* mean, and how do I get labels for it?"
— exact-match (same product) and visually-similar are different objectives with
different mining strategies. Pin this before choosing a loss.
</Callout>

<ThinkFirst question="One surface needs exact-product matches ('buy this lamp'), another needs style-similar browsing, a third needs shop-the-look. Three embeddings or one — and what changes at the data level?">
Pinterest tried both and consolidated: separate specialized embeddings became
an operational tax (three models to train, serve, and keep consistent), so they
moved to **one unified embedding trained multi-task** across all three
objectives, which matched or beat the specialists because the tasks share
signal. The data-level consequence: each task contributes its own labeled pairs
(engagement for browse, human-verified exact matches for shop), and the
sampling mix across tasks becomes a first-class tuning knob.
</ThinkFirst>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | < 300 ms | Precomputed embeddings + ANN, not pairwise compare |
| Catalog | Billions of images | HNSW / IVF-PQ index, quantized embeddings |
| Robustness | Real-world photos (lighting, angle, clutter) | Heavy augmentation; detect-then-crop |
| Freshness | New products searchable in minutes | Incremental embedding + index updates |

<ThinkFirst question="There's no text query — the photo is the query. What does the whole system reduce to, and what single artifact caps its quality?">
It reduces to **learning a distance**: map every image to a vector such that
"similar" means "nearby," then nearest-neighbor search does the rest. That
makes the encoder the whole game — retrieval quality is bounded by embedding
quality, and no amount of index engineering can recover similarity the
embedding failed to encode.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

**Metric / representation learning**: train an encoder so that similar images map
to nearby vectors, then do **approximate nearest neighbor** retrieval + a
re-ranking pass. Retrieval quality is bounded by embedding quality — the model
*is* the embedding.

*What was breaking before?* Classification tags are too coarse (they can't
express "this specific mid-century chair"); pairwise image comparison at query
time is computationally impossible at billions of items.

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

- **Labels / pairs:** positive pairs from the same product (different photos,
  angles), user co-engagement (clicked-together items), and human similarity
  judgments. Negatives from **hard-negative mining** (visually close but wrong).
- **Self-supervision:** augmentations (crop, color, rotate) generate positive
  pairs cheaply — powerful when labels are scarce.
- **Multimodal:** vision-language encoders let text and image share a space
  (search by photo, refine by words).

<ThinkFirst question="For contrastive training, which examples matter more — the positives or the negatives?">
The negatives — specifically the **hard** ones. Random negatives (a shoe vs. a
sofa) are separated after one epoch and teach nothing; the model's final
quality comes from learning to push apart items that are visually close but
wrong (the same chair in a different style). Hard-negative mining is where the
metric actually gets learned.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective)*

- **Encoder:** a CNN/ViT backbone fine-tuned with a **contrastive / triplet**
  loss (metric learning), or a self-supervised (SimCLR/DINO-style) then fine-tuned
  encoder.
- **Objective:** pull positives together, push hard negatives apart — the choice
  of *which* negatives dominates final quality. Train **multi-task** across
  surfaces: Pinterest's unified embedding jointly optimizes browse, search, and
  shop objectives in one network, and **binarizes** the output embedding for
  cheap storage and retrieval without measurable precision loss
  ([Zhai et al., KDD 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.01707)).
- **Multimodal option:** contrastive image–text pretraining at scale (CLIP
  trained on 400M web pairs) yields a joint space where photos and words are
  directly comparable
  ([Radford et al., ICML 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)) — the
  foundation for "this, but in blue."
- **Re-ranker:** a heavier model (or cross-attention over query+candidate) refines
  the ANN top-k on price, availability, and finer similarity.

<CLIPSpaceViz />

A shared image–text embedding space (CLIP-style) is what lets a photo query and a
catalog image land near each other, and enables "find this, but in blue."

<ThinkFirst question="A billion 512-dimensional float32 embeddings — roughly how much memory is that, and what does the number force?">
512 × 4 bytes × 10⁹ ≈ **2 TB** — too much to hold in RAM on a serving node,
before you've even built an index. That's why billion-scale ANN pairs a graph
or inverted-list index with **product quantization**, compressing each vector
~30–60× at a small recall cost, with an exact re-rank on the shortlist.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Serving & scaling

Offline: embed the whole catalog, quantize (PQ), build an ANN index — the
IVF+PQ compressed-domain search popularized by Faiss is the standard recipe at
billions of vectors ([Johnson et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08734)).
Online: detect the object → crop → embed the query once → ANN search → re-rank
→ availability/business filters, all under 300ms. The detect-then-search step
is production practice, not a nicety: Alibaba's Pailitao runs **category
prediction plus object localization** before embedding, precisely because
user photos are cluttered scenes, not catalog shots
([Zhang et al., KDD 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3219819.3219820)).
Update the index incrementally as inventory changes.

<WikiLink slug="hnsw" title="HNSW — the graph index for billion-scale ANN" />

<ThinkFirst question="You need an offline eval set for 'similar'. Clicks are abundant — why can't you just use click-through on results as ground truth, and what do you use instead?">
Clicks conflate similarity with salience, position, and price — users click the
eye-catching or cheap result, not the most similar one, and you only observe
clicks on what the *current* embedding retrieved (selection bias). The honest
setup layers three sources: **human side-by-side judgments** on a gold set for
fine-grained similarity, **known exact-match pairs** (same product, different
photos) for objective recall, and engagement as a *directional* online signal —
never the offline ground truth.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

- **Offline:** Recall@k / precision@k against similarity ground truth; mAP for
  retrieval. Embedding quality probes (does the space cluster products sensibly?).
- **Online:** click-through and purchase rate on visual-search results, plus
  zero/low-result rate.
- **Human eval:** side-by-side similarity judgments the implicit metrics miss.

<ThinkFirst question="eBay's catalog isn't a catalog — it's live listings that end, sell out, and appear by the million daily. What breaks in your beautifully precomputed index, and what's the redesign?">
Staleness in both directions: results pointing at ended listings (visible
breakage users punish immediately) and new inventory that's invisible until
the next index build. A nightly rebuild can't keep up, so the eBay-style
answer is a **streaming ingest path** — embed new listings on arrival, append
to the index, tombstone dead ones and filter them at query time — with
periodic full rebuilds to compact and retrain quantizers. Index freshness
becomes a monitored SLA, just like latency.
</ThinkFirst>

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

- **Distribution shift:** new product styles, seasonal inventory, and camera
  trends shift the query and catalog distributions; re-embed and retrain.
- **Feedback loop:** engagement on returned items becomes new positive pairs,
  improving the embedding over time.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Metric-learned embedding + ANN | Classification + tag match | Tags are too coarse for fine-grained visual similarity |
| Detect-then-crop-then-search | Search the whole photo | Cluttered photos need the object isolated first |
| Quantized ANN (PQ) | Exact NN | Exact search is infeasible at billions of vectors |
| Contrastive fine-tune | Frozen pretrained encoder | Domain fine-tuning sharply improves in-catalog similarity |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="The query photo has a couch, a rug, and a lamp. What do you return?">
Detect objects first, let the user pick (or default to the most salient), then
search on that crop. Embedding the whole cluttered scene averages the objects
together and retrieves nothing well — detect-then-search is the fix.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you support 'find this dress but in red'?">
A shared vision-language embedding space: embed the image, then compose with a
text delta ("red") in the same space, or condition the query on an attribute.
This is exactly what CLIP-style joint training buys you.
</Details>

<Details summary="Recall@k is high offline but users don't click. Why?">
The offline similarity label may not match user intent (visually similar ≠ what
they want to buy), the crop/detection is wrong, or availability/price filters gut
the results post-retrieval. Add a re-ranker on business signals and validate the
similarity label against real engagement.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Visual search is **representation learning + ANN retrieval** — the embedding
  quality caps everything.
- Define **similarity** and mine **hard negatives** carefully; contrastive/self-
  supervised training and **detect-then-crop** handle messy real-world photos.
- Serve with **quantized ANN indexes**; a shared **image–text space** unlocks
  multimodal "this but in blue" queries.
- One **unified multi-task embedding** beats a zoo of specialized ones — in
  quality and, just as importantly, in operational cost.
