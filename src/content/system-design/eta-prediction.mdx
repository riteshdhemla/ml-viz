# Design an ETA / Delivery-Time Prediction System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="DoorDash / Uber"
  scale="millions of ETAs/min · <100ms · calibrated uncertainty"
  difficulty="intermediate"
/>

ETA prediction is the classic **real-time regression** interview: predict a
continuous arrival/delivery time, fast, per request, with **calibrated
uncertainty** because the number is shown to users and used to make dispatch
decisions. The reference systems are **Uber's DeepETA** (rides and Eats,
globally) and **DoorDash's deep-learning ETA stack** (2B+ orders a year). Walk
it through the **ML project loop** — the twist versus a classifier is that the
*distribution*, not just the point estimate, matters.

## 1. Clarify the problem

- **Goal:** predict end-to-end delivery time (or ride ETA) at request time, and
  keep updating it as the trip progresses.
- **Why uncertainty:** a promised ETA is a commitment. Being *late* is worse than
  being *early*, so the loss is asymmetric and users need a reliable range, not a
  point guess.
- **Composed of stages:** e.g. food prep + courier assignment + drive time — each
  a sub-prediction that composes.
- **Scope:** the ETA model + feedback from actuals. Dispatch/assignment is
  adjacent (it *consumes* ETAs — Uber's ETAs also feed pricing and matching, so
  errors compound downstream).

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
Decompose the ETA into its physical stages (prep, wait, transit) and predict
each — a single black-box regressor on total time is weaker and harder to debug
than a composed, interpretable estimate.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | A few ms of model time inside a sub-100 ms request | Cheap model; precomputed spatial features |
| Volume | Millions of ETAs/min | Feature store + lightweight serving |
| Accuracy target | Low MAE *and* calibrated intervals | Quantile / probabilistic outputs |
| Freshness | React to live traffic, store load | Streaming features (courier supply, kitchen backlog) |

<ThinkFirst question="Suppose you train a regressor to minimize MAE and display its prediction. Roughly half your deliveries will now be “late.” Why?">
Minimizing MAE makes the model predict the **median** — by definition, actuals
land above it half the time. Since being late costs far more than being early,
the displayed ETA should be a *higher quantile* (P70–P90), which means the
model must predict a distribution, not a point. The asymmetry of the loss is
the whole design twist — Uber bakes it directly into training with an
**asymmetric Huber loss**, tunable in both its outlier-robustness and its
asymmetry.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

**Regression with uncertainty** on spatial-temporal data, and in production,
usually **residual** regression. Uber's DeepETA doesn't predict the trip time
from scratch: a routing engine first computes a physics-style ETA as the sum of
segment traversal times along the best path, and the ML model predicts the
**residual** between that estimate and reality
([Uber Engineering](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/deepeta-how-uber-predicts-arrival-times/)).
The model corrects for what the map can't see — real driver behavior, pickup
and drop-off overhead, marketplace state.

*What was breaking before?* A static "distance ÷ average speed" heuristic ignores
traffic, kitchen backlog, and courier supply; a point-only model can't express
the confidence the business needs to make promises; and a from-scratch ML
predictor throws away the routing engine's strong physical prior.

<ThinkFirst question="“Route time from the map” gets you a strong baseline. What real-world signals dominate the remaining error — and what infrastructure do they force?">
The live state of the system: current traffic, how backed up the kitchen is,
how many couriers are free right now. Those are **streaming** signals with
sub-minute freshness requirements, which forces a real-time feature store —
and the same pipeline must serve training and inference, or you've built
train-serve skew into your best features. This is why DoorDash pairs its ETA
models with time-series and embedding features computed on a shared feature
platform.
</ThinkFirst>

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

- **Labels:** observed actual times — abundant and low-latency (you learn the
  truth within an hour), the nice inverse of fraud's delayed labels.
- **Features:** origin/destination geo, historical segment speeds, live traffic,
  time-of-day/day-of-week, weather, **real-time supply/demand** (available
  couriers, kitchen backlog), and merchant-specific prep-time history.
- **Encoding matters more than you'd think:** DeepETA **buckets every
  continuous feature by quantiles and embeds the buckets** — discretizing then
  embedding beat feeding raw floats, and encodes origin/destination lat-longs
  as multi-resolution geohash grids mapped to embeddings via feature hashing.
- **Feature store:** the same streaming pipeline computes courier-supply and
  kitchen-load features for train and serve to avoid skew.

<WikiLink slug="hierarchical-forecasting" title="Hierarchical forecasting for composed estimates" />

<ThinkFirst question="You want feature interactions a GBDT gives you for free, but Uber replaced XGBoost with a transformer that must answer in a few milliseconds. A vanilla transformer is quadratic in the number of features. How do you make it fit the budget?">
Attack the quadratic term: DeepETA uses a **linear transformer** (kernelized
attention) so self-attention over the feature set no longer materializes the
full attention matrix, plus aggressive embedding lookups instead of deep
per-feature towers. Served this way, the network answers with a **median
latency of ~3.25 ms (p95 ≈ 4 ms)** — transformer interactions at GBDT-like
serving cost. The general lesson: at high QPS, architecture choices are latency
choices.
</ThinkFirst>

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective)*

- **Baseline:** GBDT (XGBoost/LightGBM) on the engineered features with a
  quantile loss — for years the production default at both Uber and DoorDash
  for tabular spatial-temporal regression.
- **Production frontier:** both companies outgrew trees. Uber's **DeepETA** — a
  linear-transformer residual model — beat the incumbent XGBoost's MAE while
  serving globally ([DeeprETA paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02127));
  DoorDash moved from tree-based models to a deep network with **parallel
  mixture-of-experts encoders** and multi-task heads to handle its diverse
  delivery scenarios in one model
  ([DoorDash Engineering](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/deep-learning-for-smarter-eta-predictions/)).
- **Segment bias correction:** one global model, many segments — DeepETA ends
  in a **bias-adjustment decoder** with per-segment adjustment (rides vs.
  delivery, long vs. short haul, city) rather than separate models per segment.
- **Uncertainty:** quantile regression (predict P10/P50/P90), an asymmetric
  loss, or a full probabilistic output — DoorDash's latest generation ships
  **probabilistic ETAs** rather than points
  ([DoorDash Engineering](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/improving-etas-with-multi-task-models-deep-learning-and-probabilistic-forecasts/)).

<ThinkFirst question="The ETA you promised at checkout is 35 minutes. Ten minutes in, the kitchen backs up and the courier is rerouted. What must the serving design support that a train-once-predict-once view misses?">
**Re-prediction as state changes.** The ETA is not one prediction but a
sequence: order placed → courier assigned → picked up → en route, each with
more observed reality and less prior. The serving path must accept live trip
state, and the model family must handle the shifting feature availability
(pre-assignment you have marketplace priors; en-route you have GPS). Uncertainty
should shrink monotonically as stages resolve — if your intervals don't
tighten, your stage decomposition is broken.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Serving & scaling

Fetch precomputed + streaming features, run the composed models (prep + transit)
or the residual model over the routing engine's base ETA, sum with uncertainty
propagation, and return an ETA + range within the request budget (the DeepETA
network itself answers in ~3–4 ms). Re-predict at each trip milestone so the
number tightens over time.

<ThinkFirst question="You show users a range, not just a number. How do you check the range is honest?">
Coverage: the P90 estimate should exceed the actual arrival time in 90% of
trips — no more, no less. Run a reliability check on each predicted quantile,
sliced by region, merchant, and trip type, because intervals that are globally
calibrated can still be badly wrong for specific segments — exactly the
segments a bias-adjustment decoder exists to fix.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

- **Point accuracy:** MAE / MAPE, and **asymmetric** metrics (lateness rate,
  minutes-late) that reflect the real cost.
- **Calibration of intervals:** does the P90 actually cover 90% of actuals? A
  reliability check on the quantiles is essential.
- **Online:** A/B on user-facing outcomes (order completion, satisfaction,
  support contacts about late orders), not just MAE — DoorDash frames ETA
  quality explicitly as a consumer-trust metric, and Uber gates new ETA models
  on marketplace metrics because ETAs feed pricing and matching.

<ThinkFirst question="MAE in one metro creeps up 5% over two days. In fraud you'd wait weeks for labels to confirm anything. What can you do here, and how fast?">
Actuals arrive within the hour, so you can localize the regression almost in
real time: slice error by stage (prep vs. transit) to find whether it's a
merchant-load shift or a traffic-model failure, check feature freshness for the
streaming inputs, and retrain or recalibrate on days of data, not weeks.
Fast, abundant labels are this system's structural gift — a candidate who
designs the same slow feedback loop as fraud is leaving accuracy on the table.
</ThinkFirst>

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

- **Fast loop:** actuals arrive quickly, so drift (new traffic patterns, a
  merchant's kitchen slowing down) is caught and retrained on rapidly —
  segment-level dashboards on lateness rate and interval coverage, not just
  global MAE.
- **Regime shifts:** weather events, holidays, surges — monitor segment-level
  error and keep event features fresh; re-fit calibration heads more often than
  the base model.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Routing-engine ETA + ML residual | End-to-end ML from raw features | Physical prior does the heavy lifting; ML corrects what maps miss |
| Composed stage models | Single total-time regressor | Interpretable, debuggable, each stage improvable |
| Quantile / probabilistic output | Point estimate only | The business needs calibrated ranges to make promises |
| GBDT baseline → deep model | Deep model first | Trees are strong and cheap; earn the upgrade with data volume (Uber, DoorDash both did) |
| Linear transformer | Vanilla transformer | Quadratic attention doesn't fit a few-ms serving budget |
| Streaming supply features | Static averages | Live courier/kitchen load is the biggest error driver |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Users complain deliveries are 'always late.' MAE looks fine. What's wrong?">
MAE is symmetric but the cost isn't. A model minimizing MAE promises the median,
so ~50% of orders are late. Predict and display a higher quantile (e.g. P70–P90)
so promises are conservative, and track lateness rate, not just MAE. Uber's
asymmetric Huber loss is the same idea pushed into training.
</Details>

<Details summary="A kitchen suddenly slows down at lunch. How does the ETA adapt?">
Streaming merchant-load features (live backlog, recent prep times) feed the prep-
time sub-model, so ETAs lengthen within minutes. Static historical averages would
lag the regime change entirely. This is why the feature store must be real-time.
</Details>

<Details summary="Why does Uber predict the residual instead of the ETA itself?">
The routing engine already encodes road network, distances, and speed profiles —
a strong physical prior that would be wasteful to relearn. Predicting the
residual makes the learning problem smaller and better-conditioned, keeps the
system interpretable (map estimate + learned correction), and degrades
gracefully: if the model fails, you still have a sane routing-engine ETA.
</Details>

<Details summary="One global model or per-city models?">
One global model with segment awareness. Per-city models fragment data, multiply
operational surface, and still miss cross-cutting segments (rain, long-haul,
new merchants). DeepETA's answer is a shared network with a bias-adjustment
decoder that learns per-segment corrections — global statistical strength,
local calibration.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- ETA is **real-time regression with calibrated uncertainty** — the *interval*
  matters because the number is a promise.
- Production systems **anchor on a physical prior** (routing engine) and learn
  the residual; **compose** the estimate from stages for debuggability.
- **Asymmetric lateness cost** means a conservative quantile (or asymmetric
  loss), not the mean; **fast actuals** make drift easy to catch and retrain on.
- At scale, **architecture is a latency decision**: bucketed-embedding features
  and linear attention are how a transformer serves in ~3 ms.
