# Design an ETA / Delivery-Time Prediction System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="ML System Design"
  company="DoorDash / Uber"
  scale="millions of ETAs/min · <100ms · calibrated uncertainty"
  difficulty="intermediate"
/>

A customer opens a food-delivery app and sees "35–45 min." That number is
not a decoration — it's a **promise**. The customer decides whether to
order based on it; the dispatch system decides which courier to assign
based on it; and when the food arrives at minute 52, the customer
remembers. Meanwhile the same model is making millions of these promises
per minute, each computed in under 100 milliseconds from the live state of
kitchens, couriers, and traffic.

That's the shape of the ETA problem, and it's the classic **real-time
regression** interview. The surface task — predict a continuous number —
looks like textbook regression. The twist that separates a good answer
from a mediocre one: because the number is a promise with asymmetric
consequences (being late is much worse than being early), the model must
predict a *distribution*, and the business chooses which quantile of it to
promise. A point estimate isn't just insufficient — as we'll see, a model
trained the obvious way makes half your deliveries "late" by construction.
Walk it through the ML project loop with that distribution-first mindset.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
The concepts this case builds on:

- **Quantiles (P50 / P90)** — the P90 of a delivery-time distribution is
  the time that 90% of deliveries beat. "Promise the P80" means being
  early four times out of five.
- **MAE minimizes toward the median** — a regressor trained to minimize
  mean absolute error learns to predict the *median* of the target
  distribution. This innocuous statistical fact is the hinge of the whole
  case: promise the median and half your actuals land above it.
- **Quantile regression / pinball loss** — a loss function that makes a
  model predict a chosen quantile instead of the median; train several
  heads and you get a predictive interval (P10/P50/P90).
- **Coverage** — the honesty check for intervals: a P90 prediction should
  exceed the actual outcome in 90% of cases. More means padded promises;
  less means broken ones.
- **GBDT (LightGBM/XGBoost)** — gradient-boosted trees, the default model
  family for tabular features with fast inference.
- **Feature store & train-serve skew** — infrastructure ensuring the
  features the model trains on are computed *identically* at serving
  time; "streaming" features update within seconds of real-world events.
- **MAE / MAPE** — mean absolute error, and its percentage cousin;
  standard point-accuracy metrics for regression.
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

The system predicts end-to-end delivery time (or ride ETA) at request time
— *before* the customer orders, since the number influences the ordering
decision — and then keeps re-predicting as the trip progresses, so the
promise tightens as uncertainty resolves.

Two structural facts shape the design. First, the target **decomposes into
physical stages**: for food delivery, prep time (kitchen) + courier
assignment and travel-to-store + transit to the customer. Each stage has
different drivers and different uncertainty, which argues for predicting
the stages and composing them rather than regressing on the total. Second,
**the loss is asymmetric**: an early delivery is a pleasant surprise; a
late one is a broken promise that generates support tickets and churn.
That asymmetry is why the *distribution* matters — the business wants to
promise a deliberately conservative quantile, and it can only do that if
the model provides one.

Scope: the ETA model and its feedback loop from actual delivery times.
The dispatch/assignment optimizer is adjacent — it *consumes* ETAs to
decide courier assignments — and stays out of scope.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
Decompose the ETA into its physical stages (prep, wait, transit) and
predict each. A single black-box regressor on total time is weaker and —
worse for production — undebuggable: when ETAs go bad, you can't tell
whether kitchens slowed down or traffic changed.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | < 100 ms per request | Cheap model; spatial features precomputed |
| Volume | Millions of ETAs/min | Feature store + lightweight serving tier |
| Accuracy | Low error *and* calibrated intervals | Quantile / probabilistic outputs |
| Freshness | React to live traffic, kitchen load, courier supply | Streaming features with sub-minute freshness |

<ThinkFirst question="Suppose you train a regressor to minimize MAE and display its prediction. Roughly half your deliveries will now be “late.” Why?">
Because minimizing MAE makes the model predict the **median** — and by
definition, actual times land above the median half the time. Nothing is
wrong with the model; the promise policy is wrong. Since lateness costs
far more than earliness, the *displayed* ETA should be a higher quantile
(P70–P90), which the business can only choose if the model predicts a
distribution rather than a point. This asymmetry is the entire design
twist of the case.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. ML problem framing

Formally: **regression with calibrated uncertainty on spatial-temporal
data**. The model outputs both a point estimate and a predictive interval
— via quantile regression or a distributional head — and the product
surfaces a chosen quantile as the promise. The asymmetric lateness cost
sets *which* quantile: slightly conservative, tuned to the acceptable
lateness rate.

What was breaking before ML? The heuristic baseline — distance ÷ average
speed, plus a fixed prep-time constant — fails on exactly the inputs that
dominate real variance: live traffic, how backed up this particular
kitchen is right now, whether any courier is nearby. And even a good
*point*-prediction model can't express confidence, which the promise
policy needs. The framing must deliver both the live-signal features and
the distributional output.

<ThinkFirst question="“Distance ÷ average speed” gets you a crude baseline. What real-world signals dominate the remaining error — and what infrastructure do they force?">
The **live state of the system**: current traffic on the route, the
kitchen's real-time backlog, how many couriers are free nearby. These
signals change minute to minute, so they must be **streaming** features
with sub-minute freshness — which forces a real-time feature store. And
the same pipeline must compute them for training and for serving:
hand-compute them differently in the training job and you've built
train-serve skew into precisely your most valuable features.
</ThinkFirst>

## 4. Data & features *(spine: data)*

Start with the good news, because it's rare in this course: **labels here
are abundant, clean, and fast**. Every delivery produces its actual
end-to-end time within the hour. Compare that to fraud (labels arrive
weeks late via chargebacks) — ETA's fast ground truth is what makes the
tight retraining loop in section 8 possible.

Features layer from static to live. Static and slow-moving: origin and
destination geography, historical speeds per road segment, merchant
prep-time history, time-of-day and day-of-week patterns, weather. Live
and streaming — the layer the ThinkFirst identified as decisive:
current courier supply in the area, each kitchen's current backlog and
recent prep times, live traffic. The feature store's job is keeping that
live layer fresh at serving time *and* consistent with what training saw.

## 5. Model *(spine: hypothesis space + objective)*

The baseline — and, as usual for tabular features, quite plausibly the
production model — is **gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM) trained with
quantile loss**. Trees handle the mixed feature types natively, score fast
enough to be irrelevant to the latency budget, and the quantile objective
directly produces the P10/P50/P90 heads the promise policy needs.

Refinements attach where structure pays: learned embeddings for road
segments and merchants (letting the model share strength across similar
entities), and deep sequence models where route structure genuinely
matters (e.g. turn-by-turn transit estimation from live GPS traces). Each
stage of the decomposition — prep, wait, transit — gets its own model,
trained on that stage's own actuals.

The through-line: **the shown ETA is a chosen quantile of a predicted
distribution, not the mean of anything.** Every modeling choice serves
that output.

## 6. Serving & scaling

At request time: fetch precomputed spatial features and streaming
supply/load features, run the per-stage models, compose them —
summing stage estimates with uncertainty propagated, since independent
stage errors partially cancel — and return an ETA with its range, all
inside 100ms. The model itself is microseconds; the budget is spent on
feature fetches, which is why everything is precomputed or streamed
rather than calculated inline.

Prediction doesn't stop at order time. As the trip progresses — courier
assigned, food picked up, en route — the system re-predicts at each
milestone. Stages that have completed contribute their *actual* time
instead of an estimate, so the interval tightens monotonically: the
"35–45 min" at order time becomes "8–11 min" once the courier is driving.

<ThinkFirst question="You show users a range, not just a number. How do you check the range is honest?">
**Coverage.** The P90 estimate should exceed the actual arrival in
exactly 90% of trips — more means you're padding promises (and losing
orders to inflated ETAs), less means you're breaking them. Check each
predicted quantile against realized frequencies — and do it *sliced* by
region, merchant type, and time of day, because globally-calibrated
intervals can still be badly dishonest for specific segments.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

Offline metrics come in matched pairs — one symmetric, one honest about
the asymmetry. MAE/MAPE track point accuracy, but the metrics the
business feels are **lateness rate** (what fraction of promises were
broken) and **minutes-late-when-late** (how badly). A model can improve
MAE while worsening lateness rate, if its errors shift toward the late
side — so both must be tracked. For the intervals, the coverage check
from the ThinkFirst is the standing evaluation, per quantile and per
segment.

Online, A/B tests measure what ETAs are *for*: order conversion (do
honest, tighter ETAs increase ordering?), completion and cancellation
rates, and support contacts about late orders. MAE is a proxy; "did
customers stop complaining about lateness" is the outcome.

## 8. Monitoring & feedback *(spine: feedback)*

Because actuals arrive within the hour, this system enjoys the fastest
feedback loop in the ML-systems catalog — and the design should exploit
that. Drift that would take weeks to detect in fraud (where labels lag)
shows up here the same day: a merchant's kitchen slowing down, a road
closure changing segment speeds, a new neighborhood coming online.
Segment-level error monitoring catches these; frequent retraining absorbs
them.

The events that outrun even a fast retrain — weather shocks, holidays,
demand surges — are handled as *features* rather than retraining
problems: as long as event covariates are fresh, the model conditions on
the anomaly instead of being surprised by it.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Composed stage models | Single end-to-end regressor | Interpretable and debuggable; each stage improvable and re-predictable independently |
| Quantile / probabilistic output | Point estimate only | The promise policy needs a distribution to choose its quantile from |
| GBDT baseline | Deep model first | Tabular spatial-temporal features → trees are strong, cheap, fast |
| Streaming supply features | Static averages | Live courier/kitchen state is the biggest error driver; averages lag regime changes |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Users complain deliveries are 'always late.' MAE looks fine. What's wrong?">
The promise policy, not the model. MAE is symmetric, so a model that
minimizes it predicts the median — and half of actuals exceed the median
by construction, so half of orders are "late" even though average error
is small. Display a higher quantile (P70–P90) so promises are
conservative, and put **lateness rate** on the dashboard next to MAE so
the asymmetry is measured, not assumed away.
</Details>

<Details summary="A kitchen suddenly slows down at lunch. How does the ETA adapt?">
Through the streaming features: the kitchen's live backlog and its last
few orders' actual prep times feed the prep-time sub-model, so ETAs for
that merchant lengthen within minutes of the slowdown starting. This is
the concrete case for the real-time feature store — a model on static
historical averages would keep quoting the merchant's usual prep time
through the entire lunch rush.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you keep the ETA accurate as the trip progresses?">
Re-predict at every milestone: order placed → courier assigned → picked
up → en route. Completed stages swap their estimates for actuals, and
the transit sub-model switches from a statistical prior to live
GPS-based estimation once the courier is moving — so uncertainty shrinks
monotonically and the displayed range tightens from "35–45" toward
"8–11."
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- An ETA is a **promise**, and the cost of breaking it is asymmetric —
  so the model predicts a **distribution** and the business promises a
  conservative quantile, never the MAE-trained median.
- **Compose the estimate from physical stages** (prep, wait, transit):
  more accurate, debuggable when things go wrong, and re-predictable
  stage by stage as the trip progresses.
- The dominant error drivers are **live-state signals** — kitchen
  backlog, courier supply, traffic — which force a streaming feature
  store with train/serve consistency.
- **Fast actuals are a gift**: labels arrive within the hour, so build
  the tight loop they enable — segment-level drift monitoring, frequent
  retraining, and coverage checks that keep the promised intervals honest.
