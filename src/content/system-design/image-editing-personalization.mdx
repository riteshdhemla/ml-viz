# Design an Image Editing & Personalization System

<SystemDesignMeta
  track="Generative AI System Design"
  company="Adobe Firefly / DreamBooth"
  scale="mask-conditioned edits · per-user personalization"
  difficulty="advanced"
/>

Text-to-image gets the headlines, but watch what users actually do
next. They don't want *an* image — they want *this* image, changed:
"remove the person in the background," "extend the sky upward,"
and the request that breaks every prompt-only system — "put **my
dog** in a spacesuit." Not a generic golden retriever. This one.

Those requests define the two capabilities this case adds on top of
the plain text-to-image system (which this case assumes as its
foundation). **Editing** means the generation must be *conditioned
on an existing image and a mask* — change only what was asked,
keep everything else pixel-faithful, and blend the boundary
invisibly. **Personalization** means teaching the model a subject it
has never seen from a handful of user photos — and the key fork to
name in an interview is that this is a *fine-tuning* problem, not a
prompting one: no sequence of words can select a specific unseen dog
out of the model's imagination. Both capabilities come with a
distinctive systems twist — per-user training jobs, per-user weights,
and a serving story that must not become "a model copy per user" —
plus the sharpest consent question in the generative catalog.

<Callout type="info" title="What this case assumes you already know">
This case stacks on the text-to-image case and the fine-tuning
course:

- **Latent diffusion** — generating by iterative denoising in a
  compressed latent space; the base model here. See the text-to-image
  case and generative-models/05.
- **Inpainting / outpainting** — regenerating a masked region of an
  image so it fits its surroundings / extending the canvas beyond the
  original borders.
- **LoRA** — low-rank adapters: instead of updating a model's full
  weights, train a tiny low-rank delta (megabytes) on top of the
  frozen base; from the PEFT lesson. The economic hinge of this case.
- **DreamBooth** — the subject-personalization recipe: fine-tune a
  diffusion model on a few photos of a subject bound to a rare token
  ("a photo of sks dog"), with **class-preservation regularization**
  (training also on generic dogs) so the model doesn't forget what
  dogs in general look like.
- **Textual inversion** — a lighter alternative: learn only a new
  *embedding* for the subject, leaving all model weights untouched.
- **Overfitting** — here in a vivid form: an over-trained adapter
  memorizes the exact training photos and can only reproduce them.
- **CLIP/DINO similarity, FID** — automated measures of subject
  fidelity and realism used in evaluation.
</Callout>

## 1. Clarify the problem

Two goals, each with its own quality bar. **Editing**: inpainting,
outpainting, and instructed edits ("make the jacket red") that change
*only* what was asked while keeping the rest coherent — the failure
users punish hardest is a visible seam or a background that subtly
shifted. **Personalization**: from 3–20 user photos, learn the
subject well enough to generate it in entirely new scenes, poses, and
styles — preserving **identity** without collapsing into copies of
the training shots.

Scope: the conditioning and personalization machinery on top of a
diffusion base, plus its serving and safety. The base model's
training, sampling, and general safety story live in the
text-to-image case.

<Callout type="tip" title="Interview signal">
The two hard subproblems are **mask-conditioned generation that stays
coherent** and **fast, cheap per-user personalization**. Naming
personalization as a *fine-tuning* problem (not prompting) is the key
fork — and LoRA as the production answer is the follow-through.
</Callout>

## 2. Requirements & scale

| Dimension | Target | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Edit fidelity | Change only the masked region | Mask-conditioned inpainting; latent blending |
| Personalization | Few images, minutes to train | LoRA/textual-inversion, not full fine-tune |
| Storage | Per-user weights | Tiny LoRA adapters, not full models |
| Serving | Swap adapters at inference | Adapter hot-loading on a shared base |

<ThinkFirst question="The base model has never seen the user's dog. What are the candidate ways to teach it — and what does each cost?">
Prompting is out — words describe categories, and no description
selects a specific unseen individual. That leaves a spectrum of
"how much do we change the model." **Textual inversion** learns only
a new embedding: the lightest option (kilobytes, no weight changes)
but the lowest fidelity — often enough for a *style*, rarely for a
*face*. **Full DreamBooth fine-tuning** changes the whole model: best
fidelity, but hours of GPU and gigabytes of weights *per user* —
economically dead at scale. The production sweet spot is the **LoRA
adapter**: minutes to train, megabytes to store, and — crucially for
serving — hot-swappable onto a shared frozen base.
</ThinkFirst>

## 3. GenAI problem framing

**Editing** is conditional diffusion with a richer condition: instead
of just a text prompt, the model receives the input image, the mask,
and the instruction. Denoising regenerates the masked latent region
*conditioned on the untouched context around it* — which is how the
new content comes out lit, styled, and perspective-matched to its
surroundings rather than pasted on.

**Personalization** is few-shot concept learning by fine-tuning:
bind the subject to a rare token, adapt the model on the user's
photos (DreamBooth recipe), and pay for the data efficiency with
regularization against forgetting — implemented economically as a
LoRA adapter per the ThinkFirst.

The pre-generative baseline is manual Photoshop work: enormously
capable, but gated on skill and time. Generative editing collapses
"an hour of masking and clone-stamping" into a sentence — that's the
product's value proposition, and also why edit *fidelity* is the bar:
the tool competes with a careful human's output.

## 4. Data & training *(spine: data)*

Editing models train on (image, mask, target) triples and
instruction-edit pairs — with the practical note that masks are
usually *synthesized* during training (random regions, object
segments), because "images with human-drawn masks" isn't a dataset
that exists at scale.

Personalization inverts the usual data story: the training set is the
*user's own photos, supplied at request time*. That has two
consequences worth naming. First, privacy: these per-user images and
the adapters trained from them are sensitive data — scoped to the
user, deletable on request. Second, statistics: with 3–20 images,
overfitting isn't a risk, it's the default — which is why
**class-preservation regularization** (training alongside generic
images of the subject's class) is part of the recipe, keeping the
model's general concept of "dog" intact while it learns *this* dog.

## 5. Model & generation *(spine: hypothesis-space + optimization)*

The architecture assembles from parts already established: one
**shared latent-diffusion base**, frozen, serving everyone. Per-user
**LoRA adapters** — trained in minutes on a single GPU, a few
megabytes each — carry the personalization. **Inpainting** runs the
conditioned denoiser on the masked region and blends in latent space,
which is what makes edits seamless rather than pasted.

The visualization shows why LoRA is the economic unlock: the base

so "thousands of personalized models" is really "one model plus
thousands of small files."

<WikiLink slug="ddim-sampling" title="DDIM sampling for diffusion edits" />

<ThinkFirst question="A million users each have a personalized model. How do you serve that without a million model copies?">
Notice what personalization actually changed: a few megabytes of
low-rank deltas — nothing else. So keep **one** frozen base model
resident on the GPUs, and **hot-load the requesting user's LoRA
adapter** onto it per request; training itself is an async job whose
entire output is the adapter file. The reframing — "a model per user"
becomes "a weight-delta per user" — is the whole economics of the
feature. Without it, personalization is a demo; with it, it's a
product.
</ThinkFirst>

## 6. Serving & scaling

The serving design implements the ThinkFirst directly. The shared
base stays resident; adapters live in storage keyed by user and
hot-load in milliseconds at request time. Personalization requests
enqueue an async training job (minutes on one GPU) whose completion
makes the adapter available — the user uploads photos, gets a
progress indicator, and returns to a personalized model.
Editing requests carry their image and mask as conditioning inputs
and batch like ordinary generation traffic.

<ThinkFirst question="Personalization “worked” — by what measure? Two qualities pull against each other here. Which?">
**Identity fidelity versus editability.** Overtrain the adapter and
it memorizes the training photos: perfect likeness, but every
generation is the same pose against the same backyard, and prompts
bounce off it — the dog cannot be put in the spacesuit, which was the
entire point. Undertrain and it re-poses beautifully but stops
looking like *this* dog. These are two axes, and optimizing likeness
alone quietly destroys the product; both must be measured, and the
training recipe (LoRA rank, steps, regularization) tuned against the
pair.
</ThinkFirst>

## 7. Evaluation *(spine: evaluation)*

For **edits**, two measurements matching the two promises: quality of
the regenerated region (realism, human-rated and FID-style), and —
the one teams forget — **background preservation**: did anything
outside the mask change? Pixel-difference checks outside the mask
make the "change only what was asked" promise verifiable rather than
aspirational.

For **personalization**, the ThinkFirst's two axes, measured
explicitly: **subject fidelity** via CLIP/DINO embedding similarity
between generations and the reference photos, and **prompt
editability** via adherence checks on a standard battery of re-posing
prompts ("in the snow," "as a watercolor"). An adapter is evaluated
as a point on that two-axis plane — never on likeness alone — with
overfitting flagged when generations start reproducing training-shot
compositions.

## 8. Safety & guardrails

Personalization sharpens the safety question, because its defining
abuse is specific: **training an adapter on a real person without
their consent** — the machinery of a deepfake, offered as a product
feature. The gates: consent requirements for human subjects,
face/identity checks that block celebrity and nonconsensual
personalization, and watermarking on outputs. This is a hard
guardrail, not a filter with a threshold.

Editing adds its own abuse patterns on top of the base model's
content-safety stack: watermark removal, evidence tampering, and
compositing real people into fabricated scenes — screened at the
edit-instruction and output layers. And the privacy line from
section 4 holds throughout: user photos and adapters are private
artifacts, deletable on request.

## 9. Tradeoffs & alternatives

| Choice | Alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| LoRA personalization | Full DreamBooth fine-tune | Minutes vs. hours, MBs vs. GBs, hot-swappable |
| Adapter hot-loading | Per-user model copies | One shared base serves everyone |
| Mask-conditioned inpainting | Regenerate whole image | Preserves the untouched regions exactly |
| Textual inversion | LoRA | Even lighter, but usually lower fidelity — fine for styles, weak for faces |

## 10. Interviewer follow-ups

<Details summary="Personalization either doesn't look like the subject or can't be re-posed. Tradeoff?">
That's the identity-vs-editability axis showing up as a support
ticket. Over-trained: the adapter memorized the training images —
great likeness, zero flexibility. Under-trained: flexible but
generic. The levers: LoRA rank and training steps (capacity and
exposure), class-preservation regularization (protects the general
concept so prompts still work), and — the process fix — evaluating
every adapter on both axes before it ships, so the tradeoff is chosen
deliberately instead of discovered by the user.
</Details>

<Details summary="How do you serve millions of personalized users without a model per user?">
By noticing that a "personalized model" is 99.9% the same weights as
everyone else's. One shared frozen base sits on the GPUs; each user
is a few-megabyte LoRA adapter hot-loaded for their request;
personalization is an async training job whose output is just that
file. Storage scales in megabytes per user, GPU memory holds one
model, and the marginal serving cost of personalization is an adapter
load — that reframing is the entire feasibility argument.
</Details>

<Details summary="A user uploads photos of a celebrity to personalize. What happens?">
Blocked, as a hard guardrail: the consent-and-likeness policy gates
personalization on real people, enforced by face/identity matching
against public figures and consent attestation for private ones, with
output watermarking as the downstream backstop. Nonconsensual
likeness personalization is *the* signature abuse of this feature —
it's deepfake tooling if unguarded — so it gets the
never-should-happen treatment rather than a tunable threshold.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Editing and personalization extend text-to-image with **image+mask
  conditioning** and **per-user fine-tuning** — and personalization
  is a fine-tuning problem by nature; no prompt selects an unseen
  subject.
- **LoRA + adapter hot-loading is the economic unlock**: one frozen
  base, a megabytes-scale weight-delta per user, minutes-scale async
  training.
- Quality is two-dimensional twice over: edits need **masked-region
  realism *and* background preservation**; personalization needs
  **identity fidelity *and* editability** — each pair trades off and
  must be measured as a pair.
- **Consent and likeness are the defining guardrails**: unguarded,
  this feature is deepfake machinery — real-person personalization is
  gated hard, watermarked, and privacy-scoped.
