# MCMC Sampling: Metropolis–Hastings & Gibbs

Bayesian inference produces a posterior $p(\theta \mid x) \propto p(x \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)$. The proportionality hides a problem: the normalizing constant $p(x) = \int p(x \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\, d\theta$ is usually an intractable high-dimensional integral. **Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)** sidesteps it entirely — it draws samples from the posterior *knowing the density only up to that constant*, then answers every downstream question (means, intervals, predictions) with sample averages.

## Why sampling, and why Monte Carlo works

Almost every Bayesian quantity is an expectation under the posterior:

$$
\mathbb{E}[g(\theta) \mid x] = \int g(\theta)\, p(\theta \mid x)\, d\theta \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(\theta^{(i)}), \qquad \theta^{(i)} \sim p(\theta \mid x)
$$

If we can *draw samples* $\theta^{(i)}$ from the posterior, the integral becomes an average — the **Monte Carlo** estimate, which converges by the law of large numbers. The catch: we can't sample directly from a distribution we only know up to a constant. MCMC's trick is to construct a **Markov chain** whose stationary distribution *is* the posterior; run it long enough and its states are (correlated) samples from the target.

## Metropolis–Hastings

The general-purpose recipe. To sample from a target $\pi(\theta)$ known up to a constant:

1. From the current state $\theta$, draw a candidate $\theta'$ from a **proposal** $q(\theta' \mid \theta)$ (e.g. a Gaussian centered at $\theta$).
2. Compute the **acceptance ratio**

$$
\alpha = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\pi(\theta')\, q(\theta \mid \theta')}{\pi(\theta)\, q(\theta' \mid \theta)}\right)
$$

3. Accept $\theta'$ with probability $\alpha$ (set next state to $\theta'$); otherwise **stay** at $\theta$ (and record $\theta$ again).
4. Repeat.

The normalizing constant **cancels in the ratio** $\pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta)$ — which is the whole point. You only ever need the unnormalized posterior $p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta)$.

When the proposal is symmetric ($q(\theta'\mid\theta) = q(\theta\mid\theta')$, as for a Gaussian random walk), the $q$ terms cancel too and the ratio collapses to the original **Metropolis** form $\alpha = \min(1, \pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta))$ — always move to higher density, sometimes move to lower.

<Callout type="info" title="Detailed balance is why it works">
  The acceptance rule is engineered so the chain satisfies **detailed balance**: $\pi(\theta)\,P(\theta \to \theta') = \pi(\theta')\,P(\theta' \to \theta)$. A chain in detailed balance with $\pi$ is reversible and has $\pi$ as its stationary distribution — so its long-run samples are distributed according to the target, whatever the (positive) proposal.
</Callout>

What to notice as you step:

- **The constant never appears.** Every decision is made from
  $\pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta)$, and the normalizer cancels there. That single fact
  is why MCMC works on posteriors nobody can integrate.
- **Downhill moves are accepted on purpose.** A proposal with ratio $0.41$ is
  taken 41% of the time. Reject those always and you have hill-climbing, which
  converges to the mode and gets the *distribution* completely wrong.
- **A rejection still emits a sample.** The current state is recorded again,
  which is why trace plots have flat stretches — and why a high rejection rate
  destroys the effective sample size without shortening the chain.
- **The first steps are burn-in**, and they describe the starting point rather
  than the target.
- **The last step is the tuning lesson**: σ = 0.005 accepts 98% of proposals and
  is nearly useless (ESS 6); σ = 5 rejects 97% and is nearly useless (ESS 55);
  σ = 0.3 gets ESS 813 at a 46% acceptance rate — almost exactly the ~44% that
  the next section recommends for a single parameter.

## Gibbs sampling

When you can derive the **full conditional** distribution of each parameter given all the others, $p(\theta_j \mid \theta_{-j}, x)$, and sample from it directly, Gibbs sampling applies. Cycle through the parameters, updating one at a time from its full conditional:

$$
\theta_1^{(t+1)} \sim p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2^{(t)}, \ldots, \theta_d^{(t)}, x), \quad
\theta_2^{(t+1)} \sim p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1^{(t+1)}, \theta_3^{(t)}, \ldots, x), \quad \ldots
$$

Gibbs is a special case of Metropolis–Hastings where the proposal is the full conditional and **every move is accepted** ($\alpha = 1$). It shines for **conditionally conjugate** models (hierarchical models, mixtures with latent labels) where each conditional is a standard distribution. Its weakness: when parameters are highly correlated, updating one axis at a time produces slow, zig-zag exploration.

## The practical machinery

A raw chain is not yet a clean set of posterior samples. Four issues to handle:

- **Burn-in.** The chain starts at an arbitrary point and needs time to reach the high-density region. Discard the first $K$ samples (e.g. the first 10–50%) so the start point doesn't bias estimates.
- **Thinning.** Consecutive states are autocorrelated. Keeping every $k$-th sample reduces storage and autocorrelation — though for estimating means it's usually better to keep everything and rely on the effective sample size. Thin only when storage or per-sample cost is the bottleneck.
- **Acceptance rate tuning.** The proposal scale is the key knob. Too small → nearly all proposals accepted but tiny steps, glacial exploration. Too large → big jumps almost always rejected, the chain gets stuck. The classic target is **~23%** acceptance for high-dimensional random-walk Metropolis (≈44% for a single parameter).
- **Multiple chains.** Run several chains from dispersed starts to diagnose convergence and detect multimodality a single chain might miss.

## Convergence diagnostics

You can never *prove* convergence, but several diagnostics catch failure:

- **Trace plots.** Plot each parameter against iteration. A converged chain looks like a stationary "fuzzy caterpillar"; trends, drift, or a chain stuck in a region signal trouble.
- **$\hat R$ (Gelman–Rubin).** Compares between-chain and within-chain variance across multiple chains. $\hat R \approx 1$ (conventionally $< 1.01$) indicates the chains have mixed to the same distribution; $\hat R$ well above 1 means they haven't.
- **Effective sample size (ESS).** Because samples are autocorrelated, $N$ draws carry the information of fewer independent ones: $\mathrm{ESS} = N / (1 + 2\sum_{k\ge 1}\rho_k)$, where $\rho_k$ is the lag-$k$ autocorrelation. ESS is what actually determines Monte-Carlo error — report it, not the raw chain length.

<Callout type="warning" title="A converged-looking chain can still be wrong">
  Good trace plots and $\hat R \approx 1$ show the chains *agree* — not that they found the whole posterior. If every chain starts near the same mode of a multimodal posterior, they can all converge to that mode and report $\hat R = 1.00$ while missing the rest of the distribution. Disperse the starting points and, where feasible, use samplers built for multimodality (parallel tempering, HMC/NUTS).
</Callout>

## Beyond random-walk MCMC

Random-walk Metropolis explores by diffusion, which scales poorly in high dimensions. **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC)** uses the gradient of the log-posterior to propose distant, high-acceptance moves; the **No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS)** auto-tunes its trajectory length and is the default engine in Stan, PyMC, and NumPyro. When you have gradients and a continuous parameter space, HMC/NUTS typically delivers far higher ESS per second than the methods on this page — which remain the conceptual foundation and the right tool for discrete or non-differentiable targets.
