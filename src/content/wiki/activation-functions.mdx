# Activation Functions: From Sigmoid to GELU

An **activation function** is the non-linearity applied to a neuron's pre-activation $z = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b$. Without it, stacking layers is pointless: the composition of linear maps is still linear, so a 100-layer network would collapse to a single matrix multiply. The activation is what lets depth buy expressive power, and the *choice* of activation governs how gradients flow, which is the difference between a network that trains and one that stalls.

## The job of an activation

Two properties matter for training a deep network by gradient descent:

1. **Non-linearity** — so composing layers produces genuinely new functions (curved decision boundaries, not just hyperplanes).
2. **A well-behaved derivative**: backprop multiplies derivatives layer by layer. If each factor is much smaller than 1, the product shrinks toward zero through depth (the **vanishing gradient**); if much larger, it explodes. The activation's derivative is one factor in every one of those products.

This is why "use a non-linearity" is necessary but not sufficient — sigmoid is non-linear yet trains deep nets poorly, because its derivative saturates.

## Saturation and the vanishing gradient

A function **saturates** when its output flattens for large $|z|$, driving its derivative toward 0. Sigmoid $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$ has derivative $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$, which **peaks at just 0.25** (at $z=0$) and is near 0 once $|z| \gtrsim 5$. Stack ten sigmoid layers and the gradient reaching the first layer is scaled by at most $0.25^{10} \approx 10^{-6}$ — the early layers barely learn.

<Callout type="warning" title="Sigmoid is also not zero-centered">
  Sigmoid outputs are all positive (in $(0,1)$), so every weight feeding into a downstream neuron receives a gradient of the same sign on a given example. That forces inefficient zig-zag updates. Tanh fixes the centering (range $(-1,1)$) but still saturates — its derivative $1-\tanh^2(z)$ peaks at 1 but vanishes in the tails just like sigmoid.
</Callout>

## ReLU and its family

**ReLU** $\max(0, z)$ is the modern default for hidden layers. Its derivative is exactly **1** for $z > 0$ (it does not shrink gradients in the active region) and it costs a single comparison. That combination is why deep nets became trainable at scale.

Its weakness is the **dead/dying ReLU**: a neuron whose pre-activation is negative for *every* input outputs 0 with derivative 0, so it receives no gradient and can never recover. A bad initialization or too-large a learning rate can kill a large fraction of units permanently.

The variants patch this leak by giving negative inputs a small, nonzero slope:

| Function | Formula ($z<0$ branch) | Negative-side gradient | Note |
|----------|------------------------|------------------------|------|
| **Leaky ReLU** | $\alpha z$ (e.g. $\alpha=0.01$) | constant $\alpha$ | never fully dies |
| **PReLU** | $\alpha z$, $\alpha$ **learned** | learned per-channel | extra parameters |
| **ELU** | $\alpha(e^z - 1)$ | smooth, $\to 0$ in tail | pushes mean activation toward 0 |

## Smooth modern activations: GELU and Swish

Transformers and recent vision models favor smooth, non-monotonic activations that empirically train better than ReLU at scale:

- **GELU** (Gaussian Error Linear Unit): $\text{GELU}(z) = z\,\Phi(z)$, where $\Phi$ is the standard-normal CDF. It weights the input by the probability it is retained — a "soft" gate. The default in BERT, GPT, and ViT.
- **Swish / SiLU**: $\text{SiLU}(z) = z\,\sigma(z)$. Closely related to GELU; self-gated and smooth. Used in EfficientNet and many LLMs (often as the gate in **SwiGLU** feed-forward blocks).

Both are smooth everywhere (no kink at 0) and allow small negative outputs, which helps gradient flow without the dead-unit problem.

## Softmax: a special case for outputs

**Softmax** is not a hidden-layer activation; it is the output layer for **multi-class classification**. It maps a vector of logits to a probability distribution:

$$
\text{softmax}(z)_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}
$$

It couples all outputs (they sum to 1) and pairs with cross-entropy loss, whose combined gradient simplifies to the clean $\hat{p} - y$. For numerical stability, subtract $\max_j z_j$ from every logit before exponentiating. This is exact (softmax is shift-invariant) and prevents overflow.

## Derivatives at a glance

| Activation | $f(z)$ | $f'(z)$ | Max of $f'$ |
|------------|--------|---------|-------------|
| Sigmoid | $\sigma(z)$ | $\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$ | 0.25 |
| Tanh | $\tanh(z)$ | $1-\tanh^2(z)$ | 1.0 |
| ReLU | $\max(0,z)$ | $\mathbf{1}[z>0]$ | 1.0 |
| Leaky ReLU | $\max(\alpha z, z)$ | $1$ if $z>0$ else $\alpha$ | 1.0 |
| ELU | $z$ if $z>0$ else $\alpha(e^z-1)$ | $1$ if $z>0$ else $f(z)+\alpha$ | 1.0 |

The pattern that matters: sigmoid's $0.25$ ceiling vs ReLU's $1.0$ is the heart of why the field moved away from saturating activations for hidden layers.

## A practical chooser

- **Hidden layers, default:** ReLU. Cheap, well-understood, strong baseline.
- **ReLU units dying:** switch to Leaky ReLU / ELU, or lower the learning rate and fix initialization.
- **Transformers / large models:** GELU (or SwiGLU in the feed-forward block).
- **Binary classification output:** sigmoid (one logit → probability).
- **Multi-class output:** softmax over the logits.
- **Regression output:** usually **no activation** (linear) on the final layer.

<Callout type="info" title="Initialization is tuned to the activation">
  He initialization is designed for ReLU (variance $2/n_\text{in}$); Xavier/Glorot for tanh/sigmoid ($1/n_\text{in}$). Pairing the wrong init with an activation reintroduces vanishing or exploding signals on the very first forward pass, which is why activation choice and weight init are decided together.
</Callout>
