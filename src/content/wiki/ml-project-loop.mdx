# The ML Project Loop

Most ML curricula are organised as a catalogue — supervised, unsupervised, then a
parade of algorithms, which leaves you with fifty disconnected techniques and no
way to place a fifty-first. This page is the antidote: a single decomposition that
**every** technique on the site attaches to.

$$
\text{data} \rightarrow \text{hypothesis space} \rightarrow \text{objective}
\rightarrow \text{optimization} \rightarrow \text{evaluation}
\rightarrow \text{deployment feedback} \rightarrow (\text{back to data})
$$

<ProjectLoopViz variant="ml" />

Pick this decomposition and never abandon it. A new paper, a new trick, a new loss
— each is a modification to **one or two** of those six slots, and almost never all
six at once. Once you internalise that, learning stops being memorisation and
becomes placement.

## The slot test

For anything you encounter, ask two questions:

> **Which slot does this modify, and what was breaking before?**

The slot names the *kind* of change; "what was breaking" names the *reason it
exists*. The delta is the understanding — the technique on its own is trivia.

| Technique | Slot it changes | What was breaking before |
|---|---|---|
| Focal loss | **objective** | class imbalance let easy negatives dominate the loss, drowning rare positives |
| Adam | **optimization** | plain SGD crawled through ill-conditioned, ravine-shaped loss surfaces |
| Transformer | **hypothesis space** | RNNs couldn't reach long-range dependencies and couldn't be parallelised |
| Dropout | **hypothesis space** (capacity) | over-parameterised nets memorised the training set |
| Walk-forward validation | **evaluation** | random k-fold leaked the future into training on time-ordered data |
| Feature store | **data** / **feedback** | features were computed one way in training and another in serving (train/serve skew) |

<Callout type="tip" title="The transfer test">
You've genuinely learned the loop when you can place a technique you were **never
taught**. Try it now: which slot does *label smoothing* change, and what was
breaking? (Objective — hard one-hot targets made the model over-confident and
mis-calibrated.) The [notebook](https://ml-viz-ruby.vercel.app/wiki/ml-project-loop)
turns this into a drill.
</Callout>

## One problem, all the way around: daily demand forecasting

A single problem revisited at every layer builds more intuition than any amount of
cross-referencing. The running example across the curriculum is a **daily demand
forecast** — it has a tabular baseline, a natural sequence-model version, a
distribution-shift story, an uncertainty story, and a live-serving feedback loop.

**The canonical dataset (`daily-demand`, seed = 7).** A synthetic series of
$T = 730$ days so every notebook can regenerate it identically:

$$
y_t = \underbrace{200 + 0.15\,t}_{\text{trend}}
\;\cdot\; \underbrace{w_{(t \bmod 7)}}_{\text{weekly}}
\;\cdot\; \underbrace{p_t}_{\text{promo}}
\;+\; \underbrace{60 \cdot \mathbb{1}[t \ge 400]}_{\text{regime shift}}
\;+\; \varepsilon_t,
\qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 12^2)
$$

with weekday multipliers $w = [1.0, 0.95, 0.98, 1.0, 1.15, 1.30, 1.20]$ (Mon–Sun),
a promotion multiplier $p_t \in \{1.6, 1.9\}$ on a random $\approx 4\%$ of days
(else $1$), and a permanent level jump of $+60$ from day 400 (a new store opens).
That regime shift is deliberate — it's what makes the **feedback** stage matter.

Here is the whole loop on this one problem:

### 1 · Data
Build the series, then features that respect **time**: calendar features
(day-of-week, day-of-year) and **lags** ($y_{t-1}, y_{t-7}$). Split
chronologically — train on days 0–599, validate 600–664, test 665–729 — because a
random shuffle would let tomorrow's value help predict today (leakage). This slot
is where most real projects live and die.
→ deeper: [ML Systems Design](/courses/ml-in-practice/04-ml-systems-design),
[Feature Engineering](/courses/ml-in-practice), [Time Series](/courses/time-series).

### 2 · Hypothesis space
Choose the family of functions the model may express. Start small: a **linear model**
on the lag + calendar features — the tabular baseline. That choice *is* an inductive
bias (additive, no interactions). Later you can widen it to gradient boosting or an
RNN; each is a hypothesis-space change, nothing else.
→ deeper: [Linear Regression](/courses/linear-regression),
[Neural Networks](/courses/neural-networks), [Ensemble Methods](/courses/ensemble-methods).

### 3 · Objective
Define what "good" means. Squared error (MSE) if large misses hurt most; absolute
error (MAE) if you want robustness to promo spikes; the **pinball loss** if you need
quantiles for a safety-stock decision. Add an $\ell_2$ penalty to keep the lag
weights from exploding. Same data, same model — a different objective encodes a
different business cost.
→ deeper: [Loss functions](/courses/optimization-ml),
[Regularization](/courses/linear-regression).

### 4 · Optimization
Search the hypothesis space for parameters that minimise the objective. A linear
model with squared error has a closed-form solution (normal equations); anything
larger is gradient descent and its variants. The objective's *shape* — convex here,
ravine-like for deep nets — decides how hard this slot is.
→ deeper: [Optimization for ML](/courses/optimization-ml),
[Gradient Descent](/courses/neural-networks).

### 5 · Evaluation
Estimate generalisation **honestly**. On time-ordered data that means
**walk-forward** backtesting, not random k-fold. Report MAE / MAPE, check whether the
predicted quantiles are actually calibrated (does the 90% band contain 90% of
outcomes?), and do error analysis — you'll find the error spikes right at day 400.
→ deeper: [Model Evaluation](/courses/model-evaluation),
[Walk-forward validation](/wiki/walk-forward-validation),
[Bias–variance](/wiki/bias-variance-decomposition).

### 6 · Deployment feedback
Ship it, then watch it. The regime shift at day 400 quietly wrecks a model trained
only on days 0–399: residuals drift upward, MAPE degrades, and a drift monitor
should fire and trigger a retrain on fresh data. That retrain **closes the loop** —
the feedback stage feeds straight back into *data*.
→ deeper: [Monitoring & drift](/courses/ml-in-practice),
[Continual learning](/courses/ml-in-practice/09-continual-learning-and-test-in-production),
[Streaming ML](/courses/streaming-ml).

## Order by the moving bottleneck

The loop also explains why the curriculum is *ordered* the way it is. Read the eras
as a story where **each era's fix creates the next era's constraint**:

- **Classical ML** is where all six slots are visible at once and the math is
  tractable — the best place to learn the loop itself.
- **Deep learning** made the *hypothesis space* expressive enough that
  **optimization** became the hard part (vanishing gradients, initialization,
  normalization).
- **Scale** pushed the bottleneck into **memory bandwidth and communication** —
  the whole subject of [GPU programming](/courses/gpu-programming).
- **Production** moved it into **data and feedback loops**: drift, labelling,
  train/serve skew, retraining cadence — the subject of
  [ML in Practice](/courses/ml-in-practice) and [Streaming ML](/courses/streaming-ml).

You can predict what comes next by asking which slot is currently the bottleneck.
That predictive power is the real test that the structure has clicked.
