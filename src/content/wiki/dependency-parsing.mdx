# Dependency Parsing

<Callout type="warning" title="Advanced material">
  A specialized classical-NLP topic. Modern LLMs rarely parse explicitly, but dependency structure remains important for linguistics, information extraction, and interpretability. Read [Text Preprocessing](/courses/nlp/01-text-preprocessing) first for tokenization background.
</Callout>

Dependency parsing recovers the **grammatical structure** of a sentence as a tree of binary relations between words. Each word (except the root) has exactly one **head** (the word it grammatically depends on) labeled with a relation type like `nsubj` (nominal subject) or `dobj` (direct object).

## The dependency tree

For *"The cat sat on the mat"*:

```
        sat (ROOT)
       /  |    \
    cat  on   (punct)
    /      \
  The      mat
            |
           the
```

- `sat` is the **root** (the main verb)
- `cat` is the subject of `sat` (`nsubj`)
- `on` is a prepositional modifier (`prep`), `mat` is its object (`pobj`)
- `The`, `the` are determiners (`det`)

A valid dependency tree satisfies:
1. **Single head**: every word has exactly one parent
2. **Connected & acyclic**: it forms a tree rooted at a single node
3. **Projective** (often, but not always): no crossing edges when words are laid out in order

## Dependency vs. constituency parsing

| | Constituency (phrase-structure) | Dependency |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | Nested phrases (NP, VP, …) | Word-to-word head relations |
| **Nodes** | Non-terminal phrase nodes + words | Just words |
| **Best for** | Languages with rigid word order (English) | Free-word-order languages; downstream relation extraction |

Dependency parses are flatter and map more directly to "who did what to whom," which is why they dominate modern applied NLP.

## Transition-based parsing

Treats parsing as a sequence of actions over a **stack** and **buffer**, building the tree incrementally — fast ($O(n)$) and the basis of most neural parsers. The **arc-standard** system has three actions:

- **SHIFT**: move the next buffer word onto the stack
- **LEFT-ARC**: add a dependency edge; the second-top stack item becomes a child of the top; remove it
- **RIGHT-ARC**: add an edge the other direction; remove the top item

A classifier (originally a SVM/perceptron with hand-crafted features; now a neural network) predicts the next action from the current configuration. Chen & Manning (2014) showed a simple feed-forward network over word/POS/arc embeddings dramatically outperformed feature-engineered parsers — an early NLP deep-learning win.

## Graph-based parsing

Instead of greedy local actions, score **every possible edge** and find the maximum-spanning-tree over the complete directed graph of words:

$$
\hat{T} = \arg\max_{T \in \mathcal{T}(x)} \sum_{(h, m) \in T} \text{score}(h, m)
$$

The **Chu-Liu/Edmonds algorithm** finds the maximum spanning arborescence in $O(n^2)$. Graph-based parsers (e.g., the **biaffine attention parser**, Dozat & Manning 2017) are globally optimal over edges and currently achieve state-of-the-art accuracy. The biaffine parser uses a transformer/BiLSTM encoder, then a biaffine scorer:

$$
\text{score}(h, m) = \mathbf{h}_h^\top \mathbf{U}\, \mathbf{h}_m + \mathbf{b}
$$

## Evaluation metrics

- **UAS (Unlabeled Attachment Score):** fraction of words assigned the correct head
- **LAS (Labeled Attachment Score):** fraction with both correct head *and* correct relation label

LAS is the stricter, standard metric. Modern parsers exceed 95% LAS on English newswire.

## Where parsing fits today

Even in the LLM era, dependency parsing remains useful for:

- **Information extraction**: subject-verb-object triples for knowledge graphs
- **Relation extraction**: the dependency path between two entities is a strong feature
- **Linguistic analysis & low-resource languages**: explicit structure where labeled data is scarce
- **Interpretability**: probing whether transformer attention heads encode syntactic structure (they partly do — "structural probes" recover dependency trees from BERT embeddings)
