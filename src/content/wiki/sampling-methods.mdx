# Sampling Methods: Inverse Transform, Rejection & Importance Sampling

Most quantities in probabilistic ML are **expectations** $\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(x)] = \int f(x)\,p(x)\,dx$ — a predictive average, a marginal likelihood, an expected reward. When the integral has no closed form, we estimate it by drawing samples $x^{(i)} \sim p$ and averaging. The catch: how do you sample from $p$ when all you have is a uniform random number generator and a formula for the density? This page covers the foundational answers. (For resampling *data* see the bootstrap; for sampling *correlated chains* from intractable posteriors see MCMC — both linked below.)

Everything starts from one primitive: `rng.random()`, a draw from $\text{Uniform}(0,1)$.

## Inverse transform sampling

If you can write down and invert the CDF $F(x) = P(X \le x)$, sampling is exact and one-shot:

1. Draw $u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$.
2. Return $x = F^{-1}(u)$.

**Why it works:** $F(X)$ is itself uniform on $(0,1)$ (the *probability integral transform*), so pushing a uniform back through $F^{-1}$ reproduces the target. For the exponential distribution $F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$, inverting gives the well-known generator $x = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln(1-u)$.

**Limitation:** it requires a tractable inverse CDF. For the Gaussian, $F^{-1}$ has no elementary form (hence dedicated tricks like Box–Muller); for most multivariate or unnormalized densities there is no inverse to invert. That is where the next two methods come in.

## Rejection sampling

When you can *evaluate* the target $p(x)$ (even unnormalized) but can't invert it, sample from an easy **proposal** $q(x)$ and throw away the samples that don't fit. Pick a constant $M$ such that $p(x) \le M\,q(x)$ everywhere, then:

1. Draw a candidate $x \sim q$.
2. Draw $u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$.
3. **Accept** $x$ if $u \le \dfrac{p(x)}{M\,q(x)}$; otherwise reject and repeat.

Accepted samples are distributed *exactly* as $p$. Geometrically: throw darts uniformly under the curve $M q(x)$ and keep only those that also land under $p(x)$.

<Callout type="warning" title="The acceptance rate is 1/M — and M explodes in high dimensions">
  The fraction of proposals accepted is $1/M$, so a loose envelope wastes most draws. Worse, the smallest valid $M$ grows roughly **exponentially with dimension** when $q$ doesn't closely match $p$ — in 100-D, rejection sampling can accept essentially nothing. This curse of dimensionality is precisely why MCMC, which doesn't reject its way to the target, dominates for high-dimensional posteriors.
</Callout>

## Importance sampling

Sometimes you don't need samples from $p$ at all — you only need an **expectation** under $p$, and sampling $p$ directly is hard (or you want to reuse samples from a different distribution). Importance sampling rewrites the expectation as one under a tractable proposal $q$:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(x)] = \int f(x)\,\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,q(x)\,dx = \mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[f(x)\,w(x)\right], \quad w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
$$

Draw $x^{(i)} \sim q$ and average $f(x^{(i)})\,w(x^{(i)})$. The **importance weights** $w$ correct for sampling from the wrong distribution. When $p$ is known only up to a constant (the usual Bayesian case), use **self-normalized** importance sampling, dividing by $\sum_i w(x^{(i)})$.

**Choosing $q$ is everything.** Variance is minimized when $q(x) \propto |f(x)|\,p(x)$ — you should sample *more* where the integrand is large. The classic application is estimating rare events: to estimate $P(X > 10)$ for a standard normal, sampling from $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ almost never lands in the tail, but a shifted proposal $\mathcal{N}(10, 1)$ puts samples exactly where they count, then reweights.

<Callout type="info" title="A heavy-tailed proposal is safer than a light-tailed one">
  If $q$ has thinner tails than $p$, the ratio $w = p/q$ blows up in the tails and a single sample can dominate the estimate — the variance can even become **infinite** while the estimate looks deceptively stable. Rule of thumb: pick a proposal at least as heavy-tailed as the target, and always inspect the distribution of weights (a few weights carrying all the mass is the warning sign).
</Callout>

## Variance reduction

The Monte Carlo error of an estimate shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N}$. These tricks reduce the constant in front, buying accuracy without more samples:

- **Antithetic variates**: pair each draw $u$ with its mirror $1-u$. For a monotone $f$ the two are negatively correlated, so their average has lower variance than two independent draws.
- **Stratified sampling**: partition the domain into strata and sample a fixed quota from each, eliminating the "clumping" of pure random sampling. (Latin hypercube sampling generalizes this to many dimensions.)
- **Control variates**: subtract a correlated quantity with known mean to cancel part of the noise.

## Choosing a method

| You have… | You want… | Use |
|-----------|-----------|-----|
| Invertible CDF | exact samples | inverse transform |
| Evaluable density + good proposal, low-D | exact samples | rejection sampling |
| Tractable proposal; only need an expectation | an estimate (incl. rare events) | importance sampling |
| Unnormalized high-D density (e.g. a posterior) | samples | **MCMC** (see below) |
