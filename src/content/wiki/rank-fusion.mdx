# Rank Fusion (RRF) for Hybrid Search

Hybrid search runs two retrievers — a **keyword** engine (BM25) and a **dense** embedding model — and merges their results. The obvious merge is to average the two scores. It quietly fails: BM25 scores documents on a 0–40-ish scale, cosine similarity on a 0.6–0.9 scale. Averaging a 38 with a 0.72 is averaging **40 rupees and 0.8 dollars** — the number with the bigger magnitude wins every time, so the fused list collapses onto the BM25 ranking and the embedding model contributes almost nothing.

**Scores are opinions in different currencies. Ranks are currency-free: 1st place is 1st place everywhere.** Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) fuses *positions*, not scores.

## Why averaging is absorption

A weighted score sum $s = w_1 s_1 + w_2 s_2$ is dominated by whichever retriever has the larger magnitude **and variance**. If $s_1 \in [0, 40]$ and $s_2 \in [0.6, 0.9]$, then moving a document from worst to best in the dense list changes $s$ by at most $0.3$, while one step in the BM25 list can change it by several *units*. The dense signal is noise next to the keyword signal — the fused order is the BM25 order with a rounding error.

**Per-query normalization (min–max, z-score) helps but is brittle.** It rescales each list to $[0,1]$ per query, but a single outlier document reshapes the whole scale: one document scoring 200 when the rest score ~30 compresses every real distinction into the bottom 15% of the range. Normalization also has to be recomputed per query and is sensitive to how many candidates each retriever returns.

## Reciprocal Rank Fusion

Give each document a fused score that depends only on its **rank** $r_i$ in each list $i$:

$$
\text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{i} \frac{1}{k + r_i(d)}
$$

with $r_i$ 1-indexed and $k$ a constant (the original paper uses $k = 60$). Properties that fall out of the formula:

- **Scale-free.** Only positions enter, so no retriever can steamroll another regardless of its score magnitude.
- **Top-heavy.** $\frac{1}{k+1} - \frac{1}{k+2}$ is much larger than $\frac{1}{k+50} - \frac{1}{k+51}$, so moving from rank 1→2 matters far more than 50→51. Deep ranks are discounted — exactly what you want, since retrievers are only trustworthy near the top.
- **$k$ tempers the top.** Larger $k$ flattens the curve (ranks matter more uniformly); smaller $k$ sharpens the reward for being #1. $k=60$ is a mild default, not a tuned magic number.

<Callout type="info" title="A document at rank 2 and 50, vs one at rank 12 and 12">
  With $k=60$: the first scores $\frac{1}{62} + \frac{1}{110} = 0.0252$; the second scores $\frac{2}{72} = 0.0278$. RRF prefers the *consistently-good* document over the one that one system loved and the other buried — usually the right call, because a single system's rank-2 is often a fluke that the other system's rank-50 corrects.
</Callout>

## Worked trace

Two ranked lists of the same five documents (rank in parentheses):

| Doc | BM25 rank | Dense rank | RRF score ($k=60$) |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| A | 1 | 4 | $\frac{1}{61}+\frac{1}{64} = 0.0320$ |
| B | 2 | 5 | $\frac{1}{62}+\frac{1}{65} = 0.0315$ |
| C | 3 | 1 | $\frac{1}{63}+\frac{1}{61} = 0.0323$ |
| D | 4 | 2 | $\frac{1}{64}+\frac{1}{62} = 0.0318$ |
| E | 5 | 3 | $\frac{1}{65}+\frac{1}{63} = 0.0313$ |

Fused order: **C, A, D, B, E**. Document C — buried at BM25 rank 3 but loved by the dense model — takes the top spot, and D climbs from 4th to 3rd. Naive averaging of the raw scores would have left A on top and never surfaced C or D. That reshuffling *is* the value of hybrid search.

## When to reach for it

- **Any hybrid retrieval** (sparse + dense) — RRF is the default merge and needs no per-corpus tuning.
- **More than two systems** — the sum extends to any number of ranked lists (BM25 + dense + a reranker's order, etc.).
- **Ensembling the same retriever** under different query rewrites — fuse the result lists instead of picking one.

It is *not* a reranker: RRF never re-reads the documents, so it can't fix a document that both retrievers ranked poorly. Fusion improves the blend of what you already found; a cross-encoder reranker improves the *scoring* of a shortlist (see the [RAG lesson](/courses/building-with-llms/04-retrieval-augmented-generation)).

## Common mistakes

1. **Averaging raw scores** across retrievers on different scales — the fused list silently becomes the highest-magnitude retriever's list.
2. **Per-query min–max normalization** as the fix — brittle to outliers and candidate-count differences; RRF sidesteps both.
3. **Tuning $k$ obsessively** — the ranking is fairly insensitive to $k$ over a wide range; $k \approx 60$ is a fine default.
4. **Fusing scores from truncated lists** without a rank for missing documents — decide a policy (treat as rank $\infty$, i.e. contribute 0) rather than dropping the document.
