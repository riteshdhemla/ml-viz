# Newton's Method for Optimization

Newton's method minimizes a function by fitting a local quadratic model at the current point and jumping directly to its minimum. For a true quadratic this lands at the global minimum in one step. For non-quadratic functions it converges much faster than gradient descent near a minimum.

## The core idea behind Newton's method

Near a point $\mathbf{x}_0$, any smooth $f$ is approximated by the second-order Taylor expansion:

$$
f(\mathbf{x}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}_0)
+ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)
+ \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)^{\top} \mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}_0)(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)
$$

where $\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}_0) = \nabla^2 f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ is the **Hessian** (matrix of second derivatives).

Write the displacement as $\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0$. Newton's method minimizes the right-hand side over $\mathbf{s}$, ignoring the constant $f(\mathbf{x}_0)$.

## Deriving the Newton step

Take the gradient of the quadratic model with respect to $\mathbf{s}$ and set it to zero:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{s}}\!\Big[\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)^{\top}\mathbf{s}
+ \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{s}\Big]
= \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{0}
$$

Solve for $\mathbf{s}$:

$$
\mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{s} = -\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\mathbf{s} = -\mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)
$$

The Newton update is:

$$
\boxed{\;\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\;}
$$

Compare to gradient descent $\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} - \eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$: Newton replaces the scalar learning rate $\eta$ with the inverse Hessian $\mathbf{H}^{-1}$, which **rescales and rotates** the gradient to account for curvature.

<Callout type="info" title="Curvature correction">
  In a steep direction ($\lambda_i$ large), $\mathbf{H}^{-1}$ shrinks the step — preventing overshoot. In a flat direction ($\lambda_i$ small), it enlarges the step — accelerating progress. Gradient descent uses the same step size everywhere, so it struggles when the loss landscape is elongated (ill-conditioned).
</Callout>

What to notice as you step:

- **The gradient's $y$-component is zero at the origin, but the correct step's is
  $-1$.** The minimum is at $y = -1$ and the gradient has nothing to say about
  it; the Hessian's off-diagonal term is the only thing that knows $x$ and $y$
  are coupled. This is the clearest possible statement of what curvature buys.
- **One step, exactly on the optimum**, and the gradient there is exactly zero.
  For a quadratic the second-order Taylor model *is* the function, so minimizing
  the model minimizes the function.
- **The comparison step** runs gradient descent on the same function from the
  same point: 22 steps at $\eta = 0.3$, divergence at $\eta = 0.7$, and no
  $\eta$ that fixes it — the condition number is only 3 here, and real problems
  are far worse.
- **The final step is the bill**: $O(d^2)$ memory, $O(d^3)$ per solve, and steps
  that move *uphill* wherever the Hessian is not positive definite.

## A single step landing exactly on the minimum ($f(x, y) = x^2 + xy + y^2 - 3x$)

The function $f(x, y) = x^2 + xy + y^2 - 3x$ has a unique minimum at $(2, -1)$ (verified by the Hessian analysis in the multivariable optimization lesson).

**Gradient and Hessian at $(0, 0)$:**

$$
\nabla f(0,0)
= \begin{bmatrix} 2(0) + 0 - 3 \\ 0 + 2(0) \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\mathbf{H}
= \begin{bmatrix} \partial^2 f/\partial x^2 & \partial^2 f/\partial x\partial y \\
                  \partial^2 f/\partial y\partial x & \partial^2 f/\partial y^2 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

(Constant because $f$ is quadratic.)

**Invert the Hessian.** For a $2 \times 2$ matrix:

$$
\mathbf{H}^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det\mathbf{H}}
\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix},
\qquad \det\mathbf{H} = 2\cdot2 - 1\cdot1 = 3
$$

$$
\mathbf{H}^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Newton step from $(0, 0)$:**

$$
\mathbf{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
- \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
= -\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} -6 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Newton lands **exactly** on the minimum $(2, -1)$ in one step. This is the defining property: for a quadratic, the Taylor model *is* the function, so minimizing the model is the same as minimizing the function.

**Comparison to gradient descent.** A plain gradient step from $(0, 0)$ with learning rate $\eta$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \eta\begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3\eta \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

This only moves along the $x$-axis regardless of $\eta$ — it never escapes to $y = -1$. Gradient descent would require dozens of iterations (zigzagging due to the off-diagonal coupling in $\mathbf{H}$) to converge to $(2, -1)$.

```python
import numpy as np

def f(x, y):
    return x**2 + x*y + y**2 - 3*x

def grad_f(x, y):
    return np.array([2*x + y - 3, x + 2*y])

H = np.array([[2., 1.], [1., 2.]])
H_inv = np.linalg.inv(H)

# Newton step from (0, 0)
x0 = np.array([0., 0.])
g = grad_f(*x0)
x1 = x0 - H_inv @ g

print(f"Starting point:  {x0}   f = {f(*x0):.3f}")   # [0., 0.]   f = 0.000
print(f"Gradient at x0:  {g}")                          # [-3., 0.]
print(f"Newton step:    {x1}  f = {f(*x1):.3f}")      # [2., -1.]  f = -3.000
print(f"Gradient at x1: {grad_f(*x1)}")                 # [0., 0.] — we're at the minimum

# Gradient descent for comparison: many steps needed
lr = 0.1
xgd = np.array([0., 0.])
for i in range(50):
    xgd -= lr * grad_f(*xgd)
print(f"GD after 50 steps: {xgd.round(3)}  f = {f(*xgd):.3f}")   # ≈ [2., -1.]
```

## Newton vs gradient descent: iteration count and cost

| Method | Steps to reach $\|\nabla f\| < 10^{-6}$ | Cost per step |
|--------|:-----------------------------------------:|:-------------:|
| Gradient descent ($\eta = 0.1$) | $\sim 100$ | $O(d)$ |
| Newton's method | $1$ (quadratic) | $O(d^3)$ (matrix inversion) |

The $O(d^3)$ cost to compute and invert $\mathbf{H}$ is the main limitation: for a neural network with millions of parameters $d$, this is completely infeasible. Practical approximations:

- **Quasi-Newton (L-BFGS):** builds a low-rank approximation of $\mathbf{H}^{-1}$ from gradient history — $O(d \cdot m)$ per step with $m$ stored vectors.
- **Diagonal approximation (Adam, RMSProp):** keeps only the diagonal of $\mathbf{H}^{-1}$, one number per parameter — $O(d)$ per step.

<Callout type="tip" title="Adam as an approximate Newton">
  Adam maintains per-parameter running estimates of the second moment of the gradient, which approximates the diagonal of the Hessian. Dividing the gradient by $\sqrt{\text{second moment}}$ is a diagonal Newton step, which is why Adam converges faster than vanilla SGD on ill-conditioned loss surfaces.
</Callout>
