# Scaled Dot-Product Attention

**Scaled dot-product attention** is the single operation that lets every token in a sequence directly look at every other token. This page derives the $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ scaling factor from first principles and works through a complete 3-token example by hand.

## What is the formula?

$$
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
$$

where $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k}$ (queries), $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k}$ (keys), $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_v}$ (values), and $n$ is sequence length.

## What is the step-by-step procedure?

1. **Scores** — compute $QK^\top$. Entry $(i,j)$ is the dot product of query $i$ with key $j$: how relevant token $j$ is to token $i$.
2. **Scale** — divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$ to prevent large magnitudes from saturating the softmax.
3. **Softmax** — apply row-wise so each row sums to 1 (the attention weights).
4. **Weighted sum** — multiply by $V$: each output is a blend of all values, weighted by relevance.

## Why divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$?

Take query $\mathbf{q}$ and key $\mathbf{k}$ with components that are i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance 1. Their dot product is:

$$
\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i
$$

Each product $q_i k_i$ has:
- $\mathbb{E}[q_i k_i] = \mathbb{E}[q_i]\,\mathbb{E}[k_i] = 0$ (independence)
- $\operatorname{Var}(q_i k_i) = \mathbb{E}[q_i^2]\,\mathbb{E}[k_i^2] = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$

Summing $d_k$ independent unit-variance terms:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} 1 = d_k
$$

The typical score magnitude grows like $\sqrt{d_k}$. For large $d_k$ (e.g. $d_k = 64$), raw scores become large, pushing softmax into its saturated region where one weight $\approx 1$ and the rest $\approx 0$ — gradient is nearly zero there, so learning stalls.

Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ rescales the variance back to 1:

$$
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) = \frac{d_k}{d_k} = 1
$$

Empirical check: for $d_k \in \{2, 8, 64\}$, sampling 20,000 random unit-variance $\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}$ pairs gives $\operatorname{Var} \approx 2.0,\,8.0,\,64$ — matching $d_k$ exactly.

<Callout type="tip" title="The saturation intuition">
  Think of softmax as a thermostat. When all scores are moderate, the distribution is spread (the model can attend to many tokens). When one score dominates by 10×, softmax assigns ~1.0 to that entry — the model becomes a hard lookup. Large $d_k$ without scaling causes the latter by default, which is why the scale factor is not optional.
</Callout>

## Can we see the whole thing on a 3-token worked example? ($d_k = 2$)

Given projected vectors directly (each row = one token):

$$
Q = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0\\0 & 2\\2 & 2\end{bmatrix}, \quad
K = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0\\0 & 2\\1 & 1\end{bmatrix}, \quad
V = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\0 & 1\\10 & 10\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Step 1 — Scores $QK^\top$.** Entry $(i,j) = \mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{k}_j$ measures how well token $i$'s query aligns with token $j$'s key — a higher score means "pay more attention here." Row 1: $[2,0]\cdot[2,0]=4$; $[2,0]\cdot[0,2]=0$; $[2,0]\cdot[1,1]=2$:

$$
QK^\top = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 0 & 2\\0 & 4 & 2\\4 & 4 & 4\end{bmatrix}
$$

Token 1's query matches key 1 best (score 4), not at all with key 2 (score 0), and moderately with key 3 (score 2). After softmax, this will mean token 1 attends mostly to itself.

**Step 2 — Scale by $1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$.** Using $4/\sqrt{2} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.828$ and $2/\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$:

$$
\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{2}} = \begin{bmatrix}2.828 & 0 & 1.414\\0 & 2.828 & 1.414\\2.828 & 2.828 & 2.828\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Step 3 — Softmax each row.** Row 1: $e^{2.828}=16.92$, $e^0=1.00$, $e^{1.414}=4.11$, sum $= 22.03$:

$$
\text{softmax}(\text{row 1}) = \frac{1}{22.03}[16.92,\,1.00,\,4.11] = [0.768,\,0.045,\,0.187]
$$

Row 3: all three scaled scores equal ($2.828$ each), so softmax is exactly uniform $[\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3}]$. Row 2 mirrors row 1: $[0.045,\,0.768,\,0.187]$.

$$
\text{weights} = \begin{bmatrix}0.768 & 0.045 & 0.187\\0.045 & 0.768 & 0.187\\0.333 & 0.333 & 0.333\end{bmatrix}
$$

Every row sums to exactly 1.

**Step 4 — Weighted sum $\text{weights}\cdot V$.** Row 1:

$$
0.768[1,0] + 0.045[0,1] + 0.187[10,10] = [0.768 + 1.870,\; 0.045 + 1.870] = [2.638,\;1.915]
$$

Row 3 (uniform weights) = average of value rows: $\frac{1}{3}([1,0]+[0,1]+[10,10]) = [3.667,\,3.667]$.

$$
\text{output} = \begin{bmatrix}2.638 & 1.915\\1.915 & 2.638\\3.667 & 3.667\end{bmatrix}
$$

Token 1 attends mostly to key 1 (its strongest match), but the high value of token 3 still lifts its output. Token 3, matching all keys equally, returns the plain average of all values.

## How do we verify it in pure Python?

```python
import math

def softmax(row):
    e = [math.exp(s) for s in row]
    z = sum(e)
    return [x / z for x in e]

Q = [[2, 0], [0, 2], [2, 2]]
K = [[2, 0], [0, 2], [1, 1]]
V = [[1, 0], [0, 1], [10, 10]]
d_k = 2

scores  = [[sum(q[d]*k[d] for d in range(d_k)) for k in K] for q in Q]
scaled  = [[s / math.sqrt(d_k) for s in row] for row in scores]
weights = [softmax(row) for row in scaled]
out = [[sum(weights[i][j]*V[j][d] for j in range(3)) for d in range(d_k)]
       for i in range(3)]

print("scores  :", scores)                               # [[4,0,2],[0,4,2],[4,4,4]]
print("weights :", [[round(w,3) for w in r] for r in weights])
print("output  :", [[round(o,3) for o in r] for r in out])
```

## What does the PyTorch one-liner look like?

```python
import torch, torch.nn.functional as F

Q = torch.tensor([[2.,0],[0,2],[2,2]])
K = torch.tensor([[2.,0],[0,2],[1,1]])
V = torch.tensor([[1.,0],[0,1],[10,10]])

out = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(Q.unsqueeze(0), K.unsqueeze(0), V.unsqueeze(0))
print(out.squeeze(0))  # matches the hand calculation above
```
