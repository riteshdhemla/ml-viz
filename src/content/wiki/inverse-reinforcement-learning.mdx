# Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL)

<Callout type="warning" title="Advanced material">
  A graduate-level topic that builds on MDPs, policy optimization, and imitation learning. It is the conceptual foundation of the reward-modeling step in RLHF — read [From Policy Gradient to RLHF](/courses/reinforcement-learning/06-from-policy-gradient-to-rlhf) first, and the [Imitation Learning](/wiki/imitation-learning) wiki page for context.
</Callout>

Standard RL is: *given a reward function, find the optimal policy.* **Inverse RL flips the arrow**: given expert demonstrations, *recover the reward function the expert appears to be optimizing.* The motivation: rewards are often easier to learn from behavior than to specify by hand. "Drive safely" is hard to write as a number, but easy to demonstrate.

## Why recover the reward instead of the policy?

[Imitation Learning](/wiki/imitation-learning) directly copies the policy. IRL instead recovers the *reward*, which has a key advantage: a reward function is a **more transferable and compact description of intent** than a policy.

- A reward generalizes to **new environments** with different dynamics — re-run RL with the recovered reward and you get a sensible policy even where the expert never acted.
- A reward captures *why* the expert acts, not just *what* they do, so it degrades more gracefully far from demonstrated states.

## The fundamental ambiguity

IRL is **ill-posed**: many reward functions explain the same behavior. Trivially, $R(s) = 0$ everywhere makes *every* policy optimal, so it "explains" any demonstration. Less trivially, the expert's policy is invariant to:

- Adding a constant to the reward
- Scaling the reward by a positive factor
- Reward shaping with any potential function $\Phi(s)$: $R'(s,a,s') = R(s,a,s') + \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$ leaves the optimal policy unchanged

Resolving this ambiguity is what distinguishes the major IRL algorithms.

## Max-margin IRL (Ng & Russell, 2000)

The first formulation: find a reward (linear in features, $R(s) = w^\top \phi(s)$) such that the expert policy outperforms all alternatives **by the largest possible margin**.

$$
\max_{w,\, t}\; t \quad \text{s.t.}\quad w^\top \mu(\pi^*) \ge w^\top \mu(\pi) + t \quad \forall \pi
$$

where $\mu(\pi) = \mathbb{E}[\sum_t \gamma^t \phi(s_t) \mid \pi]$ is the **expected feature count** under policy $\pi$. **Apprenticeship learning** (Abbeel & Ng, 2004) iterates: find a reward that distinguishes the expert from current policies, run RL to get a new policy, repeat until the policy's feature counts match the expert's.

## Maximum Entropy IRL (Ziebart, 2008)

The most influential formulation. It resolves the ambiguity with a principled tie-breaker: among all reward functions and policies consistent with the expert's feature counts, choose the one with **maximum entropy** — the least committed, most uniform distribution over trajectories.

Under MaxEnt IRL, the probability of a trajectory $\tau$ is proportional to the exponential of its reward:

$$
P(\tau) \propto \exp\!\left(\sum_t R_w(s_t, a_t)\right)
$$

This makes the model probabilistic (the expert is assumed to be *approximately* optimal, with higher-reward trajectories exponentially more likely) and removes the degeneracy of max-margin methods. The gradient of the log-likelihood matches expert feature counts to the learner's expected feature counts:

$$
\nabla_w \mathcal{L} = \mu_{\text{expert}} - \mathbb{E}_{P(\tau)}[\mu(\tau)]
$$

## Deep / adversarial IRL

For high-dimensional problems, the reward is a neural network:

- **GCL (Guided Cost Learning):** sample-based MaxEnt IRL with neural rewards
- **AIRL (Adversarial IRL):** a GAN-style setup where a discriminator implicitly recovers a reward that is robust to changes in dynamics
- **GAIL** (Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning) is closely related — it skips explicit reward recovery and directly matches occupancy measures (see the [Imitation Learning](/wiki/imitation-learning) page)

## IRL and RLHF

RLHF for LLMs is IRL in spirit. Instead of demonstrations, it learns a reward from **human preference comparisons** ("response A is better than B"). The reward model is trained to assign higher scalar reward to preferred outputs (recovering an implicit reward function for "helpful, harmless" behavior) and PPO then optimizes the policy against it. The reward-ambiguity and reward-hacking concerns from classical IRL reappear directly: an LLM policy will exploit any flaw in the learned reward, which is why RLHF uses a KL penalty to keep the policy close to the reference model.

## When to use IRL

- The reward is hard to specify but easy to demonstrate (driving style, surgical technique)
- You need the policy to transfer to new dynamics or environments
- You want an interpretable model of *intent*, not just behavior
