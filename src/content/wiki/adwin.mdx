# ADWIN: Adaptive Windowing for Drift Detection

**ADWIN** (ADaptive WINdowing; Bifet & Gavaldà, 2007) solves two problems at once on a stream of real values — typically a model's error signal. It **detects change** (concept drift), and it **maintains a window of exactly the right length**: as long as the data looks stationary the window grows; the moment the mean shifts, it shrinks to just the recent, homogeneous part. That recovered window length is itself an estimate of "how far back the current concept extends."

## The core idea: test every split of the window

ADWIN keeps a window $W$ of the most recent stream values. On each new element it asks:

> Is there a way to cut $W$ into an older sub-window $W_0$ and a newer sub-window $W_1$ such that their sample means are *distinguishably* different?

If yes, the older data belongs to a stale concept, so ADWIN **drops $W_0$** and reports drift. Formally, it declares a change at a split when

$$
\big|\hat\mu_{W_0} - \hat\mu_{W_1}\big| \;>\; \epsilon_{\text{cut}},
\qquad
\epsilon_{\text{cut}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2m}\,\ln\frac{4\,|W|}{\delta}},
\qquad
m = \frac{1}{\tfrac{1}{|W_0|} + \tfrac{1}{|W_1|}},
$$

where $m$ is the **harmonic mean** of the two sub-window sizes and $\delta \in (0,1)$ is the confidence parameter. The $|W|$ inside the log is a union bound over the (many) possible cut points; the harmonic mean makes the threshold large when *either* side is small (little evidence) and small only when *both* sides are big.

<details>
<summary>Hoeffding vs Bernstein cut</summary>

The bound above is the Hoeffding form used in the lesson. The published ADWIN uses a slightly tighter **Bernstein**-style cut that folds in the observed variance $\hat\sigma^2_W$:

$$
\epsilon_{\text{cut}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{m}\,\hat\sigma^2_W\,\ln\frac{2}{\delta'}} + \frac{2}{3m}\ln\frac{2}{\delta'},\qquad \delta' = \frac{\delta}{|W|}.
$$

For low-variance error streams this detects a bit sooner; the qualitative behaviour is identical.
</details>

## Why the window length is the payoff

Most detectors only raise a boolean alarm. ADWIN additionally *rightsizes* its window: after a cut it retains only $W_1$, so downstream statistics (a running mean, a model refit on "the current window") automatically use only in-distribution data. There is no separate window-length hyperparameter to tune — the data sets it. This is why ADWIN is often wired directly into learners (e.g. as the change detector inside a **Hoeffding Adaptive Tree**, which grows an alternate subtree when a node's ADWIN fires).

## Making it cheap: exponential histograms

Re-scanning every possible split of a length-$|W|$ window on every element would cost $O(|W|)$ per step and $O(|W|)$ memory. ADWIN2 compresses $W$ into an **exponential histogram** of buckets — the same power-of-two bucket structure as [DGIM](/wiki/dgim-sliding-window) — storing per-bucket sums and counts with relative accuracy. This gives:

- **Memory:** $O(\log W)$ instead of $O(W)$.
- **Time:** only $O(\log W)$ candidate split points (the bucket boundaries) are tested per update, not all $|W|-1$.

The approximation of using bucket boundaries as the only cut points costs a controllable constant factor and preserves the guarantees below.

## Guarantees

For a suitable choice of the confidence $\delta$, ADWIN comes with two-sided rigor:

1. **Few false positives.** If the mean is truly constant across $W$, the probability that ADWIN wrongly shrinks the window is at most $\delta$.
2. **Reliable detection.** If the mean changes by more than roughly $\epsilon_{\text{cut}}$, ADWIN detects it and shrinks the window, with high probability, within a delay that scales with how large the change is (bigger jumps are caught faster).

The knob is $\delta$: **larger $\delta$ ⟹ smaller $\epsilon_{\text{cut}}$ ⟹ faster detection but more false alarms**; smaller $\delta$ is conservative. This is the universal detection-delay / false-alarm trade-off.

## Worked cut calculation

Suppose the current window has $|W| = 200$ error bits, and we test the balanced split $|W_0| = |W_1| = 100$. Recent history shifted, so the older half averages $\hat\mu_{W_0} = 0.08$ and the newer half $\hat\mu_{W_1} = 0.42$. With $\delta = 0.05$:

$$
m = \frac{1}{\tfrac{1}{100} + \tfrac{1}{100}} = 50,\qquad
\epsilon_{\text{cut}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2\cdot 50}\,\ln\frac{4\cdot 200}{0.05}}
= \sqrt{0.01 \cdot \ln(16000)} = \sqrt{0.01 \cdot 9.68} \approx 0.311.
$$

The observed gap $|\,0.08 - 0.42\,| = 0.34 > 0.311$, so the test **fires**: ADWIN drops $W_0$ and reports drift. Had the gap been the stationary-noise level (say $0.03$), no split would exceed $\epsilon_{\text{cut}}$ and the window would keep growing. Note the threshold falls as the window grows ($m$ rises), so a change too subtle to catch now becomes detectable once more post-change data accumulates.

**What to notice:**

- **Frame 2 — the threshold is a U.** Plotting $\epsilon_{\text{cut}}$ against every candidate split shows why the harmonic mean matters: the threshold is enormous at both ends, where one sub-window is a handful of bits, and lowest in the middle. A change near the edge of the window is structurally harder to see than one in the middle.
- **Frames 3–4 — delay is evidence accumulation, not sluggishness.** ADWIN cannot fire right after the change because the newer sub-window is small, so $m$ is small and its threshold is huge. The larger jump (0.10 → 0.45) is caught in 56 steps and the smaller one (0.45 → 0.18) in 66.
- **Frame 5 — the honest version of "no hyperparameter".** ADWIN does *not* beat every fixed window: a fixed $W=100$ scores marginally better on this stream. What ADWIN does is land within 4% of the best fixed width without being told what it is, while the spread across plausible widths is 2×. On a live stream you cannot identify the best width — that requires the ground truth you are estimating.
- **Frame 6 — the δ trade-off is one-sided.** Sweeping δ over five orders of magnitude, detection delay grows 2.4× while false alarms stay at **zero across 300,000 stationary steps, even at δ = 0.5**. δ sits inside a logarithm inside a square root, so the Hoeffding threshold is far more conservative than the nominal δ implies.

That last point qualifies the "larger δ ⟹ faster detection but more false alarms" rule above. The direction is right, but on streams of this length the false-alarm half is unobservable, so the practical advice inverts: **pick δ loose**, because tightening it buys a reduction you cannot measure and costs detection delay you can. δ is an *upper* bound on the false-alarm probability, and nothing promises it is tight.

## Gotchas

- **δ is a rate, not a magnitude.** It controls false-alarm probability, not the size of change you care about; pick it for your tolerance to spurious resets.
- **It monitors a scalar.** ADWIN watches one real-valued signal (an error rate, a residual). For multivariate drift, run one detector per signal or feed it a scalar summary.
- **Recurring concepts.** After a reset ADWIN forgets the old window, so genuinely recurring concepts are re-learned rather than recalled — pair it with an ensemble if recurrence matters.
