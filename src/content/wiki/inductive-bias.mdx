# Inductive Bias & Equivariance

The [hypothesis-space stage](/wiki/ml-project-loop) of the ML loop asks: *which family
of functions can the model express?* Architecture is how you answer that, and the
architectures look like four unrelated topics only until you see the single idea
underneath. **Match the model's built-in symmetry to the symmetry in the data.** Do
that and four topics collapse into one, with four instantiations.

## Inductive bias, precisely

An **inductive bias** is any assumption a model makes that isn't in the data — it's
what lets a finite training set pick out a function over infinite unseen inputs.
Without a bias, learning is impossible (the [no free lunch](/wiki/vc-dimension) idea):
every function that fits the training data equally is equally preferred.

The most powerful biases in deep learning are **symmetries** — transformations of the
input that *shouldn't* change the answer (or should change it in a predictable way).

## Equivariance vs invariance

For a transformation $g$ (a shift, a permutation, a rotation) and a layer $f$:

- **Invariance:** $f(g \cdot x) = f(x)$ — the output ignores the transformation.
  (An image *classifier* should be shift-**invariant**: a shifted cat is still a cat.)
- **Equivariance:** $f(g \cdot x) = g \cdot f(x)$ — the output transforms *the same way*
  the input did. (A convolution is shift-**equivariant**: shift the image and every
  feature shifts by the same amount.)

$$
f(g \cdot x) = g \cdot f(x) \quad \text{(equivariance)}
\qquad\qquad
f(g \cdot x) = f(x) \quad \text{(invariance)}
$$

Deep nets are built from **equivariant** layers (convolutions, attention, message
passing) and reach **invariance** only at the end, via pooling or a readout. Keeping
equivariance through the depth is what preserves spatial/structural information.

## One idea, four architectures

| Architecture | Symmetry it assumes | Equivariant to | What it shares (the "tied weights") |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CNN** | translation | shifting the input | the same kernel slides over every position |
| **RNN** | time-translation | shifting in time | the same recurrent weights at every step |
| **Transformer** | permutation | reordering the tokens | the same attention applied to every pair |
| **GNN** | permutation over a graph | relabelling the nodes | the same message function on every edge |

The shared column is the point: an inductive bias is almost always **weight sharing
across a symmetry group**. That sharing is what buys **sample efficiency** — the model
learns a pattern once and reuses it everywhere the symmetry says it should, instead of
re-learning "edge detector" independently in all $224 \times 224$ positions.

<Callout type="info" title="Why this makes attention feel inevitable">
A transformer assumes **only** permutation symmetry — the weakest of the four. That is
exactly why it needs **positional encodings**: pure permutation-equivariance throws away
order, so you *inject* position back as a signal. Read attention as "remove the strong
locality bias of convolution, keep only permutation symmetry, and add position as data."
It generalises the others because it assumes the least, and pays for that generality
with data.
</Callout>

## The bias/data trade-off

A stronger, correct bias needs less data; a weaker bias needs more but bends to more
problems. That is the same axis as [bias–variance](/wiki/bias-variance-decomposition),
one level up — architectural bias instead of estimator bias.

- **Right bias, small data → wins.** A CNN beats an MLP on images in the small-data
  regime *because* translation equivariance is true of images and the MLP must learn it
  from scratch.
- **Wrong bias → it breaks.** Impose a bias the data doesn't have and you cap accuracy
  no matter how much data you add. A CNN on tabular data assumes a spatial locality that
  isn't there; a plain RNN assumes recency matters most and starves long-range
  dependencies (the vanishing-gradient story).
- **No bias, huge data → also wins, eventually.** With enough data and compute a
  low-bias transformer overtakes a high-bias CNN even on images (ViT) — it *learns* the
  locality the CNN was born with. The bias was a data-efficiency shortcut, not a ceiling.

This is the moving-bottleneck story of the [ML loop](/wiki/ml-project-loop) seen from the
hypothesis-space slot: as data and compute grew, the field could afford weaker biases.
