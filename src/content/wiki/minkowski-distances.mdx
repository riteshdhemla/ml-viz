# Minkowski Distance Family

Every algorithm that relies on "nearness" — KNN, K-Means, hierarchical clustering, kernel methods — needs a formula for distance. The **Minkowski family** unifies the most common metrics under a single parameter $p$, letting you interpolate between Manhattan and Euclidean and beyond.

**The questions:**

1. What are the four standard metrics?
2. What is the master Minkowski formula and its special cases?
3. What do all the metrics give on a worked example?
4. How does the parameter $p$ change the geometry?
5. How do we compute and compare every metric in Python?
6. What about categorical features — the Hamming distance?

---

## Q1 — What are the four standard metrics?

| Metric | Formula | Typical use |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| Euclidean | $\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - y_i)^2}$ | Default for continuous features |
| Manhattan | $\sum_i \lvert x_i - y_i\rvert$ | High-dimensional, sparse, or grid-like data |
| Minkowski ($p$) | $\left(\sum_i \lvert x_i - y_i\rvert^p\right)^{1/p}$ | Generalizes both; choose $p$ via CV |
| Chebyshev | $\max_i \lvert x_i - y_i\rvert$ | When the largest gap dominates |

---

## Q2 — What is the master Minkowski formula and its special cases?

$$
d_p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{d} \lvert x_i - y_i\rvert^p\right)^{1/p}
$$

Special cases:
- $p = 1$: **Manhattan** — sum the absolute gaps; like walking a city grid, no diagonals.
- $p = 2$: **Euclidean** — square the gaps, sum, square-root; the ruler distance.
- $p \to \infty$: **Chebyshev** — only the single largest coordinate gap survives, since $(\sum a_i^p)^{1/p} \to \max_i a_i$.

---

## Q3 — What do all the metrics give on a worked example?

Take $(1, 2, 3)$ to $(4, 6, 3)$.

Per-coordinate absolute gaps:

$$
\lvert x_1 - y_1\rvert = \lvert 1 - 4\rvert = 3, \quad
\lvert x_2 - y_2\rvert = \lvert 2 - 6\rvert = 4, \quad
\lvert x_3 - y_3\rvert = \lvert 3 - 3\rvert = 0
$$

Feed gaps $(3, 4, 0)$ into each formula:

**Manhattan ($p = 1$):** add the gaps.

$$
d_1 = 3 + 4 + 0 = 7
$$

**Euclidean ($p = 2$):** square, sum, square-root.

$$
d_2 = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 0^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5
$$

**Minkowski ($p = 3$):** cube the gaps, sum, take the cube root.

$$
d_3 = \left(3^3 + 4^3 + 0^3\right)^{1/3} = (27 + 64)^{1/3} = 91^{1/3} \approx 4.50
$$

**Chebyshev ($p \to \infty$):** the maximum gap wins.

$$
d_\infty = \max(3, 4, 0) = 4
$$

**Ordering:** $d_1 = 7 \ge d_2 = 5 \ge d_3 \approx 4.50 \ge d_\infty = 4$. Larger $p$ lets the single biggest gap dominate while smaller gaps fade, so the distance decreases toward $\max(3, 4, 0) = 4$ as $p \to \infty$.

---

## Q4 — How does the parameter p change the geometry?

The **unit ball** — the set of all points at distance 1 from the origin — changes shape with $p$:

| $p$ | Unit ball shape | KNN neighborhood |
|-----|----------------|------------------|
| 1 | Diamond (square rotated 45°) | Axis-aligned diamond; gaps in each coordinate count separately |
| 2 | Circle / sphere | Isotropic; most natural for continuous data without strong axes |
| $\infty$ | Square / hypercube | Square neighborhood; dominated by the single largest gap |

As $p$ increases from 1 to $\infty$, the ball transitions from diamond to circle to square. Features with the largest individual gap gain more influence relative to the rest.

<Callout type="warning" title="Feature scaling is critical">
  All Minkowski metrics treat coordinate magnitudes as meaningful. A feature measured in millimeters (range 0–10 000) completely overwhelms one in meters (range 0–10). Always normalize or standardize before computing distances.
</Callout>

## Q5 — How do we compute and compare every metric in Python?

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.array([1, 2, 3], dtype=float)
y = np.array([4, 6, 3], dtype=float)
gaps = np.abs(x - y)  # [3, 4, 0]

d1   = gaps.sum()                          # Manhattan:   7.0
d2   = np.sqrt((gaps**2).sum())            # Euclidean:   5.0
d3   = (gaps**3).sum() ** (1/3)            # Minkowski p=3: ~4.497
dinf = gaps.max()                          # Chebyshev:   4.0

print(f"Manhattan  (p=1):   {d1:.4f}")
print(f"Euclidean  (p=2):   {d2:.4f}")
print(f"Minkowski  (p=3):   {d3:.4f}")
print(f"Chebyshev  (p→∞):   {dinf:.4f}")

# Verify limit: as p grows, Minkowski → Chebyshev
for p in [1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 50, 100]:
    dp = (gaps**p).sum() ** (1/p)
    print(f"  p={p:3d}: d_p = {dp:.6f}  (Chebyshev = {dinf:.6f})")
```

## Q6 — What about categorical features — the Hamming distance?

For features that are categories rather than numbers, Euclidean distance is meaningless. The **Hamming distance** counts the fraction of positions where the two vectors disagree:

$$
d_H(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^d \mathbf{1}[x_i \neq y_i]
$$

A Hamming distance of 0 means the vectors are identical; a distance of 1 means they differ in every coordinate.
