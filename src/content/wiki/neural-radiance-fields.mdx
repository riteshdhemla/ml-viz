# Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)

<Callout type="warning" title="Advanced material">
  A specialized 3D-vision topic at the intersection of graphics and deep learning. It assumes comfort with MLPs, gradients, and basic 3D geometry. This goes beyond the core 2D computer-vision curriculum.
</Callout>

NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2020) solves **novel-view synthesis**: given a sparse set of photos of a scene from known camera positions, generate photorealistic images from *new*, unseen viewpoints. The radical idea is to represent the entire 3D scene not as a mesh or voxel grid, but as the **weights of a single small neural network**.

## The scene as a function

A NeRF is a function that maps a 3D point and viewing direction to color and density:

$$
F_\Theta : (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)
$$

- $\mathbf{x} = (x, y, z)$ — a 3D location in the scene
- $\mathbf{d} = (\theta, \phi)$ — the viewing direction (a unit vector)
- $\mathbf{c} = (r, g, b)$ — the emitted color at that point, seen from that direction
- $\sigma$ — the **volume density** (differential opacity): how much light is blocked at that point. $\sigma \to \infty$ for solid surfaces, $\sigma = 0$ for empty air

The network $F_\Theta$ is just an MLP (~8 layers). Density depends only on position (geometry is view-independent); color depends on both position and direction (so the model can capture specular highlights that change with viewpoint).

## Volume rendering — making it differentiable

To render a pixel, cast a camera ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$ from the camera through that pixel and accumulate color along it. The expected color is the classic volume-rendering integral:

$$
C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{d})\, dt
$$
$$
T(t) = \exp\!\left(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\, ds\right)
$$

- $T(t)$ is **transmittance** — the probability that the ray travels from the near bound $t_n$ to $t$ without being blocked. It down-weights points hidden behind opaque surfaces.
- In practice the integral is approximated by **stratified sampling**: partition $[t_n, t_f]$ into $N$ bins, draw one sample per bin, and use a discrete quadrature sum.

Because the whole rendering pipeline is differentiable, NeRF is trained by simple gradient descent: render the rays for the training images, compare to the ground-truth pixel colors with an MSE loss, and backpropagate into $\Theta$.

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} \| C(\mathbf{r}) - C_{\text{gt}}(\mathbf{r}) \|^2
$$

No 3D supervision is needed — only 2D photos and their camera poses.

## Positional encoding — the key trick

An MLP fed raw $(x, y, z)$ coordinates produces blurry results: networks are biased toward low-frequency functions and can't represent sharp geometric/texture detail. NeRF fixes this by mapping each coordinate through a high-frequency **positional encoding** before the MLP:

$$
\gamma(p) = \bigl(\sin(2^0\pi p), \cos(2^0\pi p), \ldots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi p), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi p)\bigr)
$$

This lifts the inputs into a high-dimensional space where the MLP can fit high-frequency detail. (This is the same Fourier-feature idea as transformer positional encodings — see [Multi-Head & Positional Encoding](/courses/transformers/02-multi-head-and-positional).) It is the single most important component for sharp NeRF reconstructions.

## Hierarchical sampling

Uniformly sampling along each ray wastes computation on empty space. NeRF trains two networks:

1. A **coarse** network sampled uniformly, whose densities reveal where the surface likely is
2. A **fine** network that samples more densely near the high-density regions found by the coarse pass (importance sampling)

## Strengths and limitations

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Photorealistic novel views | Slow to train (hours per scene) |
| Tiny storage (~5 MB of weights vs GBs of voxels) | Slow to render (many MLP queries per ray) |
| Continuous, no discretization artifacts | One network per scene — no generalization |
| Only needs 2D images + poses | Struggles with reflective/transparent surfaces, dynamic scenes |

## The follow-up explosion

NeRF spawned a huge research line addressing its weaknesses:

- **Instant-NGP** — hash-grid encoding cuts training from hours to seconds
- **Mip-NeRF** — anti-aliasing across scales
- **Dynamic / 4D NeRFs** — scenes that change over time
- **3D Gaussian Splatting** (2023) — replaces the MLP+ray-marching with explicit anisotropic Gaussians, achieving real-time rendering and largely superseding vanilla NeRF for many applications

## Why it matters

NeRF reframed 3D reconstruction as **coordinate-based representation learning** — encode a continuous signal in network weights and query it differentiably. That "neural field" paradigm now extends well beyond rendering, to signed-distance functions, physics simulation, and compression.
