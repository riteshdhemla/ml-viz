# The Baum–Welch Algorithm

When you have observation sequences but *no* hidden labels, **Baum–Welch** (an instance of the [EM algorithm](/courses/probabilistic-models/02-em-algorithm)) estimates the HMM parameters $\pi$ (initial distribution), $A$ (transition matrix), and $B$ (emission matrix).

The loop alternates between two steps until the log-likelihood converges:

- **E-step** — given current parameters, compute how *responsible* each hidden state (and each transition) is for the observed data.
- **M-step** — re-estimate $\pi, A, B$ as normalized expected counts.

## Notation and key quantities

An HMM with $N$ hidden states emits an observation sequence $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_T)$. The **forward variable** from the forward algorithm captures all information up to and including time $t$:

$$
\alpha_t(i) = P(x_1, \ldots, x_t,\, z_t = i)
$$

Its value: the product $\alpha_t(i)\,\beta_t(i)$ (defined below) equals $P(\mathbf{x},\, z_t = i)$ — the joint probability of the full observation sequence and being in state $i$ at time $t$. Normalizing across states $\sum_k \alpha_t(k)\beta_t(k) = P(\mathbf{x})$ gives the state occupancy $\gamma_t(i) = P(z_t=i \mid \mathbf{x})$. Baum–Welch additionally needs a **backward pass**.

## The backward pass

The E-step starts by running a recursion backward through the sequence.

Define $\beta_t(i) = P(x_{t+1}, \ldots, x_T \mid z_t = i)$ — the probability of the *future* observations given the current state:

$$
\beta_T(i) = 1 \quad \text{(base case)}
$$

$$
\beta_t(i) = \sum_{j=1}^N A_{ij}\, b_j(x_{t+1})\, \beta_{t+1}(j)
$$

The recursion runs backwards from $t = T$ to $t = 1$: each state's backward value is a sum over where it could transition next, weighted by the emission probability of the next observation.

## Computing the state occupancy γ

Combine forward and backward to get the probability of being in state $i$ at time $t$, given the *whole* sequence:

$$
\gamma_t(i) = P(z_t = i \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\alpha_t(i)\,\beta_t(i)}{\sum_{k=1}^N \alpha_t(k)\,\beta_t(k)}
$$

The numerator combines everything before-and-including $t$ ($\alpha$) with everything after $t$ ($\beta$); the denominator normalizes over all states.

## Computing the transition counts ξ

We also need the expected transitions, not just the per-state occupancies.

The probability of transitioning $i \to j$ at time $t$:

$$
\xi_t(i,j) = P(z_t=i,\, z_{t+1}=j \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\alpha_t(i)\, A_{ij}\, b_j(x_{t+1})\, \beta_{t+1}(j)}{\sum_{k,l} \alpha_t(k)\, A_{kl}\, b_l(x_{t+1})\, \beta_{t+1}(l)}
$$

Read the numerator left to right: arrive at state $i$ by time $t$, take the transition to $j$, emit the next observation from $j$, then account for the rest of the sequence.

## The M-step parameter re-estimation

With the expected counts in hand, re-estimate every parameter as a ratio of expected counts:

$$
\hat{\pi}_i = \gamma_1(i), \qquad
\hat{A}_{ij} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \xi_t(i,j)}{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \gamma_t(i)}, \qquad
\hat{b}_j(k) = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t:\, x_t = k} \gamma_t(j)}{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^T \gamma_t(j)}
$$

In words:

- $\hat{\pi}_i$ — expected fraction of sequences starting in state $i$
- $\hat{A}_{ij}$ — expected number of $i \to j$ transitions, divided by expected number of times in state $i$
- $\hat{b}_j(k)$ — expected number of times state $j$ emitted symbol $k$, divided by expected time in state $j$

enough that every $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ and $\xi$ fits on one screen:

What to notice as you step:

- **Why there are two passes.** $\alpha_t(i)$ knows only the past,
  $\beta_t(i)$ only the future. Neither is a posterior on its own; the product,
  normalized, is. Watch $\gamma_3$ swing to Sun on the dry third day even though
  the transition matrix prefers staying put — evidence from the *end* of the
  sequence reaching backwards.
- **Why $\xi$ exists at all.** $\gamma$ tells you where the chain was;
  re-estimating $A$ needs to know where it *went*. That is a different posterior
  and needs its own quantity.
- **The M-step is just counting.** Every re-estimate is expected-count over
  expected-total — precisely the maximum-likelihood estimator you would write
  down if the hidden states had been observed.
- **The final step iterates to convergence.** $\log P(\mathbf{x})$ rises every
  time, and $A$ and $B$ sharpen toward 0/1 — a single 3-step sequence cannot
  support a 2-state HMM, so the model overfits it exactly. Real training sums
  expected counts across many sequences before normalizing.

## Connection to Gaussian Mixture Models

<Callout type="tip" title="It's GMMs with a Markov chain">
  This is the same E-step/M-step loop as [Gaussian Mixture Models](/courses/probabilistic-models/01-gaussian-mixture-models) — HMMs are essentially "GMMs with a Markov chain tying the components across time." γ plays the role of the GMM responsibilities; the M-step is the same weighted-count normalization.
</Callout>

## Convergence guarantees and pitfalls

- Each iteration **provably increases** the likelihood (the standard EM guarantee), but only to a **local** optimum. Use multiple random restarts.
- Multiplying many probabilities underflows fast; real implementations run all recursions in **log space** (or rescale $\alpha_t$, $\beta_t$ at each step). `hmmlearn` handles this for you.
- With multiple training sequences, sum the expected counts across sequences before normalizing.
