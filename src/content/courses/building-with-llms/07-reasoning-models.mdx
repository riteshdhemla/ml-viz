# Reasoning Models

Standard LLMs predict the next token given their context. They do a fixed amount of computation per token, which means a one-step multiplication and a twenty-step proof look equally hard from the model's perspective. **Reasoning models** break that constraint: they spend extra compute *thinking* before answering, and they are trained to exploit it. The result is dramatically better performance on tasks requiring multi-step logic, math, and code.

**Real-world examples:** OpenAI o1/o3, DeepSeek-R1, Google Gemini Flash Thinking, and Qwen QwQ. All are trained on top of strong base LLMs but with an additional reasoning-focused post-training stage.

## How standard CoT differs from reasoning models

[Chain-of-thought prompting](/courses/building-with-llms/02-chain-of-thought) asks a base model to produce intermediate steps before its final answer. This helps, but the model was not *trained* to produce those steps reliably — it just completes a pattern.

Reasoning models internalize CoT: they are explicitly trained to generate long reasoning traces before committing to an answer. The traces may be:
- **Visible** (DeepSeek-R1 outputs `<think>...</think>` blocks before the answer)
- **Hidden** (o1/o3 run an internal "thinking" pass that users don't see)

Either way, the model allocates more forward passes, and more tokens, to hard problems than to easy ones.

<Callout type="tip" title="Same architecture, different training">
  Reasoning models use the same decoder-only Transformer architecture as base LLMs. The difference is entirely in how they are trained after pretraining: RL that rewards correct final answers over long reasoning chains.
</Callout>

## Train-time compute scaling: RL on reasoning traces

The training recipe for reasoning models uses **reinforcement learning** to teach the model to reason:

1. **Generate** several candidate reasoning chains + final answers for each problem.
2. **Verify** the final answer against a ground-truth checker (math answer, unit-test pass, or a reward model for open-ended problems).
3. **Reinforce** chains that reached the correct answer; penalize those that didn't.

The model learns (entirely through trial and error) that longer, more deliberate chains lead to better answers. No human-written reasoning traces are required; the reward signal comes from verifying the *end result*.

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}} = -\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\big[R(\tau)\big]
\quad \text{where } R(\tau) = \mathbf{1}[\text{final answer is correct}]
$$

Popular algorithms for this step include **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization, used by DeepSeek) and **PPO** (used by OpenAI o1). Both keep the model close to a reference policy to prevent reward hacking.

**Scaling law at train time:** running RL for more steps lets the model discover more effective reasoning strategies. In the DeepSeek-R1 paper, model accuracy on competition math benchmarks rises near-monotonically with RL training duration — longer training = longer and better reasoning traces at inference.

## Test-time compute scaling: budget forcing

Once a reasoning model is trained, you can control how much it thinks at inference time. The technique is called **budget forcing**: inject keywords or constraints that tell the model to think more or less.

**"Wait" trick:** appending the string `"Wait"` to the model's partial reasoning trace nudges it to pause and reconsider, often catching errors before committing. This can be done multiple times.

```text
[Problem] Prove that √2 is irrational.

[Model thinks...]
Assume √2 = p/q in lowest terms. Then 2 = p²/q², so p² = 2q²...
Wait, let me check the parity argument more carefully.
If p² is even then p must be even — write p = 2k...
[continues longer reasoning chain]
[Answer] √2 is irrational because any representation p/q leads to a contradiction.
```

**Scaling curve:** empirically, reasoning accuracy improves with more thinking tokens, but with diminishing returns. There is an optimal compute budget per problem difficulty; hard competition problems benefit from much longer chains than grade-school arithmetic.

### Sampling more, and the verifier that decides what it is worth

Budget forcing spends compute *inside* one answer. The other way to spend it is **across** answers: sample $N$ attempts and pick the best. That makes the diminishing-returns story concrete, because the two halves separate cleanly.

<TestTimeComputeViz />

$\text{pass@}N = 1 - (1-p)^N$ is the chance a correct answer is somewhere in the batch — what a *perfect* verifier would score, and a hard ceiling on any way of choosing among the same samples. A model right 35% of the time has a correct answer in 16 samples 99.9% of the time. Whether you can **find** it is a separate problem, and it is the one that decides the payoff.

**The verifier sets how much of the ceiling you collect.** At $N = 16$ a near-perfect verifier collects all 0.999 of it; one with noise 1.0 collects 75.9%; one with noise 3.0 collects 50.0%. The sampling is identical in all three cases.

**So returns to more compute are non-monotone in verifier quality.** Going from 16 to 128 samples (8× the spend) buys **+0.001** with a near-perfect verifier (already saturated against the ceiling), **+0.130** with a mediocre one, and **+0.096** with a bad one. Both extremes waste the extra budget, for opposite reasons.

**And that reframes the train-vs-test-time choice as a bet on selection.** Best-of-16 on the 35% model with a good verifier scores 0.953 — equivalent to owning a 95.3%-single-shot model. With a poor verifier the same 16× spend scores 0.500. Same sampler, same compute, completely different substitution rate against simply training a better model. Measure your verifier against pass@N before budgeting for samples; that ratio, not the sample count, is what to improve.

<Callout type="info" title="Train-time vs test-time scaling">
  | | Train-time | Test-time |
  |---|---|---|
  | What is scaled | RL training steps | Tokens generated before answering |
  | Effect | Model learns better strategies | Model applies strategies more carefully |
  | Cost | One-time (training) | Per-query (inference) |
  | Control | Fixed at deployment | Adjustable per request |
</Callout>

## DeepSeek-R1: a reference implementation

DeepSeek-R1 (2025) is the most transparent publicly documented reasoning model. Key facts:

- **Architecture:** decoder-only Transformer (same class as LLaMA/GPT)
- **Training:** pretraining → SFT → GRPO-based RL with verifiable reward signals
- **Reasoning traces:** explicitly output between `<think>` and `</think>` tags; the final answer follows after `</think>`
- **Emergent behaviour:** the RL stage produced spontaneous "aha moments" — the model learned to revisit and correct its own earlier steps without being told to
- **Open weights:** DeepSeek released both the 671 B MoE model and smaller distilled variants (7 B, 14 B, 70 B parameters)

```text
Example output format:
<think>
  Let me break this into cases...
  Case 1: if n is even, then...
  Wait, that doesn't work for n=2. Let me reconsider.
  Actually, the key insight is...
</think>
The answer is 42, because [brief explanation].
```

## When to use a reasoning model

Reasoning models are not universally better. They are slower and more expensive per query. Choose them when:

✅ **Hard, verifiable tasks** — math olympiad problems, competitive programming, formal proofs, scientific calculations where correctness is binary and checkable

✅ **Multi-step planning** — tasks requiring correct intermediate state (e.g. agentic coding that spans many files)

✅ **Low latency tolerance** — the user or system can wait several seconds or more for a better answer

❌ **High-volume, latency-sensitive tasks** — classification, extraction, summarisation of a single document: standard LLMs or even small fine-tuned models are cheaper and fast enough

❌ **Tasks requiring real-time streaming** — reasoning models often hold back output until thinking is complete

❌ **Creative generation** — longer thinking rarely helps prose, dialogue, or style

<Callout type="warning" title="Thinking tokens cost money">
  A reasoning model might generate 2,000–10,000 thinking tokens before outputting 200 answer tokens. At typical API prices, this is 10–50× more expensive than a standard completion of the same length. Always measure whether the quality uplift is worth the cost for your specific task.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

1. **Using a reasoning model for simple tasks**: calling o1 to classify a sentiment or extract a date is wasteful; a standard model or even a smaller fine-tuned model does it just as well for a fraction of the cost.
2. **Prompting like a base LLM**: don't tell a reasoning model to "think step by step"; it already does. Adding CoT triggers can *interfere* with its internal reasoning strategy.
3. **Ignoring the thinking trace**: for debugging, the `<think>` trace (when visible) is the best diagnostic. If the reasoning is sound but the answer is wrong, the issue is in the final-step extraction. If the reasoning itself is flawed, the problem is the model's knowledge or strategy.
4. **Equating reasoning length with quality**: longer traces are generally better, but a model can also ramble. Evaluate the final answer, not the token count.

## Key takeaways

- Reasoning models are standard LLMs post-trained with **RL on verifiable outcomes** — they learn to generate useful CoT traces without explicit trace supervision.
- **Train-time scaling** (more RL steps) and **test-time scaling** (more thinking tokens) both improve accuracy, and they compose.
- **Budget forcing** (e.g. the "Wait" trick) is a simple way to give the model more test-time compute without retraining.
- DeepSeek-R1 is the most documented open example: GRPO-based RL, `<think>` tags, emergent self-correction.
- Use reasoning models for **hard, verifiable, low-throughput tasks**; fall back to standard LLMs for speed and cost.
