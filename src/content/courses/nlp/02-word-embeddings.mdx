# Word Embeddings

One-hot vectors tell the model nothing about word meaning — "cat" and "feline" have orthogonal representations despite being synonyms. **Dense word embeddings** map each word to a point in a continuous vector space where semantically similar words are geometrically close.

## Word2Vec

Word2Vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) learns embeddings by training a shallow neural network to predict context from words or words from context. The key insight: **a word's meaning can be inferred from the company it keeps**.

### Skip-gram

**Task:** given a center word, predict its surrounding context words.

For the sentence "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog":
- Center word: "fox", window size $w=2$
- Predict: ["quick", "brown", "jumps", "over"]

The model maximizes:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{-w \leq j \leq w, j \neq 0} \log p(w_{t+j} \mid w_t)
$$

with a softmax over the full vocabulary:

$$
p(w_O \mid w_I) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{v}_{w_O}^{\prime\top} \mathbf{v}_{w_I})}{\sum_{w=1}^V \exp(\mathbf{v}_w^{\prime\top} \mathbf{v}_{w_I})}
$$

where $\mathbf{v}_w$ is the input embedding and $\mathbf{v}_w'$ is the output embedding.

**Negative sampling** approximates the expensive softmax: instead of normalizing over $|V|$ words, sample $k$ random "noise" words and train a binary classifier:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NS}} = \log \sigma(\mathbf{v}_{w_O}^{\prime\top} \mathbf{v}_{w_I}) + \sum_{i=1}^k \mathbb{E}_{w_i \sim P_n}\left[\log \sigma(-\mathbf{v}_{w_i}^{\prime\top} \mathbf{v}_{w_I})\right]
$$

### CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words)

**Task:** given the context words, predict the center word. Cheaper to train than skip-gram; better for frequent words. Skip-gram is better for rare words.

| Model | Predicts | Better for |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| **Skip-gram** | Context from center | Rare words, small data |
| **CBOW** | Center from context | Frequent words, fast training |

### Semantic analogies

The famous "king – man + woman = queen" analogy works because Word2Vec embeddings encode semantic relationships as vector offsets:

$$
\mathbf{v}_{\text{king}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{man}} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{woman}} \approx \mathbf{v}_{\text{queen}}
$$

This holds for many relationships: Paris–France+Italy ≈ Rome, walked–walk+swim ≈ swam.

## GloVe (Global Vectors)

Word2Vec uses **local context windows**. GloVe (Pennington et al., 2014) instead uses **global co-occurrence statistics**: it builds a corpus-wide count matrix of which words appear near which, then learns embeddings whose dot products match the *log co-occurrence counts* via weighted least squares. The key insight is that co-occurrence **ratios** carry meaning — $P(k \mid \text{ice}) / P(k \mid \text{steam})$ is large for $k = \text{solid}$ and small for $k = \text{gas}$ — and GloVe's objective makes vector differences capture exactly these ratios.

In practice GloVe and Word2Vec produce embeddings of similar quality; GloVe's batch formulation parallelizes more naturally.

## FastText

FastText (Bojanowski et al., 2017) extends Word2Vec by representing each word as a **bag of character n-grams**:

$$
\mathbf{v}_{\text{where}} = \mathbf{v}_{\text{wh}} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{whe}} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{her}} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{ere}} + \ldots
$$

**Key advantages:**
- Handles OOV words: "unhappiness" can be represented via its n-grams even if not in the vocabulary
- Better for morphologically rich languages (German, Turkish, Finnish)
- Better at capturing suffixes and prefixes

## Contextual embeddings

Word2Vec, GloVe, and FastText produce a **single vector per word** regardless of context. "Bank" in "river bank" and "bank account" gets the same embedding.

**Contextual embeddings** (ELMo, BERT, GPT) produce different vectors depending on context:

$$
\mathbf{h}_t = f(\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_T)[t]
$$

The representation of "bank" at position $t$ depends on the full sentence.

**ELMo** (Peters et al., 2018): run a bidirectional LSTM over the entire sentence, combine hidden states from multiple layers. Used as features on top of task-specific models.

**BERT** (Devlin et al., 2018): Transformer encoder trained on masked language modeling. Every token's representation encodes its full sentence context.

<Callout type="success" title="The embedding revolution">
  Static embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe) were state-of-the-art until ~2018. Contextual embeddings (ELMo, BERT) dramatically improved performance on nearly every NLP task by handling polysemy and long-range dependencies. Today, all large language models use contextual token representations.
</Callout>

**Evaluation: semantic similarity**

Embeddings are evaluated on tasks like WordSim-353 (human-judged similarity pairs) and the analogy task:

$$
\text{sim}(\mathbf{v}_a, \mathbf{v}_b) = \frac{\mathbf{v}_a \cdot \mathbf{v}_b}{\|\mathbf{v}_a\| \|\mathbf{v}_b\|}
$$

## Common mistakes

1. **Using static embeddings for polysemous words** — "bat" (animal) and "bat" (sports equipment) get identical vectors with Word2Vec; use contextual embeddings if this matters.
2. **Averaging word vectors for sentence similarity** — averaging is order-blind, so "Dog bites man" and "Man bites dog" collapse to the *exact same* vector. Use sentence-level models for similarity tasks.
3. **Not normalizing vectors before cosine similarity** — unnormalized dot products mix magnitude with direction.
4. **Training word vectors on too little data** — Word2Vec embeddings need >1M words to produce meaningful geometry. Use pre-trained vectors otherwise.
5. **Forgetting to align embedding spaces when combining** — GloVe-300d and Word2Vec-300d live in different vector spaces; you can't concatenate and compare across models without alignment.

## Key takeaways

- Word2Vec trains a neural network to predict context (skip-gram) or center word (CBOW); the hidden-layer weights are the embeddings.
- Skip-gram uses negative sampling to approximate the full softmax; it's better for rare words.
- GloVe factorizes the global co-occurrence matrix; similar quality to Word2Vec, more parallelizable.
- FastText uses character n-grams, handling OOV and morphologically rich languages.
- Contextual embeddings (BERT, GPT) produce different vectors per word per context, solving polysemy and enabling transfer learning.
