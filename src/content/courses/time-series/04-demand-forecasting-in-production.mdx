# Demand Forecasting in Production

An ARIMA model trained on a single aggregated demand signal is a starting point — not a production system. DoorDash forecasts delivery demand per restaurant per hour at 30-minute resolution. Uber models ride demand per geohex cell per 5-minute interval. Instacart predicts grocery item demand across thousands of SKUs and hundreds of zip codes. Each faces the same fundamental challenges: **hierarchical structure**, **sparse history at fine granularity**, **special events**, and **real-time latency requirements**.

<Callout type="info" title="The forecasting thread">
This is where the site's running `daily-demand` problem reaches the **deployment-feedback** slot of the [ML project loop](/wiki/ml-project-loop). The toy series had one regime shift; a real system has a new one every week (a store opens, a promo lands, a holiday hits). The model that was accurate at launch rots, so production forecasting is a *loop*: forecast → monitor error → detect drift → retrain on fresh data → redeploy. The same closing move you'll see in [Streaming ML](/courses/streaming-ml).
</Callout>

**Real-world examples:** DoorDash (order volume forecasting for driver supply planning), Lyft (market-level and driver-level demand), Uber (surge pricing and driver rebalancing), Instacart (inventory and delivery slot planning), Foodpanda (kitchen capacity allocation), Swiggy (driver availability matching), ODA (grocery inventory forecasting), Oda (last-mile logistics).

## Hierarchical time series

Most business forecasting problems are **hierarchical**: total national demand is the sum of regional demands, which are sums of store-level demands, which are sums of SKU-level demands.

```
Total demand
├── Region A
│   ├── City 1
│   │   ├── Restaurant #101
│   │   └── Restaurant #102
│   └── City 2
└── Region B
```

A naive approach: fit independent models at each level. The problem: forecasts at different levels won't be **coherent** — summing city-level forecasts may not equal the regional-level forecast.

### Reconciliation approaches

| Approach | Method | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Top-down** | Forecast aggregate; disaggregate using historical proportions | Stable series, sparse lower levels |
| **Bottom-up** | Forecast leaf nodes; sum up | Low-count series where aggregation loses info |
| **Optimal reconciliation (MinT)** | Jointly minimize forecast error across all levels using GLS | When you need coherence + accuracy |

**MinT (Minimum Trace)** casts reconciliation as a linear projection:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = S(S^{\top}W^{-1}S)^{-1}S^{\top}W^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{y}}
$$

where $S$ is the summing matrix encoding the hierarchy, $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ are base forecasts, and $W$ is the covariance matrix of forecast errors. The result is a coherent set of forecasts that minimize total mean squared error across all levels.

The formula is short; the reason you need it is not. Walk the pipeline below on a simulated five-store hierarchy — forecast every node independently, watch the numbers fail to add up, then apply each reconciliation method as the *same* projection with a different $G$. The method control re-runs everything, and the accuracy table averages over 200 draws of the future rather than one test window, because on a single window these methods differ by less than the noise.

## Handling special events

Time series models extrapolate from history, but **holidays, promotions, and external events** create demand spikes outside the historical distribution. Models that ignore them produce large, systematic errors.

### Prophet's additive decomposition

Facebook Prophet decomposes demand as:

$$
y(t) = g(t) + s(t) + h(t) + \epsilon(t)
$$

- $g(t)$: trend (piecewise linear or logistic growth)
- $s(t)$: seasonality (Fourier series with weekly and yearly periods)
- $h(t)$: **holiday effects** — user-specified calendar events with individually estimated impact coefficients
- $\epsilon(t)$: noise

The holiday component is the key: supply a list of dates (Thanksgiving, Super Bowl, local public holidays) and Prophet estimates a separate additive effect for each. At DoorDash, promotions and restaurant campaigns are treated as "holidays" and injected into the Prophet decomposition.

<Callout type="tip" title="External regressors for planned events">
  For promotional events where magnitude is known in advance (e.g., a planned $5 discount starting Tuesday), add the promotion indicator as an **exogenous regressor** in ARIMAX or as a future covariate in TFT (Temporal Fusion Transformer). The model learns the typical promotion lift from history and applies it to the future event.
</Callout>

### Anomaly robustification

Black swan events (COVID lockdowns, extreme weather) contaminate the training signal. Two defenses:

1. **Robust STL decomposition**: use median-based trend extraction, which is resistant to outliers.
2. **Truncated training window**: weight recent data more heavily (exponential decay) so ancient anomalies don't distort the fitted model.

## ETA prediction: forecasting with uncertainty

ETA (Estimated Time of Arrival) prediction is a special-case demand forecast: the target is a **continuous duration** (minutes to delivery) rather than a volume. Calibrated uncertainty is critical — telling a customer "30 minutes" when the true distribution is 20–50 minutes damages trust.

### Feature engineering for ETA

| Feature | Source | Lag |
|---|---|---|
| Distance (crow-flies) | Maps API | — |
| Historical travel time for route | Log store | 30-min bins |
| Current traffic index (segment-level) | Real-time traffic feed | < 1 min |
| Courier speed percentile | Courier feature store | 1-hour rolling |
| Order complexity (# items, prep time) | Order system | — |
| Time of day, day of week | Clock | — |

Swiggy uses gradient boosted trees over these features; their ETA model improves primarily by adding more fine-grained traffic features, not by model complexity.

### Quantile regression for uncertainty

Instead of predicting the mean ETA, predict **multiple quantiles** (p10, p50, p90):

$$
\mathcal{L}_q(\hat{y}, y) = q(y - \hat{y})^+ + (1-q)(\hat{y} - y)^+
$$

Optimizing this pinball loss at $q = 0.9$ produces the 90th percentile ETA — a conservative bound shown to the customer. DoorDash shows a range ("arriving in 25–40 minutes") instead of a point estimate, using the p25 and p75 quantiles.

## Ensemble forecasting

No single model wins on all horizons and series types. Production systems blend:

- **Statistical models** (ETS/ARIMA/Prophet): robust on short history, no GPU required, fast to retrain.
- **Gradient boosting** (LightGBM/XGBoost): powerful for feature-rich tabular forecasting, handles missing values.
- **Deep learning** (N-BEATS, TFT, PatchTST): best on long histories and cross-series patterns (global models trained on all SKUs simultaneously).

The ensemble is typically a **stacking regressor**: a secondary model (often Ridge regression) trained on the validation-set predictions of each base model learns optimal blending weights. Instacart's system uses a similar approach: ETS + LightGBM + a transformer are blended, with the transformer upweighted for SKUs with 6+ months of history.

<Callout type="warning" title="Backtesting ≠ production evaluation">
  Backtesting with a fixed test window overfits your model selection to that window. Use **expanding window validation** (walk-forward): repeatedly train on all data up to time $t$, forecast $h$ steps ahead, advance $t$ by one period, repeat. This simulates the actual rolling-retrain production cadence.
</Callout>

## Production architecture

```
Raw data (orders, traffic, weather)
        ↓
Feature store (precomputed lag features)
        ↓
Multiple base models trained on rolling windows
        ↓
Stacking / blending layer
        ↓
Coherency reconciliation (if hierarchical)
        ↓
Forecast served to downstream systems:
  ├─ Driver dispatch & supply planning
  ├─ Kitchen / prep time scheduling
  └─ Customer ETA display
```

Uber's forecasting platform (built on Apache Flink) produces horizon-5-minute predictions for thousands of geo-cells, updating every 5 minutes. The entire pipeline (from raw data to deployed forecast) runs on an automated retraining cadence with automated regression detection.

## Key takeaways

- **Hierarchical forecasting** requires coherent reconciliation across levels (MinT / bottom-up) so sub-forecasts sum to the aggregate.
- **Special events** must be modeled explicitly — Prophet's holiday component, ARIMAX regressors, or TFT future covariates; generic models produce large systematic errors on known events.
- **ETA prediction** benefits from quantile regression to produce calibrated uncertainty intervals rather than point estimates.
- **Ensemble + stacking** consistently outperforms any single model; the blend weights themselves should be learned, not hand-tuned.
