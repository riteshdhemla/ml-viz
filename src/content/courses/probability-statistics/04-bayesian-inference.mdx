# Bayesian Inference

MLE gives a single point estimate of $\theta$. But what if we have prior beliefs about $\theta$ — for example, that weights in a neural network should be small? **Bayesian inference** formalizes this by treating $\theta$ itself as a random variable, so the output is a whole *distribution* over $\theta$ rather than one number.

**Real-world examples:** Bayesian neural networks model parameter uncertainty; spam filters update beliefs with each new email; medical diagnosis combines prior prevalence with test sensitivity/specificity.

## Bayes' theorem

$$
\underbrace{p(\theta | \mathcal{D})}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\underbrace{p(\mathcal{D} | \theta)}_{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(\mathcal{D})}_{\text{evidence}}}
$$

The **posterior** $p(\theta | \mathcal{D})$ combines what the data says (likelihood) with what we believed before seeing data (prior).

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Prior** $p(\theta)$ | Belief about $\theta$ before seeing data |
| **Likelihood** $p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$ | How probable the data is under $\theta$ |
| **Posterior** $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})$ | Updated belief after seeing data |
| **Evidence** $p(\mathcal{D})$ | Normalizing constant; often intractable |

The evidence is just whatever number makes the posterior integrate to $1$:

$$
p(\mathcal{D}) = \int p(\mathcal{D} | \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta
$$

Because it does not depend on $\theta$, we often drop it and write the *unnormalized* posterior, restoring the constant only at the end:

$$
p(\theta | \mathcal{D}) \;\propto\; p(\mathcal{D} | \theta)\, p(\theta)
$$

### Worked example: medical test (Bayes' theorem in numbers)

Bayes' theorem also works for discrete events. A disease affects $1\%$ of the population. A test is $99\%$ sensitive (catches sick people) and $95\%$ specific (correctly clears healthy people). **You test positive. What is the probability you are actually sick?**

Define the events: $D$ = "has the disease", $T^+$ = "tests positive". We are given:

$$
p(D) = 0.01, \qquad p(T^+ | D) = 0.99, \qquad p(T^+ | \neg D) = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05
$$

We want $p(D | T^+)$. Apply Bayes' theorem:

$$
p(D | T^+) = \frac{p(T^+ | D)\, p(D)}{p(T^+)}
$$

**Step 1 — compute the evidence** $p(T^+)$ by summing over both ways to test positive (sick *or* healthy):

$$
p(T^+) = \underbrace{p(T^+ | D)\,p(D)}_{\text{true positive}} + \underbrace{p(T^+ | \neg D)\,p(\neg D)}_{\text{false positive}}
$$

$$
p(T^+) = (0.99)(0.01) + (0.05)(0.99) = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594
$$

**Step 2 — divide:**

$$
p(D | T^+) = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx 0.167
$$

Despite a positive result from a "$99\%$ accurate" test, you are only about **$17\%$** likely to be sick. The prior $p(D) = 0.01$ is so small that false positives from the huge healthy population swamp the true positives. This is the classic **base-rate** lesson: the prior matters enormously.

## Updating beliefs with data

Bayesian inference is sequential. Start with a prior; after seeing data, your posterior becomes the prior for the next observation.

**Intuition:** Toss a coin once and see heads. $p(\text{fair}) = 0.5$. After 100 heads in a row, $p(\text{fair}) \approx 0$.

The posterior concentrates around the true parameter as data accumulates:

$$
p(\theta | x_1, \ldots, x_n) \propto p(\theta) \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i | \theta)
$$

## Conjugate priors

A prior is **conjugate** to a likelihood if the posterior is in the same family as the prior — making the update analytical (no integral to evaluate).

| Likelihood | Conjugate Prior | Posterior |
|------------|----------------|-----------|
| Bernoulli$(p)$ | Beta$(\alpha, \beta)$ | Beta$(\alpha + n_1, \beta + n_0)$ |
| Gaussian$(\mu, \sigma^2)$ known $\sigma^2$ | Gaussian$(\mu_0, \tau^2)$ | Gaussian (updated mean/precision) |
| Categorical$(\boldsymbol{\pi})$ | Dirichlet$(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ | Dirichlet$(\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \mathbf{n})$ |

### Deriving the Beta-Bernoulli posterior end-to-end

Let's *prove* the first row of the table from scratch. Model: each flip $x_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$, and the prior on the bias is $p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$.

**Step 1 — write the likelihood.** Suppose we observe $n$ flips with $k$ heads (so $n - k$ tails). Each flip contributes $p$ if it's a head and $1-p$ if it's a tail:

$$
p(\mathcal{D} | p) = \prod_{i=1}^n p^{\,x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i} = p^{\,k}\,(1-p)^{\,n-k}
$$

**Step 2 — write the prior.** The Beta density is

$$
p(p) = \frac{1}{B(\alpha, \beta)}\, p^{\,\alpha - 1}(1-p)^{\,\beta - 1}, \qquad B(\alpha,\beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}
$$

where $B(\alpha,\beta)$ is a constant that does not depend on $p$.

**Step 3 — multiply (Bayes, unnormalized).** Drop every factor that does not contain $p$:

$$
p(p | \mathcal{D}) \;\propto\; \underbrace{p^{\,k}(1-p)^{\,n-k}}_{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \underbrace{p^{\,\alpha - 1}(1-p)^{\,\beta - 1}}_{\text{prior}}
$$

**Step 4 — collect exponents.** Add the powers of $p$ and of $(1-p)$:

$$
p(p | \mathcal{D}) \;\propto\; p^{\,(\alpha + k) - 1}\,(1-p)^{\,(\beta + n - k) - 1}
$$

**Step 5 — recognize the shape.** This is *exactly* the kernel of a Beta density with parameters $\alpha + k$ and $\beta + n - k$. Since a probability distribution is determined by its kernel, the normalizing constant must be $B(\alpha + k,\ \beta + n - k)$, and therefore:

$$
\boxed{\,p \mid \mathcal{D} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + k,\; \beta + n - k)\,}
$$

No integral was needed — the conjugacy did the work. Writing $n_1 = k$ heads and $n_0 = n - k$ tails recovers the table entry $\text{Beta}(\alpha + n_1,\ \beta + n_0)$. The posterior mean is

$$
\mathbb{E}[p | \mathcal{D}] = \frac{\alpha + k}{\alpha + \beta + n}
$$

$\alpha$ and $\beta$ act like **pseudo-counts** — imaginary prior heads and tails. With $\alpha = \beta = 1$ (uniform prior), the posterior mean is $\frac{k+1}{n+2}$, which approaches the MLE $\frac{k}{n}$ as $n$ grows.

**Numeric instance.** Prior $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$ (a mild "probably fair" belief), then observe $k = 7$ heads in $n = 10$ flips ($n_0 = 3$ tails):

$$
\alpha_\text{post} = 2 + 7 = 9, \qquad \beta_\text{post} = 2 + 3 = 5
$$

$$
\mathbb{E}[p | \mathcal{D}] = \frac{9}{9 + 5} = \frac{9}{14} \approx 0.643, \qquad \hat{p}_\text{MLE} = \frac{7}{10} = 0.700
$$

The prior pulls the estimate from $0.70$ down toward $0.50$, landing at $0.643$. With more data the pull weakens and the posterior mean converges to the MLE.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import beta as beta_dist

# Prior: Beta(2, 2) — slight belief coin is fair
alpha_prior, beta_prior = 2, 2

# Observe 7 heads, 3 tails
n_heads, n_tails = 7, 3
alpha_post = alpha_prior + n_heads   # 9
beta_post  = beta_prior  + n_tails   # 5

# Posterior mean
posterior_mean = alpha_post / (alpha_post + beta_post)
print("Posterior mean: {:.3f}".format(posterior_mean))   # 0.643
print("MLE estimate:   {:.3f}".format(n_heads / (n_heads + n_tails)))  # 0.700

# The prior shrinks us toward 0.5
p_vals = np.linspace(0, 1, 200)
prior     = beta_dist.pdf(p_vals, alpha_prior, beta_prior)
posterior = beta_dist.pdf(p_vals, alpha_post, beta_post)
```

## MAP estimation

**Maximum a Posteriori (MAP)** estimation finds the mode of the posterior — the single most probable $\theta$:

$$
\hat{\theta}_\text{MAP} = \arg\max_\theta p(\theta | \mathcal{D})
$$

Because the evidence $p(\mathcal{D})$ does not depend on $\theta$, it cannot change *where* the maximum sits, so we may drop it. Taking $\log$ (which is monotonic, so it preserves the location of the maximum) turns the product into a sum:

$$
\hat{\theta}_\text{MAP} = \arg\max_\theta \big[\, p(\mathcal{D}|\theta)\,p(\theta)\,\big] = \arg\max_\theta \left[\underbrace{\ln p(\mathcal{D} | \theta)}_{\text{MLE objective}} + \underbrace{\ln p(\theta)}_{\text{prior penalty}}\right]
$$

So **MAP = MLE + log-prior penalty**. If the prior is flat (uniform), $\ln p(\theta)$ is a constant and MAP collapses back to MLE — the data-only estimate. The prior only matters when it is *informative*.

<Callout type="tip" title="L2 regularization is MAP with a Gaussian prior">
  Put a zero-mean Gaussian prior on each weight, $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$. Its log is
  $$\ln p(\theta) = -\frac{\|\theta\|^2}{2\tau^2} + \text{const}.$$
  Plug this into the MAP objective (and flip the sign to turn the maximization into the usual loss minimization):
  $$\hat{\theta}_\text{MAP} = \arg\min_\theta \Big[\underbrace{-\ln p(\mathcal{D}|\theta)}_{\text{data loss}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2\tau^2}\|\theta\|^2}_{\text{penalty}}\Big].$$
  Setting $\lambda = \tfrac{1}{2\tau^2}$, that is exactly **L2 (Ridge) regularization** with penalty $\lambda\|\theta\|^2$. A *tighter* prior (small $\tau^2$) means a *stronger* penalty (large $\lambda$). **L2 regularization is MAP estimation with a Gaussian prior.**
</Callout>

Similarly, **L1 regularization (Lasso)** corresponds to a Laplace prior $\theta \sim \text{Laplace}(0, b)$, whose log is $-\frac{1}{b}|\theta| + \text{const}$ — an absolute-value penalty $\lambda\|\theta\|_1$.

### Worked example: MAP vs MLE for the coin

The Beta-Bernoulli model gives a clean closed form for both estimates, so we can compare them by hand. With $k$ heads in $n$ flips and prior $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$:

**MLE** maximizes the likelihood $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$. Differentiate the log-likelihood and set it to zero:

$$
\frac{d}{dp}\big[k\ln p + (n-k)\ln(1-p)\big] = \frac{k}{p} - \frac{n-k}{1-p} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \hat{p}_\text{MLE} = \frac{k}{n}
$$

**MAP** maximizes the posterior, i.e. the *mode* of $\text{Beta}(\alpha + k,\ \beta + n - k)$. The mode of $\text{Beta}(a, b)$ is $\frac{a-1}{a+b-2}$, so:

$$
\hat{p}_\text{MAP} = \frac{(\alpha + k) - 1}{(\alpha + k) + (\beta + n - k) - 2} = \frac{\alpha + k - 1}{\alpha + \beta + n - 2}
$$

**Numbers.** With $\alpha = \beta = 2$, $k = 7$, $n = 10$:

$$
\hat{p}_\text{MLE} = \frac{7}{10} = 0.700, \qquad \hat{p}_\text{MAP} = \frac{2 + 7 - 1}{2 + 2 + 10 - 2} = \frac{8}{12} \approx 0.667
$$

The MAP estimate sits between the prior mode ($0.5$) and the MLE ($0.7$), pulled toward the prior. Note all three summaries differ: $\hat{p}_\text{MLE} = 0.700$, the posterior **mode** (MAP) $= 0.667$, and the posterior **mean** $= 0.643$. As $n \to \infty$, the $-1$ and $-2$ corrections become negligible and $\hat{p}_\text{MAP} \to \hat{p}_\text{MLE}$ — the data overwhelms the prior.

## Bayesian vs frequentist

| | Frequentist (MLE) | Bayesian |
|-|-------------------|---------|
| $\theta$ is... | Fixed unknown constant | Random variable |
| Output | Point estimate | Full posterior distribution |
| Uncertainty | Confidence intervals (tricky to interpret) | Credible intervals (direct probability) |
| Prior knowledge | Cannot incorporate | Naturally incorporated |
| Scalability | Fast | Can be slow (posterior integration) |

## Predictive distribution

Rather than a single $\hat{\theta}$, Bayesian models make predictions by averaging over all parameters weighted by the posterior:

$$
p(x_\text{new} | \mathcal{D}) = \int p(x_\text{new} | \theta)\, p(\theta | \mathcal{D})\, d\theta
$$

This **marginalizes out** $\theta$ and naturally quantifies uncertainty in the prediction. In practice, this integral is often approximated with variational inference or MCMC.

<Callout type="info" title="Bayesian deep learning">
  Full Bayesian inference over neural network weights is computationally intractable. Practical approximations include: **dropout as approximate Bayes** (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016), **variational inference** (Bayes by Backprop), and **deep ensembles** (train multiple networks from different initializations).
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

1. Choosing an **uninformative prior** carelessly — a flat prior over a parameter is not uninformative after a change of variables (Jeffreys prior addresses this).
2. Treating the **posterior mean** as the answer without acknowledging posterior width — narrow posteriors can be overconfident on small datasets.
3. Confusing **MAP estimation** (maximizes the posterior, ignores uncertainty) with full Bayesian inference (integrates over the posterior).

## Key takeaways

- Bayesian inference treats model parameters as random variables with a **prior** distribution.
- The **posterior** = likelihood × prior (normalized) — it combines data evidence with prior beliefs.
- **MAP** = MLE + log-prior; Gaussian prior → L2 regularization; Laplace prior → L1 regularization.
- The predictive distribution averages over parameters, naturally capturing uncertainty.
