# Entropy & KL Divergence

Every classifier you will ever train with cross-entropy loss is secretly doing information theory. This lesson builds the three quantities that run modern ML — entropy, cross-entropy, and KL divergence — from one primitive: **surprise**. All three show up in the loop's **objective** stage: cross-entropy *is* the classification loss, and KL divergence is the objective behind variational methods and distillation.

## Surprise and entropy

If an event has probability $p$, define its surprise as $-\log p$. Rare events are very surprising ($-\log 0.01 \approx 4.6$ nats); certain events aren't surprising at all ($-\log 1 = 0$). The log makes surprise *additive*: the surprise of two independent events is the sum of their surprises.

**Entropy** is the expected surprise of a distribution, the central quantity introduced by [Shannon (1948)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1948.tb01338.x):

$$
H(p) = -\sum_x p(x) \log p(x)
$$

It measures how unpredictable a source is:

- A fair coin: $H = \log 2 \approx 0.69$ nats (1 bit) — maximal for two outcomes
- A coin with $p(\text{heads}) = 0.99$: $H \approx 0.056$ nats — almost no uncertainty
- A uniform die: $\log 6$ — uniform distributions maximize entropy, certainty minimizes it

Entropy is also a *compression limit*: you cannot losslessly encode samples from $p$ in fewer than $H(p)$ nats per symbol on average — Shannon's source-coding theorem ([Shannon, 1948](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1948.tb01338.x)). You met entropy already as a split criterion in [decision trees](/courses/knn-decision-trees/02-decision-trees) — information gain is just entropy reduction.

## Cross-entropy: paying for the wrong codebook

Suppose data really comes from $p$, but you model it with $q$. Your average surprise is the **cross-entropy**:

$$
H(p, q) = -\sum_x p(x) \log q(x)
$$

This is exactly the classification loss: the data distribution puts probability 1 on the true label, so the sum collapses to $-\log q(\text{true label})$ — the log-loss of your model's predicted probability. Minimizing cross-entropy = maximizing [log-likelihood](/courses/probability-statistics/03-maximum-likelihood-estimation). Same math, two vocabularies.

## KL divergence: the gap between them

Cross-entropy splits into two parts:

$$
H(p, q) = H(p) + \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q)}_{\text{your modeling penalty}}
$$

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
$$

KL divergence — introduced by [Kullback & Leibler (1951)](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-mathematical-statistics/volume-22/issue-1/On-Information-and-Sufficiency/10.1214/aoms/1177729694.full) — is the *extra* surprise you suffer for using $q$ when the truth is $p$. Three properties to internalize:

1. $D_{\mathrm{KL}} \ge 0$, with equality only when $p = q$ — it behaves like a distance from the truth
2. It is **not symmetric**: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) \ne D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$, and the difference matters
3. If $q(x) = 0$ where $p(x) > 0$, the divergence is **infinite** — never assign zero probability to something that can happen (the same lesson as Laplace smoothing in [Naive Bayes](/courses/probabilistic-models/03-naive-bayes))

```python
import numpy as np

p = np.array([0.7, 0.2, 0.1])          # truth
q = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])          # model

H_p  = -(p * np.log(p)).sum()          # entropy of the truth
H_pq = -(p * np.log(q)).sum()          # cross-entropy of model on truth
kl   = (p * np.log(p / q)).sum()       # KL divergence

print(f"H(p) = {H_p:.4f}, H(p,q) = {H_pq:.4f}, KL = {kl:.4f}")
print(f"identity check: H(p) + KL = {H_p + kl:.4f}")   # equals H(p,q)
```

<Callout type="info" title="Which direction? Mode-seeking vs mean-covering">
  Minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ (forward, used by MLE) forces $q$ to **cover** everywhere $p$ has mass — spread out, or pay infinite penalty. Minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ (reverse, used by variational inference and [VAEs](/courses/generative-models/03-variational-autoencoders)) lets $q$ **lock onto one mode** of $p$ and ignore the rest. Same formula, opposite personalities.
</Callout>

- **Information gain** — entropy reduction as a tree-split criterion

## Key takeaways

- Entropy = expected surprise $-\sum p \log p$: uniform maximizes it, certainty zeroes it
- Cross-entropy = entropy + KL: training on log-loss minimizes the KL between data and model
- KL is non-negative, zero only at equality, asymmetric, and infinite when the model says "impossible" about something real
- Forward KL covers all modes; reverse KL picks one — the choice shapes generative models
