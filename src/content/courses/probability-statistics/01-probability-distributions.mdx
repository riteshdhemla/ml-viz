# Probability Distributions

Machine learning is fundamentally about uncertainty — uncertain data, uncertain labels, uncertain predictions. Probability theory gives us the language to reason precisely about uncertainty. Most ML models are probability distributions in disguise.

## Random variables and distributions

A **random variable** $X$ maps outcomes to numbers. Its **probability distribution** describes how likely each value (or range of values) is. There are two flavours, and the difference matters.

### PMF vs PDF — the one distinction to get right

For a **discrete** random variable (countable values like $0, 1, 2, \ldots$) we use a **probability mass function** (PMF). The PMF gives the actual probability of each value:

$$
p(k) = P(X = k), \qquad 0 \le p(k) \le 1, \qquad \sum_k p(k) = 1
$$

Here each $p(k)$ *is* a probability, so it can never exceed $1$.

For a **continuous** random variable (any real value, like a height of $1.732\ldots$ m) the probability of hitting *exactly* one value is zero. Instead we use a **probability density function** (PDF) $p(x)$. Density is "probability per unit length", and we get a probability only by integrating over an interval:

$$
P(a \leq X \leq b) = \int_a^b p(x)\, dx, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^\infty p(x)\, dx = 1
$$

<Callout type="warning" title="A density is not a probability">
  A PDF value $p(x)$ can be **larger than 1** — it is a *rate*, not a probability. For example a Gaussian with $\sigma = 0.1$ peaks at $p(\mu) = \tfrac{1}{0.1\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 3.99$. Only the **area** under the curve is a probability, and that area is always $\le 1$.
</Callout>

In both cases the total "stuff" sums (discrete) or integrates (continuous) to exactly $1$ — that normalization is what makes it a valid distribution.

## Expectation and variance

The **expected value** (mean) is the probability-weighted average of $X$ — its long-run average over infinitely many draws:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_k k \cdot p(k) \quad \text{(discrete)}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\, p(x)\, dx \quad \text{(continuous)}
$$

The **variance** measures spread around the mean. The most useful form is the "mean of the square minus the square of the mean", which we will derive once and reuse:

$$
\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2
$$

<Callout type="info" title="Where that shortcut comes from">
  Let $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$. Expand the square and use linearity of expectation ($\mathbb{E}[aX + b] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b$, and $\mu$ is a constant):
  $$
  \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X] + \mu^2 = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2 = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2.
  $$
</Callout>

The **standard deviation** $\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}$ has the same units as $X$.

**Key identity:** $\text{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \text{Var}(X)$ — shifting doesn't change variance, scaling does.

## Key distributions in ML

### Bernoulli — binary outcomes

$X \in \{0, 1\}$ (think: one coin flip) with success probability $p$. Its PMF is just two numbers:

$$
P(X = 1) = p, \qquad P(X = 0) = 1 - p
$$

A neat way to write both at once is $p(k) = p^{k}(1-p)^{1-k}$ for $k \in \{0,1\}$.

**Deriving $\mathbb{E}[X]$ step by step.** Sum value $\times$ probability over the only two outcomes:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{k \in \{0,1\}} k\, p(k) = 0\cdot(1-p) + 1\cdot p = p.
$$

**Deriving $\text{Var}(X)$.** First $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$. Since $0^2 = 0$ and $1^2 = 1$, squaring the values changes nothing here:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0^2\cdot(1-p) + 1^2\cdot p = p.
$$

Now apply the shortcut $\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$:

$$
\text{Var}(X) = p - p^2 = p(1-p).
$$

Note this is largest at $p = 0.5$ (a fair coin is the *most* uncertain) and zero at $p = 0$ or $p = 1$ (a certain outcome has no spread).

**Where:** Binary classification output. A logistic regression outputs a Bernoulli probability.

### Categorical — multi-class outcomes

$X \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$ with probabilities $\boldsymbol{\pi} = [\pi_1, \ldots, \pi_K]$, $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$.

**Where:** Multi-class classification. Softmax output = categorical distribution.

### Gaussian (Normal) — the bell curve

$$
p(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

with $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu$ and $\text{Var}(X) = \sigma^2$. Let's see where each piece comes from.

**Where the $\tfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$ comes from (normalization).** The shape of the bell is the bare exponential $e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}$; the constant in front is whatever makes the total area equal $1$. We need the famous **Gaussian integral**. Start from the standard case $\mu = 0, \sigma = 1$ and call the unknown total area $I$:

$$
I = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2/2}\, dx.
$$

The trick is to compute $I^2$ as a 2-D integral and switch to polar coordinates ($x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$):

$$
I^2 = \int_{-\infty}^\infty\!\!\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}\, dx\, dy
= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}\, r\, dr\, d\theta.
$$

The inner integral is now elementary — substitute $u = r^2/2$, $du = r\,dr$:

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}\, r\, dr = \int_0^\infty e^{-u}\, du = 1, \qquad \text{so}\quad I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi} 1\, d\theta = 2\pi.
$$

Therefore $I = \sqrt{2\pi}$. Rescaling by $\sigma$ (substitute $z = (x-\mu)/\sigma$, $dx = \sigma\,dz$) multiplies the area by $\sigma$, giving $\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}$. Dividing by exactly that constant forces the area to $1$ — that is the normalization factor.

**Deriving $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu$.** Substitute $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$ so $x = \mu + \sigma z$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x\, p(x)\, dx
= \underbrace{\mu \int_{-\infty}^\infty \phi(z)\, dz}_{=\,\mu\cdot 1} + \underbrace{\sigma \int_{-\infty}^\infty z\,\phi(z)\, dz}_{=\,0},
$$

where $\phi(z) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}$. The first term is $\mu$ because $\phi$ integrates to $1$; the second term vanishes because $z\,\phi(z)$ is an **odd** function integrated over a symmetric range. So $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu$.

**Deriving $\text{Var}(X) = \sigma^2$.** With the same substitution, $\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2] = \sigma^2 \int z^2 \phi(z)\, dz$. Integrating $z^2\phi(z)$ by parts (let $u = z$, $dv = z\phi(z)\,dz$, so $v = -\phi(z)$) gives:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^\infty z^2 \phi(z)\, dz = \Big[-z\,\phi(z)\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int_{-\infty}^\infty \phi(z)\, dz = 0 + 1 = 1.
$$

The boundary term is $0$ (the Gaussian decays faster than $z$ grows), and the leftover integral is again $1$. Hence $\text{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 \cdot 1 = \sigma^2$.

<Callout type="tip" title="Why Gaussian is everywhere">
  The **Central Limit Theorem**: the mean of many independent random variables converges to a Gaussian, regardless of the original distribution. Features in real datasets are often (approximately) Gaussian — gradients of large neural networks are too.
</Callout>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Bernoulli: flip 10000 biased coins, check E[X]=p and Var=p(1-p)
p = 0.7
x = rng.binomial(n=1, p=p, size=10000)
print("Bernoulli  E[X]: {:.3f} (true {:.3f})".format(x.mean(), p))
print("Bernoulli  Var:  {:.3f} (true {:.3f})".format(x.var(), p * (1 - p)))

# Gaussian: check E[X]=mu and Var=sigma^2
mu, sigma = 3.0, 1.5
y = rng.normal(mu, sigma, size=10000)
print("Gaussian   E[X]: {:.3f} (true {:.3f})".format(y.mean(), mu))
print("Gaussian   Var:  {:.3f} (true {:.3f})".format(y.var(), sigma ** 2))
```

### Multivariate Gaussian

$$
p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{1/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right)
$$

The covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ captures both variance (diagonal) and correlations (off-diagonal). It's always symmetric positive semi-definite.

**Where:** Gaussian Mixture Models, Kalman filters, Gaussian Processes, VAEs.

## Conditional probability and independence

$$
P(A | B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}
$$

Events $A$ and $B$ are **independent** if $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$, equivalently $P(A|B) = P(A)$.

**In ML:** Naïve Bayes assumes features are conditionally independent given the class. This strong assumption is often violated but still works well in practice.

## The sum and product rules

$$
P(A) = \sum_B P(A, B) \quad \text{(sum rule / marginalization)}
$$

$$
P(A, B) = P(A|B) P(B) = P(B|A) P(A) \quad \text{(product rule)}
$$

Combining them gives **Bayes' theorem**:

$$
P(A | B) = \frac{P(B | A)\, P(A)}{P(B)}
$$

<Callout type="info" title="KL divergence — comparing distributions">
  The **Kullback–Leibler divergence** measures how different distribution $q$ is from distribution $p$:
  $$D_\text{KL}(p \| q) = \int p(x) \ln \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} dx \geq 0$$
  $D_\text{KL}(p\|q) = 0$ iff $p = q$. KL divergence appears in the VAE loss, reinforcement learning policy gradient, and variational inference.
</Callout>

## From distributions to ML: the likelihood

Why does any of this matter for machine learning? Because **a model is a distribution with adjustable parameters**, and we *fit* those parameters by asking: "which parameter values make the data we actually observed most probable?" That probability-of-the-data, viewed as a function of the parameters, is the **likelihood**.

Suppose we observe $n$ independent data points $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ drawn from a distribution with parameter $\theta$. By the product rule, the joint probability factorizes:

$$
L(\theta) = p(x_1, \ldots, x_n \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid \theta).
$$

Products of many small numbers underflow, so we almost always take the logarithm, turning the product into a sum (the **log-likelihood**):

$$
\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(x_i \mid \theta).
$$

**Worked example — fitting a Bernoulli.** Say we flip a coin $n$ times and see $s$ heads. Using $p(x_i \mid \theta) = \theta^{x_i}(1-\theta)^{1-x_i}$:

$$
\ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \big[x_i \log\theta + (1-x_i)\log(1-\theta)\big] = s\log\theta + (n-s)\log(1-\theta).
$$

To maximize, set the derivative to zero:

$$
\frac{d\ell}{d\theta} = \frac{s}{\theta} - \frac{n-s}{1-\theta} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; s(1-\theta) = (n-s)\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; \hat\theta = \frac{s}{n}.
$$

The best-fit success probability is simply the **fraction of heads** — exactly the intuitive answer, now derived from first principles. This same recipe (write the likelihood, take its log, maximize) is how logistic regression, linear regression, and neural-network classifiers are all trained; their loss functions are *negative* log-likelihoods in disguise. We pick this idea apart in the next lesson.

## Key takeaways

- Distributions summarize uncertainty; ML models output distributions (often Bernoulli or Categorical).
- **Expectation** = mean; **variance** = spread; the Gaussian is determined by just two numbers ($\mu, \sigma^2$).
- **Conditional probability** is the foundation of Bayes' theorem and most probabilistic inference.
- **Independence** means knowing one variable tells you nothing about the other — rare in real data.
