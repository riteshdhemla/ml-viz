# What Are Generative Models?

Most machine learning is **discriminative**: given an input $x$, predict a label $y$. A cat-or-dog classifier learns $P(y \mid x)$. Generative models instead learn to model the data itself, $P(x)$, and the fascinating thing through a [project-loop](/wiki/ml-project-loop) lens is that VAEs, GANs, and diffusion are mostly the *same* hypothesis space (a neural net) with radically different **objectives** for "make realistic data." Watch the objective slot do the heavy lifting across this course.

<WikiLink slug="ml-project-loop" title="The ML Project Loop" />

Generative models flip the goal. They learn the **distribution of the data itself** ($P(x)$), so they can sample *new* examples that look like the originals.

**Real-world examples:** Image generation (DALL-E, Midjourney), text generation (GPT), drug discovery, data augmentation, anomaly detection, image super-resolution.

## Two paradigms

| Approach | Learns | Example |
|----------|--------|---------|
| Discriminative | $P(y \mid x)$: boundary between classes | Cat vs dog classifier |
| Generative | $P(x)$: what the data looks like | A model that draws new cats and dogs |

<Callout type="tip" title="Intuition">
  A discriminative model learns to tell real from fake. A generative model learns to *be the forger* — it creates data so realistic that the discriminative model can't tell the difference.
</Callout>

### Fit both to the same points

<GenerativeVsDiscriminativeViz />

Both models are fitted live on the sampled points, and on this data they draw **almost the same boundary**. That is the useful surprise: the difference between the paradigms is not the decision, it is everything else the model knows.

**Structure substitutes for data.** Test error on 4000 held-out points, by training size:

| n | logistic regression | class-conditional Gaussians |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | 4.05% | **2.08%** |
| 16 | 2.80% | 1.93% |
| 30 | 2.65% | 2.00% |
| 60 | 1.85% | 2.02% |
| 400 | 1.82% | 1.90% |

With eight labels the generative model is nearly twice as accurate; by sixty they have converged and the advantage is gone. Modelling $P(x,y)$ commits you to an assumption about the data's shape, and that assumption pays exactly while you have too little data to learn the shape from scratch — the classic Ng & Jordan result.

**And only one of them can say "I have never seen this."** Move the probe off the edge of the data. At $(4.5, 3.2)$ the logistic regression reports $P(\text{orange}) = 1.0000$ (total certainty), while the generative model reports $p(x)$ on the order of $10^{-8}$. Push the probe to $(-6, 5)$ and it is $P = 0.0000$ against $p(x) \approx 10^{-24}$. A discriminative model has no vocabulary for doubt: it knows which side of a line you are on and literally nothing else, and a point a thousand miles from any training example is simply very far on one side. This is why out-of-distribution detection, anomaly detection and calibration under shift all reach for a density.

## Why generate data?

1. **Creative AI:** Image synthesis, music composition, text generation
2. **Data augmentation:** Generate synthetic training data for rare classes
3. **Anomaly detection:** If a new point has low $P(x)$, it's probably anomalous
4. **Drug discovery:** Generate novel molecular structures with desired properties
5. **Compression:** Learn efficient representations (VAEs)
6. **Missing data:** Fill in missing values by sampling from the learned distribution

## How do they learn $P(x)$?

### 1. Explicit density models

Try to directly model $P(x)$ or a tractable lower bound:

- **VAEs** (Variational Autoencoders): learn an approximate posterior over a latent space
- **Normalizing Flows**: transform a simple distribution through invertible functions

### 2. Implicit density models

Learn to *sample* from $P(x)$ without ever computing it directly:

- **GANs** (Generative Adversarial Networks): a generator fools a discriminator
- **Diffusion Models**: iteratively denoise random noise into data

### 3. Autoregressive models

Factor the joint distribution step-by-step:

$$
P(x) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(x_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1})
$$

- **RNNs / Transformers** for text generation (GPT)
- **PixelCNN** for images

```python
# Autoregressive: predict next token given previous ones
# GPT-style: P("cat") = P("c") · P("a"|"c") · P("t"|"ca")
```

<Callout type="info" title="The tradeoff">
  Explicit models give you exact $P(x)$ but are often intractable. Implicit models are more flexible but only give you samples, not probabilities. Autoregressive models are exact but slow (sequential generation).
</Callout>

## The latent space idea

Most generative models share a common trick: they don't generate data directly. Instead, they learn a **latent space** — a compressed, lower-dimensional representation where the structure of the data is simpler.

Generate a point in latent space $\mathbf{z}$, then **decode** it into a full data point $\mathbf{x}$:

$$
\mathbf{z} \sim P(\mathbf{z}) \quad \xrightarrow{\text{decoder}} \quad \mathbf{x}
$$

The latent space captures the "essence" of the data (style, pose, identity), while the decoder fills in the details.

### A concrete density: 2D Gaussian

To make $P(x)$ concrete, consider a 2D Gaussian with mean $\mu = [1, 2]^\top$ and diagonal covariance $\Sigma = \text{diag}(1, 4)$. Evaluating the density at the mean $x = [1, 2]^\top$ (where $x - \mu = \mathbf{0}$, so the exponent is zero):

$$
p(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{|\Sigma|}} \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right) = \frac{1}{2\pi\cdot 2}\exp(0) = \frac{1}{4\pi} \approx 0.0796
$$

Here $|\Sigma| = 1 \times 4 = 4$, so $\sqrt{|\Sigma|} = 2$.

A GAN trained on this distribution can sample $x \sim p(x)$ but cannot compute $p(x)$ for a given $x$ — the generator function $G(z)$ has no inverse.

## Quick comparison

| Model | Type | Pros | Cons |
|-------|------|------|------|
| VAE | Explicit | Smooth latent space, stable training | Blurry outputs |
| GAN | Implicit | Sharp, realistic outputs | Training instability, mode collapse |
| Diffusion | Explicit | Best quality, stable training | Slow sampling |
| Autoregressive | Explicit | Exact likelihood | Slow (sequential) |

## Common mistakes

1. **Confusing discriminative and generative models**: a discriminative model learns $p(y|x)$; a generative model learns $p(x)$ or $p(x,y)$. Only generative models can produce new samples.
2. **Misunderstanding latent spaces**: the latent vector $z$ is not the same as learned features in a discriminative model; it is a compressed stochastic representation, not a deterministic encoding.
3. **Equating sampling with evaluating $p(x)$**: GANs can sample but cannot compute $p(x)$ explicitly; VAEs and diffusion models can (approximately); this distinction matters for density estimation tasks.

## Key takeaways

- **Discriminative** models learn $P(y\mid x)$; **generative** models learn $P(x)$.
- Generative models can **sample** new data, impute, and detect anomalies.
- Families: explicit density (VAE, autoregressive), implicit (GAN), and diffusion.
- A **latent space** is a compact code that samples are decoded from.
