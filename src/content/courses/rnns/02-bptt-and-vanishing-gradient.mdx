# Backpropagation Through Time & the Vanishing Gradient

An RNN learns the same way any network does — gradient descent on a loss. But because the network is a *loop* unrolled over time, the gradient has to flow **backward through every time step**. This is **Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT)**, and it exposes the single biggest weakness of vanilla RNNs.

## Unrolling for training

To train, we unroll the RNN over the whole sequence, treating it as a very deep feed-forward network — one "layer" per time step — that happens to *share weights*. The total loss sums each step's loss:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathcal{L}_t(\mathbf{y}_t, \hat{\mathbf{y}}_t)
$$

Because $W_{hh}$ is reused at every step, its gradient is the **sum of contributions from all steps**:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{hh}} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_t}{\partial W_{hh}}
$$

<Callout type="tip" title="Intuition">
  BPTT is ordinary backprop on the unrolled graph. The twist: one weight matrix appears in every layer, so its update gathers a vote from every time step. Early steps get a say in the final prediction — *if* their gradient survives the trip back.
</Callout>

## The chain through time

To see why long-range learning is hard, trace how the loss at step $T$ depends on a hidden state far back at step $k$. We need $\partial \mathcal{L}_T / \partial \mathbf{h}_k$, and the only path from $\mathbf{h}_k$ to $\mathcal{L}_T$ runs *forward through every hidden state in between*. The chain rule therefore threads through $\mathbf{h}_{k+1}, \mathbf{h}_{k+2}, \dots, \mathbf{h}_T$:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_k} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_T}\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{T-1}}\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_{T-1}}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{T-2}}\cdots\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_{k+1}}{\partial \mathbf{h}_k}
= \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_T} \prod_{t=k+1}^{T} \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t-1}}
$$

So the gradient over a gap of $T-k$ steps is a **product of $T-k$ Jacobians**. Everything hinges on what one of those Jacobians looks like.

### Deriving one step's Jacobian

The recurrence is $\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{a}_t)$ where the **pre-activation** is $\mathbf{a}_t = W_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + W_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}$. Differentiate component-by-component. For output unit $i$ and input unit $j$:

$$
\frac{\partial h_{t,i}}{\partial h_{t-1,j}}
= \underbrace{\tanh'(a_{t,i})}_{\text{outer: activation}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial a_{t,i}}{\partial h_{t-1,j}}}_{= \,(W_{hh})_{ij}}
= \tanh'(a_{t,i})\,(W_{hh})_{ij}
$$

The $\tanh'(a_{t,i})$ factor depends only on the row $i$, so collecting all $i,j$ into a matrix puts those factors on a diagonal:

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t-1}} = \mathrm{diag}\!\big(\tanh'(\mathbf{a}_t)\big)\,W_{hh}\;}
$$

In backprop we propagate the gradient *backward*, which uses the transpose: $\mathbf{d}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} = W_{hh}^{\top}\,\mathrm{diag}(\tanh'(\mathbf{a}_t))\,\mathbf{d}\mathbf{h}_t$. Either way, **each step back multiplies by one copy of $W_{hh}$ and one diagonal of $\tanh'$ values.**

## Why gradients vanish (or explode)

Substitute the Jacobian into the product. Over the gap $k \to T$:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_k} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_T} \prod_{t=k+1}^{T} \mathrm{diag}\!\big(\tanh'(\mathbf{a}_t)\big)\,W_{hh}
$$

Take norms and use $\|AB\| \le \|A\|\,\|B\|$ (submultiplicativity). Let $\gamma = \max_z \tanh'(z) = \tanh'(0) = 1$ bound the diagonal, and let $\|W_{hh}\|$ be the spectral norm (largest singular value). Every one of the $T-k$ factors is bounded the same way, so:

$$
\left\|\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_k}\right\|
\;\le\; \left\|\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_T}\right\|\;\big(\,\|W_{hh}\|\cdot\gamma\,\big)^{T-k}
$$

That exponent $T-k$ is the whole story. Writing $\lambda = \|W_{hh}\|$ (its "gain" per step, $\gamma \le 1$):

- **$\lambda < 1$** → the bound shrinks like $\lambda^{T-k} \to 0$. The gradient **vanishes**: distant steps receive almost no learning signal.
- **$\lambda > 1$** → the matrix has a direction it stretches, the product grows like $\lambda^{T-k} \to \infty$, and the gradient **explodes** (huge updates, NaNs).
- **$\lambda = 1$** is the only stable knife-edge — and a *learned* matrix never sits exactly there.

The same picture holds with the largest eigenvalue (spectral radius) for the long-run growth rate: a product of a matrix with itself grows like $\rho(W_{hh})^{T-k}$ along its dominant eigenvector.

The $\tanh'$ term only makes vanishing **worse**, never better: $\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2(z) \in (0, 1]$. It equals 1 *only* at $z = 0$ and collapses toward 0 the moment a unit saturates ($\tanh'(1) \approx 0.42$, $\tanh'(2) \approx 0.07$). So the effective per-step gain is $\|W_{hh}\| \cdot \gamma_t \le \|W_{hh}\|$ — it can pull a borderline-stable RNN below 1, but it can never lift a vanishing one back up.

### Worked example: a concrete spectral radius

Pick $W_{hh}$ with largest eigenvalue exactly $0.5$ (e.g. the diagonal matrix $\mathrm{diag}(0.5, 0.3)$, whose $n$-th power is $\mathrm{diag}(0.5^n, 0.3^n)$, so its norm decays like $0.5^n$). With $\tanh' \le 1$ the gradient magnitude after $n = T-k$ steps is at most $0.5^n$:

$$
0.5^{1}=0.5,\quad 0.5^{10}\approx 9.8\times10^{-4},\quad 0.5^{20}\approx 9.5\times10^{-7},\quad 0.5^{50}\approx 8.9\times10^{-16}
$$

After 20 steps the signal is below **0.0001%** of full strength — numerically zero. Now take an eigenvalue of $1.5$ instead ($\mathrm{diag}(1.5, 1.2)$, norm grows like $1.5^n$):

$$
1.5^{1}=1.5,\quad 1.5^{10}\approx 57.7,\quad 1.5^{20}\approx 3.3\times10^{3},\quad 1.5^{50}\approx 6.4\times10^{8}
$$

— a $3300\times$ blow-up by 20 steps. Folding in a *typical* saturated $\tanh' \approx 0.4$ makes the decaying case even more brutal: the per-step gain becomes $0.5 \times 0.4 = 0.2$, so $0.2^{10}\approx 10^{-7}$ — the signal is gone in ten steps.

```python
# Pure-stdlib: no numpy needed. Per-step gain = spectral_radius * max|tanh'|.
import math

def tanh_prime(z):
    return 1 - math.tanh(z)**2

print("max tanh'(z) is at z=0:", round(tanh_prime(0.0), 4),
      "| saturated tanh'(2):", round(tanh_prime(2.0), 4))
print()

for radius in [0.5, 1.0, 1.5]:               # eigenvalue / spectral radius of W_hh
    print(f"spectral radius = {radius}  (tanh' <= 1, so gain <= {radius})")
    for gap in [1, 10, 20, 50]:
        print(f"    gap={gap:>2}: ||grad|| ~ {radius**gap:.3e}")
    print()
```

## Exploding gradients: gradient clipping

Exploding gradients have a cheap, standard fix — **clip** the gradient norm before each update:

$$
\text{if } \|\mathbf{g}\| > c:\quad \mathbf{g} \leftarrow \frac{c}{\|\mathbf{g}\|}\,\mathbf{g}
$$

```python
import torch.nn as nn

# after loss.backward(), before optimizer.step():
nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)
```

<Callout type="info" title="Clipping fixes explosions, not vanishing">
  Gradient clipping rescales an over-large gradient back to a safe norm — it stops explosions and NaNs. It does **nothing** for vanishing gradients: you cannot rescale a signal that has already decayed to zero. That problem needs an architectural fix.
</Callout>

## Truncated BPTT

For very long sequences, unrolling the entire history is too expensive in memory. **Truncated BPTT** backpropagates only through the last $k$ steps, then detaches the hidden state so the graph does not grow forever:

```python
hidden = hidden.detach()   # keep the value, drop the gradient history
```

## The real fix is architectural

You cannot tune your way out of vanishing gradients: the bound $(\|W_{hh}\|\cdot\gamma)^{T-k}$ is exponential in the gap no matter how you initialize, because *every* path back multiplies by $W_{hh}$ and $\tanh'$. The fix is to change the architecture so there is a path whose per-step Jacobian is $\approx 1$ instead of $\|W_{hh}\|\cdot\gamma$. That is exactly what LSTMs and GRUs do: their **cell state** is updated additively ($c_t \approx c_{t-1} + \text{(stuff)}$), so $\partial c_t/\partial c_{t-1} \approx 1$ and the product of Jacobians stays near 1 over long gaps — a gradient **highway**. The next lesson derives those gates.

| Problem | Symptom | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| Exploding gradient | Loss → NaN, wild swings | Gradient clipping |
| Vanishing gradient | Loss plateaus, no long-range learning | LSTM / GRU (gated cells) |

## Common mistakes

1. **Clipping to "fix" vanishing** — clipping only addresses explosions.
2. **Forgetting to `detach()`** the hidden state in truncated BPTT — backprop graph grows without bound and OOMs.
3. **Blaming the learning rate** when the model can't learn long-range patterns — it's the architecture, not the step size.

## Key takeaways

- **BPTT** is backprop on the unrolled network; one weight matrix appears at every step.
- One step's Jacobian is $\partial\mathbf{h}_t/\partial\mathbf{h}_{t-1} = \mathrm{diag}(\tanh'(\mathbf{a}_t))\,W_{hh}$; the gap-$(T-k)$ gradient is a **product of $T-k$** of these.
- That product is bounded by $(\|W_{hh}\|\cdot\gamma)^{T-k}$, so gradients **vanish** (gain below 1) or **explode** (gain above 1) *exponentially* in the gap.
- $\tanh' \le 1$ only ever makes vanishing **worse**, never lifts it back.
- **Gradient clipping** fixes explosions but not vanishing; vanishing needs an architectural fix — gated cells whose Jacobian is $\approx 1$.
