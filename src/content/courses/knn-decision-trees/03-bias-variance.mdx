# The Bias–Variance Tradeoff

You've now met two models with an obvious "complexity dial": the $k$ in [K-Nearest Neighbors](/courses/knn-decision-trees/01-knn) and the depth of a [decision tree](/courses/knn-decision-trees/02-decision-trees). Turn the dial one way and the model ignores real structure; turn it the other way and it hallucinates structure that isn't there. This tension has a name — the **bias–variance tradeoff** — and it governs every model you will ever train.

## The decomposition

For squared error, the expected test error at a point splits into three pieces:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\right] = \underbrace{\text{Bias}^2}_{\text{wrong on average}} + \underbrace{\text{Variance}}_{\text{jumpy across datasets}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}}
$$

- **Bias** — the error your model makes *on average over many training sets*, because it's too simple to represent the truth. A linear fit to a curved relationship is biased no matter how much data you give it.
- **Variance** — how much the fitted model *changes* when you redraw the training set. A wiggly model that chases every data point lands somewhere different every time.
- **Noise** $\sigma^2$ — randomness in the labels themselves. No model escapes it.

The bet: simpler models accept bias to cut variance; complex models accept variance to cut bias. Total error is minimized somewhere in the middle.

## Seeing it in k

For KNN, $k$ controls how much the prediction averages over:

| | $k = 1$ | $k = n$ |
|---|---|---|
| Prediction | copy the nearest point | average everyone |
| Bias | minimal | maximal — predicts the global mean |
| Variance | huge — one noisy neighbor flips the answer | tiny |
| Boundary | jagged islands around noise points | nearly flat |

Small $k$ = low bias, high variance. Large $k$ = high bias, low variance. The best $k$ — found by cross-validation — sits where the two curves cross.

## Seeing it in tree depth

Tree depth is the same dial wearing different clothes:

- **Depth 1** (a stump): one split, massive bias, near-zero variance
- **Unbounded depth**: a private leaf for every training point — zero training error, terrible variance

This is exactly why [bagging and Random Forests](/courses/ensemble-methods/01-bagging-and-random-forests) work: deep trees are low-bias/high-variance, and averaging many of them keeps the low bias while canceling the variance.

<Callout type="tip" title="Diagnosing which problem you have">
  Compare training and validation error. **Both high** → bias problem: your model can't even fit the data it has seen — add capacity or features. **Training low, validation high** → variance problem: regularize, simplify, or get more data. More data fixes variance; it never fixes bias.
</Callout>

<Details summary="Deep dive: the modern footnote — double descent">
  Classical theory predicts a U-shaped test error curve. Very large neural networks complicate the picture ("double descent"): past the *interpolation threshold* — the point where the model can perfectly memorize the training set — test error can start **falling again** as the model grows further. One intuition: among the many zero-training-error solutions a huge model can reach, optimization tends to find the smoothest one, which behaves as if implicitly regularized. The bias–variance lens is still the right starting intuition — just know its edges are an active research area.
</Details>

## Key takeaways

- Test error = bias² + variance + irreducible noise
- Small $k$ / deep trees → low bias, high variance; large $k$ / shallow trees → the reverse
- Diagnose by comparing training vs. validation error; the gap is variance, the floor is bias
- More data cures variance, never bias; ensembles cancel the variance of low-bias models
