# Self-Attention

RNNs process a sequence step by step, squeezing all history into a single hidden state. **Self-attention** throws that bottleneck away: every token directly looks at every other token and decides how much to borrow from each. This one idea — "attention is all you need" — powers GPT, BERT, and essentially every modern language model.

**Real-world examples:** Large language models (GPT, Claude, Llama), machine translation, protein folding (AlphaFold), vision (ViT), code completion.

## The problem with recurrence

An RNN connecting word 1 to word 100 must pass information through 99 sequential steps — slow to train (no parallelism) and lossy (vanishing gradients). Self-attention connects *any* two positions in a **single step**, and computes all positions **in parallel**.

<Callout type="tip" title="Intuition">
  Reading "The animal didn't cross the street because **it** was tired," you instantly know "it" = "animal," not "street." Self-attention lets the model do the same: when encoding "it," it attends strongly to "animal." Each word builds its meaning from the words it finds relevant — looked up by content, not by position.
</Callout>

## Query, Key, Value

Self-attention reframes each token as a tiny database lookup. From each token's embedding $\mathbf{x}_i$ we project **three** vectors via learned matrices $W_Q, W_K, W_V$:

- **Query** $\mathbf{q}_i = W_Q\mathbf{x}_i$ — "what am I looking for?"
- **Key** $\mathbf{k}_i = W_K\mathbf{x}_i$ — "what do I offer?"
- **Value** $\mathbf{v}_i = W_V\mathbf{x}_i$ — "what I'll actually hand over"

A token's query is compared against *every* key to score relevance; those scores weight the values that get summed up.

<Callout type="info" title="The library analogy">
  Your **query** is the topic you want. Every book has a **key** printed on its spine (its subject) and **value** content inside. You match your query against all spines, then read the books in proportion to how well their spine matched — a little from many books, a lot from the best one.
</Callout>

## Scaled dot-product attention

The whole mechanism is one formula:

$$
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
$$

Step by step:

1. **Scores** $QK^{\top}$ — dot product of every query with every key. High dot product = high relevance.
2. **Scale** by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$ — keeps scores from growing with dimension (see below).
3. **Softmax** — turn each row of scores into weights that sum to 1 (the **attention weights**).
4. **Weighted sum** — multiply weights by $V$: each output is a blend of all values, weighted by relevance.

### Why divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$?

Take a query $\mathbf{q}$ and key $\mathbf{k}$ whose components are independent with mean $0$ and variance $1$. Their dot product is a sum of $d_k$ independent terms:

$$
\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i .
$$

Each term $q_i k_i$ has mean $\mathbb{E}[q_i k_i] = \mathbb{E}[q_i]\,\mathbb{E}[k_i] = 0$ and variance $\operatorname{Var}(q_i k_i) = \mathbb{E}[q_i^2]\,\mathbb{E}[k_i^2] = 1\cdot 1 = 1$. Summing $d_k$ independent unit-variance terms gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} 1 = d_k ,
$$

so the typical magnitude of a raw score grows like $\sqrt{d_k}$. For large $d_k$ those scores get large, pushing softmax into saturated regions where one weight $\approx 1$ and the rest $\approx 0$ — and where the gradient is nearly zero, so learning stalls. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ rescales the variance back to $1$ (since $\operatorname{Var}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}\,\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}) = \tfrac{1}{d_k}\cdot d_k = 1$), keeping softmax in a well-behaved range regardless of dimension.

<Callout type="info" title="Check the variance yourself">
  Sampling 20,000 random $\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}$ pairs of unit-variance Gaussians gives empirical $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}) \approx 2.0$ for $d_k=2$, $\approx 8.0$ for $d_k=8$, $\approx 64$ for $d_k=64$ — matching $d_k$ exactly. The notebook reproduces this.
</Callout>

### Worked example (by hand)

Three tokens, $d_k = 2$. We give the projected vectors directly (each **row** is one token):

$$
Q = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix},\quad
K = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix},\quad
V = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 10 & 10 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Step 1 — Scores $QK^{\top}$.** Entry $(i,j)$ is $\mathbf{q}_i\cdot\mathbf{k}_j$. For example, row 1 ($\mathbf{q}_1=[2,0]$) against the three keys gives $[2,0]\cdot[2,0]=4$, $[2,0]\cdot[0,2]=0$, $[2,0]\cdot[1,1]=2$:

$$
QK^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 4 & 2 \\ 4 & 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Step 2 — Scale by $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\approx 0.707$.** Using $\tfrac{4}{\sqrt2}=2\sqrt2\approx2.828$ and $\tfrac{2}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt2\approx1.414$:

$$
\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt 2} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.828 & 0 & 1.414 \\ 0 & 2.828 & 1.414 \\ 2.828 & 2.828 & 2.828 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Step 3 — Softmax each row.** Take $e^{s}$ of each entry, then divide by the row sum. Row 1:

$$
e^{2.828}=16.92,\quad e^{0}=1,\quad e^{1.414}=4.11,\quad \text{sum}=22.03,
$$
$$
\text{softmax(row 1)} = \tfrac{1}{22.03}[16.92,\,1,\,4.11] = [0.768,\,0.045,\,0.187].
$$

Row 3 is special: all three scaled scores are **equal** ($2.828$), so $e^{s}$ is the same for each and softmax is exactly uniform: $[\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13]=[0.333,0.333,0.333]$. By symmetry row 2 mirrors row 1: $[0.045,\,0.768,\,0.187]$.

$$
\text{weights} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.768 & 0.045 & 0.187 \\ 0.045 & 0.768 & 0.187 \\ 0.333 & 0.333 & 0.333 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{(every row sums to 1).}
$$

**Step 4 — Weighted sum of values, $\text{weights}\cdot V$.** Row 1's output blends the value rows by its weights:

$$
0.768[1,0] + 0.045[0,1] + 0.187[10,10] = [0.768+1.867,\ 0.045+1.867] = [2.635,\,1.912].
$$

Row 3, with uniform weights, is just the average of the value rows: $\tfrac13([1,0]+[0,1]+[10,10]) = [3.667,\,3.667]$. The full output:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 2.635 & 1.912 \\ 1.912 & 2.635 \\ 3.667 & 3.667 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Token 1 attends mostly to **key 1** (its strongest match) but the large value of token 3 still pulls its output upward; token 3, matching everything equally, returns the plain average. The block below recomputes all of this in pure Python so you can check every number.

```python
import math

def softmax(row):
    e = [math.exp(s) for s in row]
    z = sum(e)
    return [x / z for x in e]

# Each row is one token's projected vector (d_k = 2)
Q = [[2, 0], [0, 2], [2, 2]]
K = [[2, 0], [0, 2], [1, 1]]
V = [[1, 0], [0, 1], [10, 10]]
d_k = 2

# Step 1: scores QKᵀ  (dot product of every query with every key)
scores = [[sum(q[d] * k[d] for d in range(d_k)) for k in K] for q in Q]
# Step 2: scale, then Step 3: softmax each row
scaled  = [[s / math.sqrt(d_k) for s in row] for row in scores]
weights = [softmax(row) for row in scaled]
# Step 4: weighted sum of value rows
out = [[sum(weights[i][j] * V[j][d] for j in range(3)) for d in range(d_k)]
       for i in range(3)]

print("scores   :", scores)                       # [[4,0,2],[0,4,2],[4,4,4]]
print("weights  :", [[round(w, 3) for w in r] for r in weights])
print("row sums :", [round(sum(r), 6) for r in weights])  # all 1.0
print("output   :", [[round(o, 3) for o in r] for r in out])
```

Running it prints the matrices above, including `weights row 1 = [0.768, 0.045, 0.187]` and `output row 3 = [3.667, 3.667]`.

## In PyTorch

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

X = torch.randn(1, 4, 8)                   # (batch, seq, d_model)
Wq = torch.randn(8, 8); Wk = torch.randn(8, 8); Wv = torch.randn(8, 8)
Q, K, V = X @ Wq, X @ Wk, X @ Wv

# PyTorch 2.0+ fused, numerically stable attention:
out = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(Q, K, V)
print(out.shape)                           # (1, 4, 8)
```

## Causal (masked) attention

In a language model predicting the next token, position $t$ must **not** peek at future positions. We enforce this by adding $-\infty$ to scores for future positions *before* the softmax, so their weights become 0:

```python
seq = 4
mask = torch.triu(torch.ones(seq, seq), diagonal=1).bool()  # upper triangle
scores = scores.masked_fill(mask, float('-inf'))            # future → -inf
```

This **causal mask** is what makes GPT-style autoregressive generation possible.

## Why attention won

| | RNN | Self-Attention |
|---|-----|----------------|
| Path length between any two tokens | $O(n)$ | $O(1)$ |
| Parallelizable across sequence? | No | Yes |
| Long-range dependencies | Hard (vanishing) | Direct |
| Compute per layer | $O(n \cdot d^2)$ | $O(n^2 \cdot d)$ |

The cost: attention is **quadratic** in sequence length ($n^2$ scores). For very long sequences that becomes the bottleneck — hence sparse/linear-attention research. But the constant path length and full parallelism made it the clear winner for training at scale.

## Common mistakes

1. **Forgetting the scale factor** $\sqrt{d_k}$ — softmax saturates, gradients vanish, training stalls.
2. **Omitting the causal mask** in a generative model — the model "cheats" by seeing the answer, then fails at inference.
3. **Confusing the dimensions** — attention weights are $n\times n$ (token-to-token), the output is $n\times d_v$ (same shape as input).

## Key takeaways

- Self-attention lets every token attend to every other in **one parallel step**.
- **Query·Key** scores, scaled by $\sqrt{d_k}$ and softmaxed, weight the **Values**.
- A **causal mask** enables autoregressive (GPT-style) generation.
- Cost is **quadratic** in sequence length — the main scaling bottleneck.
