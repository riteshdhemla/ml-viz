# Correlation, Causation & Confounding

Machine learning is extraordinary at finding **correlations** — but most decisions we actually care about are **causal**: *will this treatment help this patient? will this feature change increase retention?* A model can predict perfectly and still be useless (or dangerous) for these questions, because correlation and causation come apart in systematic, predictable ways. This lesson builds the intuition for *why*, which the next lesson formalizes. Confounding is fundamentally a **data** problem in the [ML project loop](/wiki/ml-project-loop): a perfect optimizer on a perfectly-fit model still gives the wrong answer to a causal question if the *data-generating process* hides a common cause. No amount of hypothesis-space or optimization work fixes a broken causal structure.

**Real-world examples:** "does this drug work?" (vs. is it correlated with recovery), "did the marketing campaign cause sales?", deciding whether a model's feature is actionable, and avoiding policies that optimize a proxy while harming the real goal.

## Prediction is not causation

A supervised model learns $P(Y \mid X)$ — what $Y$ tends to be *when we observe* $X$. A causal question asks what $Y$ would be *if we intervened to set* $X$ — written $P(Y \mid \text{do}(X))$. These are different:

$$
P(Y \mid X = x) \;\;\neq\;\; P(Y \mid \text{do}(X = x)) \quad\text{in general}
$$

Observing that hospital patients on a certain drug recover more often (correlation) does **not** mean giving the drug *causes* recovery — maybe doctors prescribe it to healthier patients. The model that predicts recovery from "is on drug" is accurate and causally wrong at the same time.

## Confounding: the core problem

A **confounder** is a variable that influences *both* the supposed cause and the effect, creating a correlation that isn't causal:

```text
        confounder Z (e.g. patient health)
        ╱                          ╲
       ▼                            ▼
   treatment X  ───?───►  outcome Y
```

Health $Z$ drives *both* who gets the treatment $X$ and who recovers $Y$, so $X$ and $Y$ are correlated even if $X$ does nothing. The classic spurious examples — ice-cream sales correlate with drownings (confounder: hot weather), shoe size correlates with reading ability in children (confounder: age) — are all this structure.

<Callout type="warning" title="You cannot detect confounding from the data alone">
  No amount of staring at $X$ and $Y$ reveals whether a confounder is inflating their correlation — the data look identical whether the link is causal or confounded. You need either an **experiment** or **assumptions about the causal structure** (a graph). This is why causal inference needs more than statistics: it needs a model of *how the data was generated*.
</Callout>

## Simpson's paradox

The most vivid demonstration: an association can **reverse** when you account for a confounder. A treatment can appear *worse* overall yet be *better* in every subgroup — because the subgroups had different base rates and different treatment assignment.

$$
P(\text{recover}\mid\text{treated}) < P(\text{recover}\mid\text{untreated})
\quad\text{overall, yet the reverse within every subgroup}
$$

The aggregate and the stratified analyses disagree, and *which one is correct depends on the causal structure* — whether the grouping variable is a confounder (adjust for it) or a mediator (don't). Statistics alone can't tell you; causal reasoning can.

<SimpsonsParadoxViz />

The generating process here is fixed and known: the dose helps **every** patient by exactly $+0.90$ recovery points per unit, and severity independently costs $2.5$ points. The slider changes one thing only — how strongly severity decides *who gets which dose*. Three things are worth doing with it:

1. **Start at 0.** Nothing is confounded yet, and the pooled fit reports $+0.898$ against a true $+0.90$. The naive analysis is not wrong in principle; it is wrong under a particular structure.
2. **Drag it up.** Near $0.35$ the pooled slope crosses zero, and past that the aggregate says the drug *harms* patients while all three stratum lines still slope up. The three within-stratum estimates never move — they sit at $+0.896$ the entire time. Nothing about the treatment changed; only who received it.
3. **Press "randomise the dose".** The strata still differ in recovery, and the slider still reads whatever you left it at — but the pooled fit snaps back to the truth. Randomisation doesn't measure the confounder or adjust for it. It deletes the arrow that made adjustment necessary.

The third point is the one worth carrying: adjustment recovers the effect *if* you knew to adjust, and randomisation recovers it *without* you knowing anything. That asymmetry is why the RCT is the gold standard and not merely a convenience.

## Why ML cares

- **Actionability.** A feature can be highly predictive yet useless to act on (it's an effect, not a cause, or it's confounded). Deciding *what to change* is causal.
- **Robustness / distribution shift.** Correlations that ride on a confounder break when the confounder's distribution shifts; causal relationships are more stable.
- **Fairness.** Whether a model is "using" a protected attribute is fundamentally a causal question about paths, not a correlation.
- **Optimizing proxies.** Pushing on a correlate of the goal (not a cause) can move the metric while the real objective stagnates or worsens.

## How causation is actually established

- **Randomized controlled trial (RCT).** Randomly assign the treatment. Randomization **severs the arrow from any confounder into $X$** — treated and untreated groups are comparable in expectation, so the observed difference *is* the causal effect. This is the gold standard, and it's exactly why A/B tests randomize.
- **Adjustment from observational data.** When you can't experiment, *if* you can identify and measure the confounders, you can statistically adjust for them (stratify / control) to recover the causal effect — under assumptions covered next lesson.

## Common mistakes

1. **Reading a predictive coefficient as a causal effect.** A regression weight is not "the effect of changing $X$" unless confounding is handled.
2. **Controlling for the wrong variable.** Adjusting for a confounder removes bias; adjusting for a *mediator* or *collider* introduces it. The causal structure decides.
3. **Trusting aggregate associations.** Simpson's paradox shows aggregates can reverse the truth; the right stratification depends on causal roles.
4. **Assuming more data fixes it.** Confounding bias does not shrink with sample size — a billion confounded observations are still confounded.

## Key takeaways

- Prediction learns $P(Y\mid X)$; causation concerns $P(Y\mid \text{do}(X))$ — **observing vs. intervening** — and they differ in general.
- A **confounder** drives both treatment and outcome, creating non-causal correlation; you **cannot detect it from $X,Y$ alone** — you need experiments or structural assumptions.
- **Simpson's paradox**: associations can reverse under stratification, and the correct analysis depends on the **causal roles** of variables.
- **Randomization** (RCT/A-B test) severs confounding and is the gold standard; otherwise recover effects by **adjusting** for measured confounders.
