# Diversity, Cold Start & Exploration

Optimizing NDCG@10 can produce a recommender that is technically accurate but practically terrible: it recommends the same ten items to everyone, ignores new users and products, and traps users in an ever-narrowing taste bubble. This lesson covers the three structural problems every production recommender must address beyond raw accuracy.

**Real-world examples:** Spotify's "Discover Weekly" (diversity by design), Netflix's causal inference for exploration, Pinterest's cold-start for new Pins, LinkedIn's cold-start for new job listings, Airbnb's neighborhood for new property listings.

## The cold start problem

Cold start occurs when a user or item has **too few interactions** to produce reliable embeddings. It comes in three flavors:

| Flavor | Problem | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **New user** | No interaction history | A just-signed-up user on Spotify |
| **New item** | No engagement data yet | A freshly listed product on Etsy |
| **New context** | Unseen feature combination | A user in a new geographic market |

### Content-based fallback

The most practical cold-start remedy: represent items by their **features** (text description, category, price, image embedding) rather than interaction-derived embeddings. When interaction data is sparse, fall back to content similarity.

For a new item $i$ with features $\mathbf{f}_i$, project to the embedding space:

$$
\mathbf{v}_i^{\text{cold}} = W_{\text{proj}}\,\mathbf{f}_i
$$

The projection $W_{\text{proj}}$ is learned during training to align content features with interaction-trained embeddings. At Airbnb, new listings' content embeddings (images, description text, amenities) are used immediately on day 0; they converge to interaction-based embeddings over ~2 weeks as bookings accumulate.

### Meta-learning for fast user adaptation

**MAML (Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning)** trains a base model such that a small number of gradient steps on a new user's first few interactions produces a high-quality personalized model. In recommendation:

$$
\theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \sum_u \mathcal{L}\left(f_{\theta - \alpha \nabla \mathcal{L}(f_\theta, \mathcal{D}_u^{\text{support}})},\, \mathcal{D}_u^{\text{query}}\right)
$$

The inner loop updates for user $u$ on their first $k$ interactions; the outer loop optimizes the initialization $\theta$ to be maximally "fast-adaptable". Effective for platforms where users are highly heterogeneous and the onboarding sequence is short.

### Onboarding intent capture

Many platforms explicitly **ask** new users for preferences at signup: Spotify's "pick your favorite artists," Tinder's "swipe on these profiles." These explicit signals bootstrap the embedding even before any implicit interaction occurs. The key is to show items that **maximally partition the latent preference space** — items that are highly discriminative — not just the most popular ones.

<Callout type="tip" title="Warm-up strategy at Netflix">
  Netflix uses a combination: new users see a set of titles chosen to elicit a preference signal (explicit rating request), and in parallel are assigned to a "taste cluster" of existing users whose early ratings matched the new user's profile. The cluster's collaborative signal is used while the new user's personal data accumulates.
</Callout>

## Diversity beyond accuracy

A ranker optimizing average relevance will recommend the best item over and over — serving only the dominant taste cluster and creating a **filter bubble**. Diversity metrics measure how varied a recommendation list is:

- **Intra-list diversity (ILD):** average pairwise dissimilarity of recommended items. $\text{ILD} = \frac{2}{K(K-1)}\sum_{i \neq j} d(i,j)$.
- **Coverage:** fraction of the item catalog that ever appears in recommendations across users.
- **Serendipity:** relevance-weighted surprise — items that are both liked and unexpected.

### Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR)

MMR re-ranks a candidate list to balance relevance and novelty. At each step, select the item that maximizes:

$$
\text{MMR}(i) = \lambda \cdot r_i - (1-\lambda) \cdot \max_{j \in S} \text{sim}(i, j)
$$

where $r_i$ is the relevance score, $S$ is the set of already-selected items, and $\text{sim}$ is embedding cosine similarity. $\lambda \in [0,1]$ trades off relevance vs diversity. A greedy selection of the top-$K$ items via MMR takes $O(KN)$ and is trivially parallelizable.

Spotify applies MMR-style diversification at the re-ranking stage: after retrieval and ranking, the list is passed through a diversity optimizer that ensures no two consecutive songs share the same artist, genre, or BPM bracket.

### Determinantal Point Processes (DPP)

DPPs assign higher probability to subsets of items that are both **high quality** and **mutually diverse**:

$$
P(S) \propto \det(L_S)
$$

where $L_{ij} = r_i^{1/2} \,\text{sim}(i,j)\, r_j^{1/2}$ (the kernel matrix). The determinant is large when items have high quality ($r_i$ large) and low mutual similarity (off-diagonal terms small). DPPs are mathematically elegant but expensive to sample from exactly; in practice, greedy approximations achieve > 90% of optimal diversity in $O(K^2 N)$.

## Exploration vs exploitation

Even with a well-trained model, there are items the model *thinks* aren't relevant but would actually be enjoyed — because there's no data to learn from. **Exploration** is the deliberate injection of uncertainty-resolved queries.

### Multi-armed bandits recap

The bandit problem (treated in full in [Exploration Strategies & Model-Based RL](/courses/reinforcement-learning/05-exploration-and-model-based)): $K$ arms with unknown reward distributions. At each step, pull an arm to get a reward and update beliefs. The fundamental tension: **exploit** the current best arm, or **explore** an uncertain arm to reduce uncertainty.

**Upper Confidence Bound (UCB):** Add an exploration bonus proportional to uncertainty:

$$
a_t = \arg\max_i \left[\hat{\mu}_i + c\sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{n_i}}\right]
$$

Items pulled fewer times ($n_i$ small) get larger bonuses, ensuring every item gets explored eventually. UCB is provably sublinear in regret for stationary distributions.

**Thompson Sampling:** Maintain a posterior distribution $P(\mu_i)$ over each arm's mean. At each step, sample $\tilde{\mu}_i \sim P(\mu_i)$ and pull the arm with the highest sample. Empirically, Thompson Sampling often outperforms UCB in practice because it balances exploration more smoothly.

### Counterfactual evaluation

How do you evaluate whether exploration helped, without running a live A/B test? **Inverse Propensity Scoring (IPS)** reweights offline logged data to estimate counterfactual policy performance:

$$
\hat{R}(\pi) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^N \frac{\pi(a_t | s_t)}{\pi_0(a_t | s_t)}\, r_t
$$

where $\pi_0$ is the logging policy and $\pi$ is the new policy being evaluated. Items the new policy would have chosen but the logging policy rarely showed get upweighted. Netflix and Lyft use IPS-style offline evaluation to estimate the lift from exploration strategies before committing to an A/B test.

## Common mistakes

1. **Optimizing NDCG alone.** Accuracy-only ranking converges on the dominant taste cluster — measure coverage and intra-list diversity alongside relevance.
2. **Onboarding with the most popular items.** Popular items are the least discriminative; ask about items that partition the preference space.
3. **Evaluating a new policy only on logged data without propensities.** Naive offline replay is biased toward the logging policy; use IPS (or an A/B test).

## Key takeaways

- **Cold start** requires falling back to content-based similarity (features, not interactions); meta-learning and explicit onboarding signals are the best tools for new users.
- **Diversity** is not a soft goal — filter bubbles reduce long-term retention. MMR (greedy sequential selection) and DPPs (probabilistic quality-plus-diversity) are the main algorithmic tools.
- **Exploration** via UCB or Thompson Sampling prevents the model from being trapped by its own past recommendations; counterfactual evaluation (IPS) lets you assess exploration strategies offline.
