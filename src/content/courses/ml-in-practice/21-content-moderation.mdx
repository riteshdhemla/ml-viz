# Content Moderation at Scale

Every platform with user-generated content faces the same problem: a small fraction of content violates policies (spam, hate speech, misinformation, explicit imagery, discriminatory listings) and it must be detected and acted on before it harms users or creates legal liability. The challenge is scale: Pinterest moderates billions of pins, YouTube hundreds of hours per minute of video, DoorDash thousands of restaurant photos per day. Human review alone is impossible; ML must handle the load.

**Real-world examples:** Pinterest (detect violative content at scale), Zillow (detect discriminatory language in listings), DoorDash (detect inappropriate restaurant photos), Etsy (policy violation detection), Snap (ad content review at scale), Roblox (in-game content moderation), LinkedIn (spam and fake profile detection), Bumble (detect offensive images).

## The framing: multilabel classification

Most content moderation is a **multilabel classification** problem: a single piece of content can simultaneously violate multiple policies (e.g., spam AND misleading). The label space is typically:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Hate speech / harassment | Slurs, threats, bullying |
| Adult / explicit | NSFW imagery, sexual content |
| Spam / inauthentic | Fake reviews, bot accounts, coordinated inauthentic behavior |
| Misinformation | Health claims, electoral interference |
| Illegal content | CSAM, drug sales, weapons |
| Policy-specific | Platform-specific rules (e.g., Zillow's fair housing violations) |

Each category often has subcategories requiring fine-grained classifiers. At Pinterest, the content policy is represented as a **taxonomy of ~200 labels** organized in a hierarchy; a single model predicts scores across all leaf nodes simultaneously.

## Labeling: the fundamental bottleneck

ML models can only be as good as their training labels. Content moderation labels are:

1. **Subjective** — is this image "suggestive" or just "artistic"? Annotators disagree.
2. **Expert-intensive** — hate speech in local dialects requires native-speaker annotators.
3. **Evolving** — new slang, new manipulation tactics, new platform features create label drift.

### Inter-annotator agreement

Always measure **inter-annotator agreement** (Cohen's kappa, Krippendorff's alpha) before computing model metrics. If kappa < 0.4 (poor agreement), the task needs better annotation guidelines or task decomposition before model training.

The usual phrasing of this rule ("a model can't outperform human agreement") is worth stating more precisely, because what it constrains is your *measurement*, not your model. Symmetric annotator noise does not move a decision boundary; averaging washes it out. What it does is corrupt the ruler you evaluate with, since your test labels are drawn from the same disagreeing annotators. In the walkthrough below, sweeping annotator noise leaves the model's true AUC flat at 0.96 while the AUC you would *report* falls to 0.84. Low kappa is a reason to build an adjudicated gold set before trusting any metric — not a reason to conclude the model is bad.

That holds for noise that is symmetric and independent of the content. Annotator bias that *correlates* with the content (the fairness case discussed below) is a different failure, and it does move the boundary.

### Active learning

Labeling every piece of content is prohibitive. **Active learning** selects the most informative unlabeled samples for human review:

- **Uncertainty sampling**: query samples where the model is least confident (entropy, least confidence, margin sampling).
- **Diversity sampling**: cluster the unlabeled pool and sample from each cluster to avoid labeling near-duplicates.
- **Expected model change**: select samples that would change the current model most if labeled.

DoorDash uses uncertainty sampling for restaurant photo moderation: the image classifier's top-2 predicted categories' confidence gap triggers manual review when small, while high-confidence predictions are auto-approved or auto-rejected.

<Callout type="tip" title="Label studios and task decomposition">
  Complex policies decompose into simpler sub-tasks. Instead of "is this content harmful?" ask: (1) Is there a person in the image? (2) Is the person unclothed? (3) Is this in a non-sexual context? Each sub-task has higher annotator agreement and maps to a specific policy rule. The final decision is a rule over the sub-task outputs.
</Callout>

## Model architectures

### Text content

Standard pipeline: fine-tune a BERT-family model on the policy taxonomy. Key choices:

- **Hierarchical multi-label head**: predict top-level category, then fine-grained subcategory conditional on the top prediction.
- **Focal loss** to handle class imbalance: $\text{FL}(p_t) = -(1-p_t)^\gamma \log p_t$, where the $(1-p_t)^\gamma$ factor downweights well-classified easy examples and upweights hard ones.
- **Cross-lingual models** (mBERT, XLM-R) for platforms with global user bases; or translate-then-classify using an MT system.

Zillow's discriminatory listing detector fine-tunes a language model on the Fair Housing Act's protected categories and flags listings with discriminatory language about race, religion, family status, etc.

### Image content

Vision Transformers (ViT) or ResNet fine-tuned on policy-specific data. Key considerations:

- **Multimodal signals**: a photo that is fine in isolation may be policy-violating when combined with its caption. Snap and Roblox use vision-language models (CLIP embeddings) to detect content where the image-text combination is the violation.
- **Hash-based matching**: for known-bad content (CSAM, specific extremist imagery), use perceptual hashes (PhotoDNA, pHash) for exact/near-duplicate detection — much cheaper and more reliable than ML for material that has already been verified.

## Decision policies and thresholds

The model produces a score; a **threshold** converts it to an action. Common action tiers:

| Threshold | Action | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| score > 0.99 | **Auto-remove** | CSAM (zero tolerance), clear spam |
| 0.85–0.99 | **Demote / hide** | Reduce distribution without removal |
| 0.60–0.85 | **Human review queue** | Borderline cases requiring judgment |
| score < 0.60 | **Approve** | Passes through |

The threshold is a **policy decision**, not an ML decision. Business, legal, and trust & safety teams set the false-positive tolerance; ML optimizes subject to that constraint. At Pinterest, different thresholds apply in different contexts: search results apply a stricter threshold than a user's own board because visibility is higher.

### Appeals and feedback loops

Every removal generates an appeal signal. Users who appeal removed content and win provide **high-quality corrective labels**: the model was wrong on exactly those cases. LinkedIn feeds winning appeals back into the training set with a corrective label and retrains weekly.

The appeal channel is a **one-sided sampler**, and that shapes what you can ever know. It observes only decisions on the action side of the threshold (in the simulation below, about 2% of all decisions), so it measures precision continuously and for free, and measures recall not at all. Nobody appeals a piece of harmful content that was quietly approved. Recall requires a **separately drawn audit sample** of approved content, reviewed on purpose; it will never arrive on its own.

Three results worth carrying out of it. **The agreement ceiling binds your metric, not your model** (0.96 true against 0.84 reported at kappa 0.19). **Active learning buys about 4× on labels, and only early** — uncertainty sampling reaches at 100 labels what random needs 400 for, then both flatten at the score you would get from labelling everything. And **the tier table barely fires**: at ~5% prevalence the model's single highest score across 20,000 items is about 0.99, so an "auto-remove above 0.99" rule catches almost nothing, while the action line that decides real volume sits far lower and trades recall against precision very steeply.

<Callout type="warning" title="Model gaming and adversarial content">
  As soon as a moderation model is deployed, bad actors probe it: they add invisible characters to evade text classifiers, add benign borders around violating images, use code words. Moderation models must be **continuously retrained** on recent violation examples and **red-teamed** regularly by a trust & safety team.
</Callout>

## Fairness in moderation

Automated content moderation systems can have **disparate impact** across protected groups: a hate speech detector may flag African American Vernacular English at higher rates than white-authored text containing similar content. At Bumble and LinkedIn, fairness audits measure false-positive rates by demographic group, and models are recalibrated or retrained to equalize error rates.

## The moderation system architecture

```
User-generated content (text/image/video)
      ↓
Hash-based deduplication (pHash, PhotoDNA)
      ↓
ML classifier (fine-tuned BERT/ViT)
      ↓
Score → action tier:
  ├─ Auto-remove (> 0.99)
  ├─ Demote + review queue (0.60–0.99)
  └─ Approve (< 0.60)
      ↓
Human review (resolves borderline, trains active learning)
      ↓
Appeals processing → feedback loop
      ↓
Weekly retraining with appeals + new violation examples
```

## Key takeaways

- Content moderation is **multilabel classification** over a policy taxonomy; use focal loss to handle extreme class imbalance.
- **Human labeling quality** (measured by inter-annotator agreement) is the ceiling on model performance; decompose complex policies into simpler sub-tasks.
- **Active learning** (uncertainty + diversity sampling) dramatically reduces labeling cost by querying only informative examples.
- The threshold is a **policy decision**: ML provides scores; legal, trust & safety, and product teams set the action tiers based on acceptable false-positive rates.
- Adversarial adaptation requires continuous retraining and red-teaming.
