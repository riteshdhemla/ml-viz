# Gradient Descent

Training a neural network means finding weights that minimize a **loss function** — a number that measures how wrong the network's predictions are. Gradient descent is the algorithm that does this, and it is the engine behind essentially all of deep learning.

**Real-world examples:** Every trained model — image classifiers, language models, recommenders — was fit by some variant of gradient descent.

## The core idea

Imagine standing on a hilly landscape in fog. You can feel the slope under your feet. The steepest downhill direction is the **negative gradient**. Take a small step that way. Repeat until you're in a valley.

$$
w \leftarrow w - \eta \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
$$

Here $\eta$ is the **learning rate** — how big each step is. We'll see exactly how it controls speed versus stability after the worked example below.

### Warm-up: a single-parameter bowl

Loss $L(w) = (w - 5)^2$, so the gradient is $\frac{dL}{dw} = 2(w - 5)$. Starting at $w = 2$ with $\eta = 0.1$:

$$
\nabla = 2(2 - 5) = -6 \quad\Rightarrow\quad w \leftarrow 2 - 0.1\,(-6) = 2.6
$$

The negative gradient is positive-pushing here, so $w$ moves toward 5, the minimum. Each step shrinks the distance to the optimum. That is the whole mechanic — now let's apply it to a real model.

## Worked example: fitting a line with MSE

Almost every "loss" you'll meet is the **mean squared error (MSE)** of a model's predictions. Let's derive the update rule for the simplest model, a straight line, and then run two iterations entirely by hand.

### 1. The model and the loss

Model with two parameters, a slope $w$ and an intercept $b$:

$$
\hat{y}_i = w\,x_i + b
$$

Over $n$ training examples $(x_i, y_i)$, the MSE loss is the average squared gap between prediction and target:

$$
L(w, b) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\hat{y}_i - y_i\bigr)^2
= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(w x_i + b - y_i\bigr)^2
$$

### 2. Deriving the gradients

We need $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}$ and $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b}$. Apply the chain rule to one term $\bigl(w x_i + b - y_i\bigr)^2$. Let $r_i = w x_i + b - y_i$ be the **residual**. The outer derivative of $r_i^2$ is $2 r_i$; the inner derivative of $r_i$ with respect to $w$ is $x_i$, and with respect to $b$ is $1$:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\bigl(r_i^2\bigr) = 2 r_i \cdot x_i,
\qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial b}\bigl(r_i^2\bigr) = 2 r_i \cdot 1
$$

Averaging over all $n$ examples gives the full gradients:

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(w x_i + b - y_i\bigr)\,x_i\;}
\qquad
\boxed{\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(w x_i + b - y_i\bigr)\;}
$$

The update rule applies the same step to both parameters at once:

$$
w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w},
\qquad
b \leftarrow b - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}
$$

### 3. The data

Three points that lie exactly on the line $y = 2x + 1$ (so we know the answer is $w = 2$, $b = 1$):

| $x_i$ | $y_i$ |
|-------|-------|
| 1 | 3 |
| 2 | 5 |
| 3 | 7 |

We start from $w = 0$, $b = 0$ with learning rate $\eta = 0.1$. At the start the predictions are all $0$, so the residuals are $r = (0-3,\,0-5,\,0-7) = (-3, -5, -7)$ and the loss is

$$
L = \frac{(-3)^2 + (-5)^2 + (-7)^2}{3} = \frac{9 + 25 + 49}{3} = \frac{83}{3} \approx 27.67
$$

### 4. Iteration 1, by hand

With $w = 0, b = 0$ the residuals are $r_i = -y_i$. Plug into the boxed formulas:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{2}{3}\Bigl[(-3)(1) + (-5)(2) + (-7)(3)\Bigr]
= \frac{2}{3}(-3 - 10 - 21) = \frac{2}{3}(-34) = -\frac{68}{3} \approx -22.67
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac{2}{3}\Bigl[(-3) + (-5) + (-7)\Bigr] = \frac{2}{3}(-15) = -10
$$

Now step downhill (subtract $\eta$ times each gradient):

$$
w \leftarrow 0 - 0.1\,(-22.67) = 2.267,
\qquad
b \leftarrow 0 - 0.1\,(-10) = 1.0
$$

The new loss is $L \approx 0.332$ — a single step took us from $27.67$ down to $0.33$ because the gradients were large.

### 5. Iteration 2, by hand

Recompute the residuals with $w = 2.267, b = 1.0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_1 &= 2.267(1) + 1 - 3 = 0.267 \\
r_2 &= 2.267(2) + 1 - 5 = 0.533 \\
r_3 &= 2.267(3) + 1 - 7 = 0.800
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{2}{3}\bigl[0.267(1) + 0.533(2) + 0.800(3)\bigr]
= \frac{2}{3}(3.733) \approx 2.489
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac{2}{3}\bigl[0.267 + 0.533 + 0.800\bigr] = \frac{2}{3}(1.6) \approx 1.067
$$

Step again:

$$
w \leftarrow 2.267 - 0.1\,(2.489) = 2.018,
\qquad
b \leftarrow 1.0 - 0.1\,(1.067) = 0.893
$$

Loss drops to $L \approx 0.0053$. Notice the gradients shrank from $\sim 23$ to $\sim 2.5$: **as you approach the minimum the slope flattens, so the steps automatically get smaller.** Keep iterating and $(w, b)$ converges to exactly $(2, 1)$.

```python
# Linear-model MSE gradient descent — reproduces the hand calculation above.
data = [(1, 3), (2, 5), (3, 7)]   # points on y = 2x + 1

def gradients(w, b):
    n = len(data)
    dw = (2 / n) * sum((w * x + b - y) * x for x, y in data)
    db = (2 / n) * sum((w * x + b - y)     for x, y in data)
    return dw, db

def loss(w, b):
    n = len(data)
    return sum((w * x + b - y) ** 2 for x, y in data) / n

w, b, lr = 0.0, 0.0, 0.1
for step in range(2):
    dw, db = gradients(w, b)
    w, b = w - lr * dw, b - lr * db
    print("step {}: w={:.3f} b={:.3f} L={:.4f}".format(step + 1, w, b, loss(w, b)))
# step 1: w=2.267 b=1.000 L=0.3319
# step 2: w=2.018 b=0.893 L=0.0053
```

## The learning rate, concretely

The learning rate $\eta$ scales every step. Using the same line-fitting problem, here is what 50 steps from $(w,b)=(0,0)$ produce for different $\eta$:

| $\eta$ | Behavior after 50 steps | Final loss |
|--------|--------------------------|------------|
| $0.001$ | Crawls — barely moved ($w \approx 0.87$) | $\approx 9.07$ |
| $0.1$ | Converges smoothly to $(2, 1)$ | $\approx 0.0001$ |
| $0.5$ | **Overshoots and diverges** to NaN | $\infty$ |

<Callout type="warning" title="Learning rate matters">
  Too large: each step overshoots the minimum and lands further out than it
  started, so the loss grows every step and explodes to NaN. Too small:
  training is stable but painfully slow and can stall in shallow minima. When
  training diverges, the first fix is to lower $\eta$ by 10×.
</Callout>

In practice $\eta$ is rarely held constant — it is *scheduled* to start large and shrink, often with a warmup ramp first:

## Batch, stochastic, and mini-batch

In the example above we summed the gradient over **all three** points before each step. That is *full-batch* gradient descent. With millions of examples, summing over the whole dataset for a single step is far too slow, so we estimate the gradient from a subset. The number of examples per step defines the variant:

| Variant | Examples per step | Trade-off |
|---------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Batch** | All ($n$) | Exact gradient, smooth path, slow & memory-heavy |
| **Stochastic (SGD)** | One | Noisy, cheap, the noise can jump out of shallow minima |
| **Mini-batch** | 32–512 | Best of both — the practical default |

The key insight: a mini-batch gradient is an **unbiased estimate** of the full gradient. On average it points the right way; any single step is noisy, but over many steps the noise averages out while each step costs a fraction of the full-batch price. With $n$ examples and batch size $B$, one pass over the data (an **epoch**) does $n/B$ updates instead of just one — so mini-batch SGD makes far more progress per pass.

## Beyond vanilla: momentum and Adam

Plain SGD crawls through narrow **ravines** — regions where the loss is very steep in one direction (say, along $w_1$) but nearly flat in another (say, along $w_2$). It bounces back and forth across the steep walls while barely advancing along the flat valley floor. Smarter optimizers fix this:

- **Momentum** keeps a running **velocity** $v$ that accumulates past gradients, so consistent downhill directions build up speed while side-to-side oscillations cancel out:
  $$
  v \leftarrow \beta v - \eta\,\nabla L, \qquad w \leftarrow w + v
  $$
  The decay $\beta$ (typically $0.9$) sets how much past momentum carries over. **One-step example:** starting from $w=0$, $v=0$, with $\eta=0.1$ and $\nabla L = -2$: first, $v \leftarrow 0.9(0) - 0.1(-2) = 0.2$, then $w \leftarrow 0 + 0.2 = 0.2$. Next step with $\nabla L = -2$ again: $v \leftarrow 0.9(0.2) + 0.2 = 0.38$ — momentum has nearly doubled the effective step size by building on the previous gradient. Think of a ball rolling downhill: it gathers speed on long slopes and isn't deflected by small bumps.
- **Adam** combines momentum with a **per-parameter adaptive** learning rate — it divides each parameter's step by a running estimate of that parameter's gradient magnitude, so flat directions get larger effective steps and steep directions get smaller ones. This is why Adam handles ravines without manual tuning.

<Callout type="info" title="Which optimizer?">
  Start with **Adam** — it works well out of the box and is the default for most deep learning. Well-tuned SGD with momentum can generalize slightly better on some vision tasks, but Adam needs far less tuning.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

1. **Learning rate too high** — the loss diverges to NaN. If that happens, lower $\eta$ by 10×.
2. **Forgetting to shuffle** mini-batches — ordered data biases the gradient and slows convergence.
3. **Not normalizing inputs** — features on wildly different scales distort the loss surface into ravines.

## Key takeaways

- Gradient descent updates parameters **opposite** the gradient: $w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\nabla L$, applied to every parameter ($w$ and $b$) at once.
- For MSE on a linear model, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{2}{n}\sum (w x_i + b - y_i)\,x_i$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac{2}{n}\sum (w x_i + b - y_i)$ — averaged residuals, weighted by the input for $w$.
- Gradients shrink as you near the minimum, so steps **automatically slow down** even with a fixed $\eta$.
- The **learning rate** is the most important hyperparameter — too big diverges to NaN, too small crawls.
- **Mini-batch** SGD is the practical default; **momentum** and **Adam** speed convergence.
- Normalize inputs and tune $\eta$ first when training stalls.
