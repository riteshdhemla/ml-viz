# Bayesian Optimization

Some functions are brutally expensive to evaluate: training a model to read off its validation accuracy, running a physical experiment, a costly simulation. You can't afford thousands of samples, and you have no gradients. **Bayesian optimization (BO)** finds the optimum of such a black box in as *few* evaluations as possible by being strategic about where to look next — using a probabilistic model of the function to decide. It's the standard tool for hyperparameter tuning and experiment design — an **optimization** method one level up from gradient descent: it searches the *hyperparameter* space (learning rate, depth, regularization) instead of the weight space, when each evaluation is a full training run.

**Real-world examples:** hyperparameter tuning (learning rate, architecture choices), AutoML, optimizing chemical/materials experiments, tuning robotics controllers, and A/B-test design.

## The setup: expensive, black-box, derivative-free

BO targets functions $f(\mathbf{x})$ that are:

- **Expensive** — each evaluation costs minutes/hours/dollars, so you want few.
- **Black-box** — no formula, just inputs→outputs.
- **Derivative-free** — no gradients, so gradient descent is out.
- Often **noisy**.

The strategy: build a cheap *probabilistic model* of $f$ from the few points seen so far, use it to decide the single most promising next point, evaluate there, update, repeat.

## Two ingredients

**1. A surrogate model.** A **Gaussian process** (previous lesson) is the standard surrogate: it predicts $f$ everywhere *with calibrated uncertainty* — exactly what we need to reason about where the optimum might hide. The GP gives a posterior mean $\mu(\mathbf{x})$ and standard deviation $\sigma(\mathbf{x})$ at every candidate point.

**2. An acquisition function.** A cheap function $\alpha(\mathbf{x})$, computed from the surrogate's $\mu$ and $\sigma$, that scores how *worth evaluating* each point is. We optimize the acquisition function (cheap — it's just the GP) to pick the next $\mathbf{x}$, then evaluate the expensive $f$ only there.

## The explore–exploit trade-off

The acquisition function must balance two pulls:

- **Exploitation** — sample where the surrogate mean $\mu(\mathbf{x})$ is already good.
- **Exploration** — sample where uncertainty $\sigma(\mathbf{x})$ is high, because the optimum might be hiding in an unexplored region.

Pure exploitation gets stuck in a local optimum; pure exploration wastes budget. Common acquisition functions encode the balance differently:

$$
\text{UCB:}\quad \alpha(\mathbf{x}) = \mu(\mathbf{x}) + \kappa\,\sigma(\mathbf{x})
$$

Upper Confidence Bound is the simplest: prefer high mean *and* high uncertainty, with $\kappa$ tuning the trade-off (large $\kappa$ → more exploration).

$$
\text{EI:}\quad \alpha(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}\big[\max(f(\mathbf{x}) - f^{+},\, 0)\big]
$$

Expected Improvement scores the *expected amount* by which $\mathbf{x}$ would beat the current best $f^{+}$ — it automatically values both promising means and uncertain regions, and is the most popular default. (Written here for **maximization**; when minimizing a loss, swap the improvement to $\max(f^{+} - f(\mathbf{x}),\, 0)$.)

<Callout type="info" title="Other acquisition functions">
  **Probability of Improvement (PI)** scores the chance of beating $f^+$ (but ignores by how much). **Thompson sampling** draws one function from the GP posterior and optimizes that sample. **Entropy search** picks the point that most reduces uncertainty about the *location* of the optimum. EI and UCB dominate in practice for their simplicity and strong performance.
</Callout>

## The Bayesian optimization loop

```text
1. Fit the GP surrogate to all (x, f(x)) seen so far.
2. Maximize the acquisition function α(x) over the surrogate → x_next.
3. Evaluate the expensive f(x_next).  ← the only costly step
4. Add (x_next, f(x_next)) to the data. Repeat until budget exhausted.
```

Each iteration spends one expensive evaluation but lots of cheap surrogate computation — exactly the right trade when evaluations are the bottleneck.

<Callout type="tip" title="Why BO beats grid and random search for HPO">
  Grid and random search are *non-adaptive*: they ignore everything learned from past trials. BO is **adaptive** — every result reshapes where it looks next, so it finds good hyperparameters in far fewer trials. The catch: BO is sequential (each step needs the last result), so when you can run hundreds of trials fully in parallel, random search or Hyperband can be more practical.
</Callout>

## Limitations

- **Dimensionality.** Standard BO works best in low dimensions (≲ 20); GP surrogates and acquisition optimization degrade in very high-dimensional spaces.
- **Sequential.** The basic loop evaluates one point at a time (batch/parallel BO variants exist but are harder).
- **Surrogate cost.** Inherits the GP's $O(N^3)$ — fine, since BO deliberately uses *few* evaluations.

## Common mistakes

1. **Pure exploitation.** Always sampling the current best-looking point gets trapped in a local optimum; the $\sigma$ term (exploration) is essential.
2. **Using BO when evaluations are cheap.** If $f$ is fast, random/grid search with thousands of cheap trials can beat BO's sequential overhead.
3. **Applying it in very high dimensions.** Vanilla BO struggles past ~20 dimensions without specialized variants.
4. **Ignoring noise.** Set the GP's noise term for noisy objectives, or the surrogate will chase observation noise.

## Key takeaways

- **Bayesian optimization** minimizes/maximizes **expensive, black-box, gradient-free** functions in few evaluations by modeling them probabilistically.
- It uses a **GP surrogate** (mean + uncertainty) plus an **acquisition function** that's cheap to optimize and picks the next point.
- Acquisition functions (**UCB** $=\mu+\kappa\sigma$, **EI**) balance **exploitation** (high mean) against **exploration** (high uncertainty).
- The **loop** — fit surrogate → maximize acquisition → evaluate once → update — makes BO **adaptive**, beating grid/random search for hyperparameter tuning when trials are costly.
