# Bayesian Linear Regression

Ordinary least squares returns a single best-fit line and a single prediction. But "the line is $y = 2.1x + 0.4$" hides a crucial question: *how sure are we?* **Bayesian linear regression** answers it by treating the weights as a probability distribution rather than a point. Instead of one line, you get a *distribution over lines* — and every prediction comes with a calibrated error bar that widens where you have no data.

**Real-world examples:** any setting where overconfidence is dangerous — scientific modeling with few samples, A/B-test effect sizes, active learning (where to sample next), and as the foundation for Gaussian processes and Bayesian optimization (next lessons).

## From a point estimate to a distribution

OLS finds the weights $\mathbf{w}$ minimizing squared error — one vector. The Bayesian view instead asks for the full **posterior distribution** over $\mathbf{w}$ given the data, via Bayes' rule:

$$
\underbrace{p(\mathbf{w}\mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{y})}_{\text{posterior}} \;\propto\; \underbrace{p(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{w})}_{\text{likelihood}}\;\underbrace{p(\mathbf{w})}_{\text{prior}}
$$

- **Prior** $p(\mathbf{w}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I})$ — before seeing data, we believe weights are small (a Gaussian centered at 0).
- **Likelihood** — assume Gaussian noise: $y = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + \epsilon$, $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \beta^{-1})$.
- **Posterior** — the updated belief after the data.

## The posterior (closed form)

Because the prior is Gaussian and the likelihood is Gaussian, the posterior is **also Gaussian** (the Gaussian is *conjugate* to itself here) — no sampling needed:

$$
p(\mathbf{w}\mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{y}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{m}_N, \mathbf{S}_N)
$$

$$
\mathbf{S}_N^{-1} = \alpha\mathbf{I} + \beta\,\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}, \qquad \mathbf{m}_N = \beta\,\mathbf{S}_N\,\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
$$

<Callout type="info" title="Ridge regression is the MAP estimate">
  The posterior **mean** $\mathbf{m}_N$ is exactly the **ridge regression** solution with $\lambda = \alpha/\beta$. So Bayesian linear regression *contains* ridge: the Gaussian prior's precision $\alpha$ is the L2 penalty. The difference is that the Bayesian version also gives you $\mathbf{S}_N$ — the full covariance, i.e. the uncertainty — not just the point.
</Callout>

## The predictive distribution

For a new input $\mathbf{x}_*$, we don't commit to one weight vector — we **average over all of them**, weighted by the posterior. This *marginalization* yields a Gaussian predictive distribution:

$$
p(y_* \mid \mathbf{x}_*, \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{y}) = \mathcal{N}\big(y_* \mid \mathbf{m}_N^{\top}\mathbf{x}_*,\; \sigma_N^2(\mathbf{x}_*)\big)
$$

$$
\sigma_N^2(\mathbf{x}_*) = \underbrace{\beta^{-1}}_{\text{noise}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{x}_*^{\top}\mathbf{S}_N\,\mathbf{x}_*}_{\text{model uncertainty}}
$$

The predictive variance has two parts: irreducible **observation noise** ($\beta^{-1}$), and **model uncertainty** ($\mathbf{x}_*^{\top}\mathbf{S}_N\mathbf{x}_*$) that depends on *where* you predict.

<Callout type="tip" title="Uncertainty grows away from the data">
  The model-uncertainty term is small near observed inputs and **large in regions with no data** — extrapolation. This is the headline benefit: a Bayesian model is *honest* about not knowing, instead of confidently extrapolating a single line to infinity. Active learning exploits exactly this: sample where predictive variance is highest.
</Callout>

## Why marginalize instead of picking the best weights?

Using only the single best $\mathbf{w}$ (MAP) throws away the spread of plausible models. Two weight vectors that fit the data equally well might disagree wildly on a far-away $\mathbf{x}_*$; averaging over the posterior captures that disagreement as predictive variance. Marginalization is what turns "here's a guess" into "here's a guess **and** how much it could be wrong."

## Common mistakes

1. **Reporting only the posterior mean.** That's just (regularized) point regression — the whole point is the variance $\mathbf{S}_N$ and the predictive interval.
2. **Forgetting the two variance sources.** Predictive variance = noise + model uncertainty; near data, only the noise floor remains.
3. **Thinking it's unrelated to ridge.** The posterior mean *is* ridge; the prior precision is the L2 penalty.
4. **Assuming you always need sampling (MCMC).** With Gaussian prior + Gaussian likelihood the posterior is closed-form — sampling is only needed for non-conjugate models.

## Key takeaways

- Bayesian linear regression puts a **prior over weights** and computes the **posterior** $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_N, \mathbf{S}_N)$ — a distribution over lines, not a single fit.
- With Gaussian prior + Gaussian noise the posterior is **closed-form**; its **mean equals ridge regression** (prior precision = L2 penalty).
- The **predictive distribution** marginalizes over the posterior, giving a mean *and* a variance = **noise + model uncertainty**.
- Predictive **uncertainty grows away from the data**, making the model honest about extrapolation — the basis for active learning, GPs, and Bayesian optimization.
