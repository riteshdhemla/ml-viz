# Graph Convolutions & GraphSAGE

The previous lesson framed every GNN as **message passing**. This lesson makes it concrete: the **Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)** packs message passing into a clean matrix operation, and **GraphSAGE** fixes the two things that stop a plain GCN from working at scale or on new data. Together they're the workhorses of practical graph learning.

**Real-world examples:** node classification on citation networks, product recommendation on huge user–item graphs (GraphSAGE was created at Pinterest for exactly this), and molecular property prediction.

## From message passing to a matrix multiply

Recall the simple mean-aggregation update. If we aggregate over neighbors **including a self-loop**, one layer for all nodes at once is just:

$$
\mathbf{H}^{(k)} = \sigma\big(\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{H}^{(k-1)}\,\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\big)
$$

- $\mathbf{A}$ — adjacency **with self-loops** ($\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{I}$), so a node keeps its own features.
- $\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{A}$ — row-normalize so each node averages (rather than sums) its neighborhood, keeping magnitudes stable.
- $\mathbf{W}^{(k)}$ — a **shared, learnable** weight matrix (same for every node — this is the "convolution": one filter slides over all neighborhoods).
- $\sigma$ — a nonlinearity (ReLU).

## The GCN normalization

The original GCN (Kipf & Welling) uses **symmetric** normalization instead of plain row-averaging:

$$
\mathbf{H}^{(k)} = \sigma\big(\hat{\mathbf{A}}\,\mathbf{H}^{(k-1)}\,\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\big),
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{A}} = \tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2}\,\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2}
$$

where $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{I}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{D}}$ is its degree matrix.

<Callout type="info" title="Why the √ on both sides?">
  Symmetric normalization $\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2}$ down-weights messages from **high-degree** neighbors (a hub shouldn't dominate everyone it touches), scaling each edge by $1/\sqrt{d_u d_v}$. It keeps the operator's eigenvalues bounded, which stabilizes training — better than the asymmetric row-average $\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{A}$ for most node-classification tasks.
</Callout>

## Spectral vs. spatial — same destination

GCNs were originally **derived** from spectral graph theory: a convolution defined via the graph Laplacian's eigenbasis, then approximated (first-order Chebyshev) down to the simple $\hat{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}$ form above. The modern, practical view is **spatial**: it's just normalized neighborhood aggregation. You don't need the spectral derivation to use it — but it's why GCNs are called "convolutions."

## GraphSAGE: sample and aggregate

A plain GCN has two practical problems: (1) the full $\hat{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{H}$ multiply needs the **entire graph** in memory, and (2) it's **transductive** — it can only score nodes present at training time. **GraphSAGE** fixes both:

- **Sample** a fixed number of neighbors per node (e.g. 25 at hop 1, 10 at hop 2) instead of using all of them — bounding compute and memory regardless of degree.
- **Aggregate** the sampled neighbors with a chosen function, then concatenate with the node's own state and transform:

$$
\mathbf{h}_v^{(k)} = \sigma\Big(\mathbf{W}^{(k)} \cdot \big[\,\mathbf{h}_v^{(k-1)} \,\Vert\, \text{AGG}\big(\{\mathbf{h}_u^{(k-1)}: u \in \mathcal{S}(v)\}\big)\,\big]\Big)
$$

Aggregators can be **mean**, **pool** (element-wise max of transformed neighbors), or even an LSTM. Because the learned aggregator and weights depend only on *features*, not node identity, GraphSAGE is **inductive**: apply it to brand-new nodes or entirely new graphs without retraining.

<Callout type="tip" title="Transductive vs. inductive">
  **Transductive** (vanilla GCN): learns embeddings tied to the specific nodes seen in training; a new node means re-running on the whole graph. **Inductive** (GraphSAGE): learns *how to aggregate*, so it generalizes to unseen nodes and graphs — essential for production systems where the graph grows constantly.
</Callout>

## Choosing an aggregator

| Aggregator | Behavior | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean** | average neighbor features | simple, smooths; the GCN default |
| **Pool (max)** | element-wise max of transformed neighbors | captures "is any neighbor X?" |
| **Sum** | total neighbor features | most expressive (distinguishes degree); basis of GIN |
| **LSTM** | order-sensitive over a random permutation | high capacity, not permutation-invariant by design |

Sum is the most discriminative (the Graph Isomorphism Network result), mean and max lose some structural information but are often more stable.

## Common mistakes

1. **Forgetting self-loops.** Without $\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{I}$, a node's update ignores its own features.
2. **Skipping normalization.** Raw $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}$ lets high-degree hubs blow up activation magnitudes; normalize by degree.
3. **Expecting a transductive GCN to score new nodes.** Use GraphSAGE (or another inductive method) when the graph changes.
4. **Using all neighbors on a huge graph.** Sample (GraphSAGE) to bound the per-node cost rather than aggregating millions of neighbors of a hub.

## Key takeaways

- A **GCN layer** is message passing as one matrix op: $\mathbf{H}^{(k)} = \sigma(\hat{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{H}^{(k-1)}\mathbf{W}^{(k)})$ with a shared learnable $\mathbf{W}$ — the "filter."
- **Symmetric normalization** $\hat{\mathbf{A}}=\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2}$ down-weights high-degree neighbors and stabilizes training.
- **GraphSAGE** samples a fixed set of neighbors and learns an aggregator, making it **scalable** and **inductive** (works on unseen nodes/graphs).
- The **aggregator choice** (mean / max-pool / sum / LSTM) trades expressiveness against stability; sum is the most discriminative.
