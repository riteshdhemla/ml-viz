# Gradient Boosting in Practice: XGBoost

[Boosting](/courses/ensemble-methods/02-boosting) gave us the recipe: fit each new tree to the gradient of the loss, nudging the ensemble downhill in function space. **XGBoost** (and its cousins LightGBM and CatBoost) turned that recipe into the engine that has dominated tabular-data machine learning for a decade. The difference is regularization — applied everywhere.

## The regularized objective

XGBoost doesn't just fit the gradient; it minimizes a penalized objective at every tree:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{i} \ell(y_i, \hat{y}_i) \;+\; \sum_{\text{trees}} \left[ \gamma T + \frac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2 \right]
$$

where $T$ is the number of leaves in a tree and $w_j$ are its leaf output values. Two penalties, two effects:

- $\gamma T$ — each leaf must justify its existence: a split happens only if it improves the loss by more than $\gamma$. Built-in pruning.
- $\lambda \sum w_j^2$ — L2 shrinkage on leaf values, the same medicine as Ridge regression, applied inside every tree.

XGBoost also uses the **second-order** Taylor expansion of the loss (gradients *and* Hessians), which gives a closed-form optimal leaf weight and split score instead of a heuristic. That's the engine — worth opening up.

## The engine: gradients and Hessians

Plain gradient boosting fits each tree to the residual — the negative gradient of the loss. XGBoost keeps the gradient but adds the **second derivative**. For every training example $i$ it computes two numbers against the current prediction $\hat{y}_i$:

$$
g_i = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \hat{y}_i}, \qquad h_i = \frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \hat{y}_i^2}
$$

- The **gradient** $g_i$ says *which way* the prediction for this point should move, and how strongly it's currently wrong.
- The **Hessian** $h_i$ says *how confident* the loss is about that direction — the local curvature. For log-loss $h_i = p_i(1 - p_i)$, largest when the model is unsure ($p \approx 0.5$) and near-zero once it's certain. It acts like a per-example weight: points the model is already confident about pull less on the next tree.

Take the second-order Taylor expansion of the objective, freeze a tree's structure, and the best value for each leaf $j$ falls out in **closed form** — no line search, just the sums of gradients and Hessians of the points that land in it ($G_j = \sum_{i \in j} g_i$, $H_j = \sum_{i \in j} h_i$):

$$
w_j^{*} = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda}
$$

This is a Newton step, shrunk by $\lambda$: a leaf holding a strong, confident signal (large $|G|$, moderate $H$) gets a bold value; $\lambda$ reins every leaf back toward zero.

## Similarity score and gain

Substitute $w_j^{*}$ back into the objective and the quality of a whole tree structure collapses to one **structure score** (lower is better):

$$
\text{obj}^{*} = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{T} \frac{G_j^2}{H_j + \lambda} \;+\; \gamma T
$$

The per-leaf term $\tfrac{G_j^2}{H_j + \lambda}$ is XGBoost's **similarity score** — high when a leaf's points agree on which direction to move. To decide whether to split a leaf, XGBoost compares the similarity of the two children against the parent:

$$
\text{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}\right] - \gamma
$$

A **positive gain** means the split separates points that wanted to move in different directions — real structure. A **negative or tiny gain** means the split mostly reshuffles noise, and $\gamma$ is the toll every new leaf must clear before it's allowed to exist. Rather than growing level by level, XGBoost repeatedly splits whichever leaf offers the **highest gain**, spending depth where the signal is richest and leaving quiet branches shallow.

<Callout type="info" title="Interview-ready framing">
  If asked "how does XGBoost actually build a tree?", answer in one breath: *for each point it computes a gradient and a Hessian; it sums those inside every candidate leaf to score splits by gain and to set leaf values in closed form; and $\lambda$, $\gamma$, and the learning rate regularize every step.* That single sentence covers the second-order objective, the similarity/gain machinery, and the regularization — the three things interviewers are probing for.
</Callout>

## Shrinkage: the learning rate

Each tree's contribution is scaled by a learning rate $\eta$:

$$
\hat{y}^{(t)} = \hat{y}^{(t-1)} + \eta \, f_t(x)
$$

Small $\eta$ (0.01–0.1) means each tree corrects only a fraction of the remaining error, leaving room for later trees to refine. This is the single most reliable quality knob: **lower $\eta$ + more trees almost always generalizes better**, at the cost of training time. The standard move is to fix $\eta$ small and use *early stopping* — keep adding trees until validation error stops improving.

## Row and column subsampling

Borrowing from [Random Forests](/courses/ensemble-methods/01-bagging-and-random-forests), each tree (or each split) sees only a random fraction of rows (`subsample`) and columns (`colsample_bytree`). Decorrelating the trees reduces variance — and as a bonus makes training faster.

<Callout type="tip" title="A sane starting configuration">
  `eta=0.05`, `max_depth=6`, `subsample=0.8`, `colsample_bytree=0.8`, `n_estimators` large with `early_stopping_rounds=50`. Then tune `max_depth` (capacity), `min_child_weight` (leaf conservatism), and the regularizers `lambda`/`gamma` — in that order. Resist tuning ten knobs at once.
</Callout>

That configuration, in code (for your own train/test split — XGBoost and LightGBM share the same shape):

```python
import xgboost as xgb
import lightgbm as lgb

xgb_model = xgb.XGBClassifier(
    n_estimators=500,
    max_depth=6,
    learning_rate=0.05,
    subsample=0.8,
    colsample_bytree=0.8,
    eval_metric='logloss',
    early_stopping_rounds=50,
)
xgb_model.fit(X_train, y_train, eval_set=[(X_test, y_test)], verbose=False)

lgb_model = lgb.LGBMClassifier(
    n_estimators=500,
    max_depth=6,
    learning_rate=0.05,
    subsample=0.8,
    colsample_bytree=0.8,
    early_stopping_round=50,
)
lgb_model.fit(X_train, y_train, eval_set=[(X_test, y_test)])
```

## What makes it *Extreme*: the systems tricks

The "X" in XGBoost is as much systems engineering as statistics. The math above describes *what* to compute; these tricks make it fast enough to compute on real data:

- **Approximate split finding via a weighted quantile sketch.** Scanning every distinct feature value for the best split is exact but slow. XGBoost instead buckets each feature into a handful of candidate split points — a histogram — and only scores those. The buckets are weighted by the Hessian $h_i$, so regions where the loss is most curved (the points that matter most) get finer resolution. This is what lets it distribute across machines and handle datasets that don't fit in memory.
- **Sparsity-aware split finding.** Missing values and zeros aren't imputed. For each split, XGBoost learns a **default direction** — it tries sending all the absent entries left, then right, and keeps whichever gives higher gain. Sparse data is a first-class citizen, and inference on it is fast because the algorithm only visits present entries.
- **Cache-aware, blocked, parallel.** Feature values are pre-sorted once into compressed column blocks that can be reused across every tree and split in parallel, with memory-access patterns tuned to the CPU cache. Boosting is inherently sequential across trees, but the expensive step — finding the best split *within* a tree — is parallelized.

The same ideas power the cousins: **LightGBM** pushes the histogram approach further with leaf-wise growth and is often faster on large data; **CatBoost** adds ordered boosting and native categorical handling to fight the target leakage that naive category encoding invites. Different trade-offs, same regularized-second-order core.

## Why trees still beat neural nets on tables

On heterogeneous tabular data — mixed scales, missing values, categorical features, low sample counts — gradient-boosted trees remain the strongest default:

- Splits are invariant to feature scaling and monotone transforms; no normalization needed
- XGBoost handles missing values natively, learning a default direction per split
- Trees capture sharp interactions and thresholds that smooth function approximators need many samples to find

Deep learning wins when there's structure to exploit — images, text, audio, huge datasets. For a 50,000-row CSV, boosted trees are the bar to beat.

## Key takeaways

- XGBoost = gradient boosting + regularization at every level: penalized leaves ($\gamma$, $\lambda$), shrinkage ($\eta$), and row/column subsampling
- Each point contributes a **gradient** (which way to move) and a **Hessian** (how confident); their sums per leaf give a closed-form leaf value $w^{*} = -G/(H+\lambda)$ and a split score
- Splits are chosen by **gain** — the similarity gained by separating the children, minus the $\gamma$ toll for a new leaf; XGBoost grows the highest-gain leaf first
- The "Extreme" part is systems: weighted-quantile-sketch histograms, sparsity-aware default directions for missing values, and cache-aware parallel split finding
- Lower learning rate + more trees + early stopping is the most dependable tuning move
- For tabular data, gradient-boosted trees are still the default champion
