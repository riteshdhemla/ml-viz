# Chain Rule and Backpropagation

Backpropagation is not magic — it's the chain rule applied to computational graphs. It's what makes the loop's **optimization** stage tractable for deep nets: without an efficient way to get the gradient of the loss w.r.t. millions of parameters, gradient descent would be a non-starter. Once you understand the chain rule, every step of backprop becomes a mechanical application of one idea.

**Real-world examples:** Backpropagation (the algorithm that trains every deep neural network) is the chain rule applied recursively through a computational graph.

## The chain rule

When a function is composed of other functions, the chain rule tells you how to differentiate the composition:

$$
\frac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) = \frac{df}{dg} \cdot \frac{dg}{dx}
$$

**Example:** $h(x) = \sigma(3x^2)$ where $\sigma$ is sigmoid.

Let $g = 3x^2$ and $f(g) = \sigma(g)$:

$$
h'(x) = \sigma'(g) \cdot 6x = \sigma(3x^2)(1 - \sigma(3x^2)) \cdot 6x
$$

The trick that powers backprop is to read this **right to left**. We never write the whole derivative as one giant formula. Instead each elementary operation contributes one local factor, and we multiply those factors together:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{dh}{dx}}_{\text{what we want}} = \underbrace{\frac{dh}{dg}}_{\sigma'(g)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{dg}{dx}}_{6x}
$$

If a third operation were stacked on top, say $p = h^2$, we would simply prepend one more factor: $\frac{dp}{dx} = \frac{dp}{dh}\cdot\frac{dh}{dg}\cdot\frac{dg}{dx}$. Backprop is nothing more than this — a chain of single-operation derivatives multiplied together.

## Multivariable chain rule

For $z = f(x, y)$ where $x = x(t)$ and $y = y(t)$:

$$
\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}
$$

When $z$ depends on $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbf{x}$ depends on $\mathbf{w}$ via $\mathbf{x} = g(\mathbf{w})$, the chain rule becomes a matrix product of **Jacobians**:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \cdot \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}}{\partial \mathbf{w}}
$$

## Computational graphs

A **computational graph** represents a function as a directed acyclic graph (DAG): nodes are operations, edges carry values forward (forward pass) and gradients backward (backward pass).

<ComputationalGraphViz />

**Example graph:** $L = (\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} - y)^2$

```
x, w → [dot product] → z
z, y → [subtract]   → r = z - y
r    → [square]      → L = r²
```

### Forward pass

Compute each node's output in topological order:

$$
z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}, \quad r = z - y, \quad L = r^2
$$

### Backward pass

Starting from $\frac{\partial L}{\partial L} = 1$, apply the chain rule in reverse:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial r} = 2r = 2(z - y)
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial r} \cdot \frac{\partial r}{\partial z} = 2(z-y) \cdot 1 = 2(z-y)
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = 2(z-y) \cdot \mathbf{x}
$$

<Callout type="tip" title="Local gradients × upstream gradient">
  Each node stores its **local gradient** (derivative of output w.r.t. its inputs). During backprop, it multiplies the incoming upstream gradient by the local gradient and passes it back. The chain rule is always: local × upstream.
</Callout>

### A fully worked numeric pass

Let's run the squared-error graph with real numbers so every multiplication is concrete. Take a single weight $w$ and feature $x$, so $z = wx$ (a one-element dot product), with

$$
w = 2, \quad x = 3, \quad y = 5.
$$

**Forward pass** — compute and *cache* each intermediate value:

$$
z = wx = 2 \cdot 3 = 6, \qquad r = z - y = 6 - 5 = 1, \qquad L = r^2 = 1.
$$

**Backward pass** — start at the output with $\frac{\partial L}{\partial L} = 1$ and walk back, multiplying upstream × local at each node:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial r} = \underbrace{1}_{\text{upstream}} \cdot \underbrace{2r}_{\text{local}} = 2 \cdot 1 = 2
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \underbrace{2}_{\partial L/\partial r} \cdot \underbrace{1}_{\partial r/\partial z} = 2
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \underbrace{2}_{\partial L/\partial z} \cdot \underbrace{x}_{\partial z/\partial w} = 2 \cdot 3 = 6
$$

So $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = 6$. We can sanity-check against the closed form $L = (wx-y)^2 \Rightarrow \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = 2(wx-y)x = 2(1)(3) = 6$. ✅ The notebook reproduces this and confirms it against a finite-difference estimate.

## Worked example: one hidden layer

Now scale the *exact same procedure* up to a tiny MLP. Network:

$$
\hat{y} = \sigma\big(\mathbf{w}_2^\top \,\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1) + b_2\big), \qquad L = -y \ln\hat{y} - (1-y)\ln(1-\hat{y}).
$$

Name every intermediate node so we can attach a gradient to each one:

$$
\mathbf{z}_1 = \mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1, \quad \mathbf{a}_1 = \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{z}_1), \quad z_2 = \mathbf{w}_2^\top\mathbf{a}_1 + b_2, \quad \hat{y} = \sigma(z_2).
$$

The backward pass is one chain-rule multiplication per node, output → input. The first step uses a classic simplification: for binary cross-entropy composed with sigmoid, the two local gradients collapse to a single clean expression,

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2}
= \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}}}_{\frac{\hat{y}-y}{\hat{y}(1-\hat{y})}}
\cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z_2}}_{\hat{y}(1-\hat{y})}
= \hat{y} - y.
$$

From there each subsequent line is upstream × local:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{w}_2} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2}\,\mathbf{a}_1, \qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_2} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2}, \qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{a}_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2}\,\mathbf{w}_2
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{a}_1}\odot \text{ReLU}'(\mathbf{z}_1), \qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}_1}\,\mathbf{x}^\top, \qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}_1}
$$

where $\odot$ is element-wise product and $\text{ReLU}'(\mathbf{z}_1)$ is $1$ where $\mathbf{z}_1 > 0$ and $0$ elsewhere. Notice how the cached forward values ($\mathbf{a}_1$, $\mathbf{w}_2$, $\mathbf{z}_1$, $\mathbf{x}$) are exactly what each local gradient needs — that reuse is why one backward pass suffices.

```python
import numpy as np

def sigmoid(z): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
def relu(z):    return np.maximum(0, z)

# Forward pass
np.random.seed(0)                 # reproducible
x  = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
W1 = np.random.randn(4, 3) * 0.1
b1 = np.zeros(4)
w2 = np.random.randn(4) * 0.1
b2 = 0.0
y  = 1.0

z1    = W1 @ x + b1      # pre-activation layer 1
a1    = relu(z1)          # activation layer 1
z2    = w2 @ a1 + b2     # pre-activation output
y_hat = sigmoid(z2)       # predicted probability
L     = -(y * np.log(y_hat) + (1-y) * np.log(1-y_hat))

# Backward pass — one line per node, upstream × local
dL_dz2 = y_hat - y                          # BCE + sigmoid collapse
dL_dw2 = dL_dz2 * a1                         # × a1
dL_db2 = dL_dz2                              # × 1
dL_da1 = dL_dz2 * w2                         # × w2
dL_dz1 = dL_da1 * (z1 > 0)                  # × ReLU'(z1)
dL_dW1 = np.outer(dL_dz1, x)                # × xᵀ
dL_db1 = dL_dz1                             # × 1
```

What to notice:

- **The cache is the whole trick.** Every local gradient is a function of a value
  the forward pass already computed — $2r$ needs $r$, $\partial L/\partial
  \mathbf{w}_2$ needs $\mathbf{a}_1$, $\partial L/\partial \mathbf{W}_1$ needs
  $\mathbf{x}$. Nothing is recomputed.
- **The BCE + sigmoid collapse is exact.** Separately the two factors are
  $-1.436$ and $0.211$; their product is $\hat{y} - y$ to the last digit. That is
  why frameworks fuse the two ops — the fused form also avoids dividing by
  $\hat{y}(1-\hat{y}) \to 0$.
- **Non-zero $\partial L/\partial \mathbf{a}_1$, zero $\partial L/\partial
  \mathbf{z}_1$.** The trace's second hidden unit sits at $z_1 = -0.1$. The loss
  is genuinely sensitive to its *activation* ($0.121$), but the ReLU gate zeroes
  the gradient to its *pre-activation*, and those are different quantities. This
  is the mechanism behind dying ReLU: the gradient that would revive the unit is
  precisely the one being switched off.
- **One dead unit zeroes 5 of the 11 parameter gradients**: the whole second row
  of $\mathbf{W}_1$, its bias, and the output weight reading from it.
- **The efficiency claim, counted.** Verifying those 11 gradients by finite
  differences takes 22 forward passes; backprop produces all of them in one
  backward pass. At $10^9$ parameters that ratio is $2\times10^9 : 1$.

## Why backprop is efficient

Naively computing $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i}$ by finite differences requires two forward passes **per parameter**. A network with millions of parameters would be intractable.

Backpropagation computes **all gradients in one backward pass** by reusing intermediate results. The key insight: gradients at earlier layers are computed by multiplying already-computed downstream gradients with local Jacobians. Total cost: $\approx 2$–$3\times$ the forward pass cost (one pass to compute activations, one to propagate gradients, plus the weight gradient accumulation).

<Callout type="info" title="Automatic differentiation">
  Modern frameworks (PyTorch, JAX) implement **automatic differentiation** — they build the computational graph on-the-fly, store forward activations, and replay the backward pass automatically. You never write the backward pass by hand.
</Callout>

## Vanishing and exploding gradients

When many small Jacobians are multiplied together (deep networks, long sequences), the gradient can **vanish**:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{w}_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{a}_n} \cdot \prod_{k=1}^{n-1} J_k
$$

If each $\|J_k\| < 1$, the gradient decays exponentially. Sigmoid's max derivative is $0.25$ — stacking 20 sigmoid layers multiplies gradients by at most $0.25^{20} \approx 10^{-12}$.

Solutions:
- **ReLU:** gradient is exactly 1 for positive inputs, no saturation
- **Batch normalization:** stabilizes activations, preventing saturation
- **Residual connections:** add the input directly, creating gradient highways

## Common mistakes

1. **Forgetting to cache forward-pass activations**: backprop needs them to compute local gradients; without caching, you'd have to recompute the entire forward pass for every parameter.
2. **Confusing vanishing gradients with the chain rule being wrong**: vanishing gradients are a consequence of multiplying many small terms (< 1) together, not an error in the chain rule itself.
3. **Applying the chain rule to matrix operations without respecting the transpose**: $\frac{\partial}{\partial W}(Wx)$ involves $x^\top$, not $x$; transposing preserves dimension consistency in the gradient.

## Key takeaways

- Backpropagation = chain rule applied to a computational graph.
- Each node multiplies the upstream gradient by its local gradient and passes it back.
- Backprop computes all gradients simultaneously in one pass. This is why neural network training is tractable.
- **Vanishing gradients** happen when many $< 1$ factors multiply; solved by ReLU, batch norm, or residual connections.
