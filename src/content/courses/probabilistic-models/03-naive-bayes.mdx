# Naive Bayes

[Gaussian mixture models](/courses/probabilistic-models/01-gaussian-mixture-models) modeled how data is *generated* without labels. Naive Bayes applies the same generative thinking to classification: model how each class generates its features, then invert with Bayes' rule. It powered the first generation of spam filters and remains a startlingly strong baseline for text.

## From Bayes' rule to a classifier

For a class $y$ and features $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_d)$:

$$
P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \propto P(y) \, P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)
$$

The joint likelihood $P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$ is the hard part — with $d$ binary features it's a table of $2^d$ entries per class. The **naive assumption** collapses it: treat features as conditionally independent given the class,

$$
P(\mathbf{x} \mid y) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} P(x_j \mid y)
$$

Now we need only $d$ small one-dimensional distributions per class, each estimable by simple counting. Prediction is:

$$
\hat{y} = \arg\max_y \; \log P(y) + \sum_{j} \log P(x_j \mid y)
$$

(Logs because multiplying ten thousand small probabilities underflows; sums of logs don't.)

## A spam filter from counts

With word counts as features (multinomial Naive Bayes), training is literally counting:

- $P(\text{spam})$ — fraction of training emails that are spam
- $P(\text{"viagra"} \mid \text{spam})$ — occurrences of "viagra" in spam, over total words in spam

Each word in a new email votes with its log-likelihood ratio, and the votes add. A word common in spam and rare in ham pushes hard toward spam; neutral words push nowhere.

One fix is mandatory: a word never seen in spam would give $P(x_j \mid \text{spam}) = 0$ and veto every other word with $\log 0 = -\infty$. **Laplace smoothing** adds a pseudo-count $\alpha$ (usually 1) to every count:

$$
P(x_j \mid y) = \frac{\text{count}(x_j, y) + \alpha}{\text{count}(y) + \alpha \, d}
$$

<Callout type="info" title="Pick the flavor to match the features">
  **Multinomial** — word counts, the text workhorse. **Bernoulli** — binary present/absent features. **Gaussian** — continuous features, fitting a per-class mean and variance to each (the same per-dimension Gaussian estimation you saw in [the EM algorithm](/courses/probabilistic-models/02-em-algorithm), but with labels making it a one-shot count).
</Callout>

## Why the wrong assumption still works

Words are *not* independent given the class — "machine" and "learning" travel together. So $P(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ is badly calibrated: correlated evidence gets double-counted and the predicted probabilities saturate toward 0 or 1. But classification only needs the **argmax** to be right, and the ranking of classes survives a lot of double-counting. Wrong probabilities, right answers.

The trade-offs follow directly:

- **Strengths** — trains in one pass, handles huge sparse vocabularies, needs little data, naturally multiclass, easy to update online
- **Weaknesses** — overconfident probabilities (don't threshold them naively), blind to feature interactions, beaten by discriminative models (logistic regression, boosted trees) when training data is plentiful

## Key takeaways

- Naive Bayes = Bayes' rule + conditional independence: $P(\mathbf{x}|y) = \prod_j P(x_j|y)$
- Training is counting; prediction is summing log-likelihood votes
- Laplace smoothing prevents a single unseen word from vetoing the entire prediction
- The independence assumption breaks calibration, not (usually) the argmax — great classifier, untrustworthy probabilities
