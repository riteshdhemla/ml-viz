# Vectors and Spaces

Every piece of data in machine learning — an image, a sentence, a medical record — is eventually represented as a **vector**: a list of numbers with direction and magnitude. Understanding vectors deeply unlocks intuition for why algorithms work.

## What is a vector?

A vector $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is an ordered list of $n$ real numbers:

$$
\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

Geometrically, you can think of it as an arrow from the origin to a point in $n$-dimensional space. In ML, $n$ is often the number of features.

**Real-world example:** A house with area 1500 sq ft, 3 bedrooms, and price $300k is the vector $\mathbf{x} = [1500, 3, 300000]^\top$.

## Core operations

### Addition and scaling

$$
\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 + v_1 \\ u_2 + v_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad c\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} cv_1 \\ cv_2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

These two operations are the building blocks of every linear model. A linear model $f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b$ is just a scaled sum of inputs.

### The Euclidean norm

The **length** (norm) of a vector measures how far it points from the origin:

$$
\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2} = \sqrt{\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{v}}
$$

A **unit vector** has norm 1. You can normalize any vector: $\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{v} / \|\mathbf{v}\|$.

```python
import numpy as np

v = np.array([3.0, 4.0])
norm = np.linalg.norm(v)       # 5.0
unit = v / norm                 # [0.6, 0.8]
print(f"‖v‖ = {norm}")
print(f"unit vector: {unit}")
```

## The dot product

The dot product is the most fundamental operation in ML:

$$
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the vectors.

<Callout type="tip" title="Why the dot product matters">
  Every neuron computes a dot product $\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}$. Cosine similarity — used in word embeddings and recommendation systems — is just the dot product of unit vectors: $\cos\theta = \hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{v}}$.
</Callout>

### What the dot product measures

| $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$ | Meaning |
|-------------------------------|---------|
| $> 0$ | Vectors point in roughly the same direction |
| $= 0$ | Vectors are **orthogonal** (perpendicular) |
| $< 0$ | Vectors point in roughly opposite directions |

### Worked example

$\mathbf{u} = [2, 1]$, $\mathbf{v} = [1, 3]$:

$$
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 2 \times 1 + 1 \times 3 = 5
$$

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{5}{\sqrt{5} \cdot \sqrt{10}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{50}} \approx 0.707 \implies \theta \approx 45°
$$

```python
u = np.array([2.0, 1.0])
v = np.array([1.0, 3.0])

dot = np.dot(u, v)              # 5.0
cos_theta = dot / (np.linalg.norm(u) * np.linalg.norm(v))  # ≈ 0.707
angle_deg = np.degrees(np.arccos(cos_theta))                # ≈ 45°
```

### Projection: the dot product's geometric job

The dot product answers *"how much of $\mathbf{b}$ points along $\mathbf{a}$?"* That quantity is the **projection** of $\mathbf{b}$ onto $\mathbf{a}$ (the shadow $\mathbf{b}$ casts on the line through $\mathbf{a}$). Start from $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta$ and divide by $\|\mathbf{a}\|$:

$$
\text{scalar proj} = \|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|}, \qquad
\text{vector proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b} = \underbrace{\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}}}_{\text{how many copies of }\mathbf{a}}\,\mathbf{a}
$$

The denominator $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2$ rescales by $\mathbf{a}$'s length so the result lands exactly on the line through $\mathbf{a}$. This single formula is the engine behind least-squares regression (project the targets onto the column space) and PCA (project data onto principal directions). ## Vector spaces and basis

A **vector space** is a set of vectors that is closed under addition and scalar multiplication. $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the most common one in ML.

A **basis** is a set of linearly independent vectors that span the space. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, the standard basis is:

$$
\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Every vector is a **linear combination** of basis vectors: $\mathbf{v} = v_1\mathbf{e}_1 + v_2\mathbf{e}_2$.

<Callout type="info" title="Orthogonal bases are special">
  When basis vectors are mutually orthogonal (all pairwise dot products = 0), projection onto any basis vector is simply the dot product. PCA finds the orthogonal basis of maximum variance — that's why it's so powerful.
</Callout>

## Linear independence and span

Vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k$ are **linearly independent** if no one of them can be written as a combination of the others. Equivalently, $\sum_i c_i \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$ implies all $c_i = 0$.

The **span** of a set is all vectors you can reach by linear combination — it describes the "reachable" subspace.

### Worked example: dependent vs independent

Take $\mathbf{a} = [1, 2]$ and $\mathbf{b} = [2, 4]$. Notice $\mathbf{b} = 2\mathbf{a}$, so $2\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$ with non-zero coefficients — they are **dependent**. Their span is just the *line* through $\mathbf{a}$; no combination ever leaves it, so they cannot describe a 2D plane.

Now take $\mathbf{a} = [1, 2]$ and $\mathbf{c} = [2, 1]$. The only way $c_1\mathbf{a} + c_2\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ is $c_1 = c_2 = 0$ (the equations $c_1 + 2c_2 = 0$ and $2c_1 + c_2 = 0$ force it), so they are **independent** and their span is *all* of $\mathbb{R}^2$ — they form a basis.

<Callout type="tip" title="The rank test">
  Stack the vectors as columns of a matrix. The number of independent vectors is the matrix **rank**. Full rank (rank = number of vectors) ⇒ independent. In ML, rank-deficient feature matrices cause unstable models — two features that are linear combinations of each other carry no new information.
</Callout>

## Key takeaways

- A vector is a list of numbers representing a point/direction in space.
- The **dot product** $\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}$ is the core of every linear model — it measures similarity and projects one vector onto another.
- **Orthogonal** vectors have zero dot product; orthogonal bases simplify projection.
- **Norm** measures length; normalizing a vector gives direction without magnitude.
