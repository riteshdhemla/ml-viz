# Vectors and Spaces

Every piece of data in machine learning — an image, a sentence, a medical record — is eventually represented as a **vector**: a list of numbers with direction and magnitude. Understanding vectors deeply unlocks intuition for why algorithms work.

## What is a vector?

A vector $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is an ordered list of $n$ real numbers:

$$
\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

Geometrically, you can think of it as an arrow from the origin to a point in $n$-dimensional space. In ML, $n$ is often the number of features.

**Real-world example:** A house with area 1500 sq ft, 3 bedrooms, and price \$300k is the vector $\mathbf{x} = [1500, 3, 300000]^\top$. (Here we pack everything — even the price — into one vector; in supervised learning you'll usually keep the target $y$ separate from the features $\mathbf{x}$.)

## Core operations

### Addition and scaling

$$
\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 + v_1 \\ u_2 + v_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad c\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} cv_1 \\ cv_2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

These two operations are the building blocks of all linear algebra — and of every algorithm in this course.

### The Euclidean norm

The **length** (norm) of a vector measures how far it points from the origin:

$$
\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2} = \sqrt{\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{v}}
$$

A **unit vector** has norm 1. You can normalize any vector: $\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{v} / \|\mathbf{v}\|$.

```python
import numpy as np

v = np.array([3.0, 4.0])
norm = np.linalg.norm(v)       # 5.0
unit = v / norm                 # [0.6, 0.8]
print(f"‖v‖ = {norm}")
print(f"unit vector: {unit}")
```

## The dot product

The dot product is the most fundamental operation in ML:

$$
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the vectors.

<Callout type="tip" title="Sign tells direction, magnitude tells similarity">
  When $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} > 0$ the vectors point roughly the same way; when it equals zero they are perpendicular; when it is negative they diverge. Dividing by both norms gives **cosine similarity** $\cos\theta = \hat{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ — a pure angle measure independent of length.
</Callout>

### What the dot product measures

| $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$ | Meaning |
|-------------------------------|---------|
| $> 0$ | Vectors point in roughly the same direction |
| $= 0$ | Vectors are **orthogonal** (perpendicular) |
| $< 0$ | Vectors point in roughly opposite directions |

### Worked example

$\mathbf{u} = [2, 1]$, $\mathbf{v} = [1, 3]$:

$$
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 2 \times 1 + 1 \times 3 = 5
$$

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{5}{\sqrt{5} \cdot \sqrt{10}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{50}} \approx 0.707 \implies \theta \approx 45°
$$

```python
u = np.array([2.0, 1.0])
v = np.array([1.0, 3.0])

dot = np.dot(u, v)              # 5.0
cos_theta = dot / (np.linalg.norm(u) * np.linalg.norm(v))  # ≈ 0.707
angle_deg = np.degrees(np.arccos(cos_theta))                # ≈ 45°
```

### Projection: the dot product's geometric job

The dot product answers *"how much of $\mathbf{b}$ points along $\mathbf{a}$?"* That quantity is the **projection** of $\mathbf{b}$ onto $\mathbf{a}$ — the shadow $\mathbf{b}$ casts on the line through $\mathbf{a}$. Start from $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta$ and divide by $\|\mathbf{a}\|$:

$$
\text{scalar proj} = \|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|}, \qquad
\text{vector proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b} = \underbrace{\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}}}_{\text{how many copies of }\mathbf{a}}\,\mathbf{a}
$$

The denominator $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2$ rescales by $\mathbf{a}$'s length so the result lands exactly on the line through $\mathbf{a}$. Drag the vectors above to watch the projection segment grow and shrink with the angle.

## Vector spaces and basis

A **vector space** is a set of vectors that is closed under addition and scalar multiplication. $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the most common one in ML.

A **basis** is a set of linearly independent vectors (defined in the next section) that span the space. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, the standard basis is:

$$
\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Every vector is a **linear combination** of basis vectors: $\mathbf{v} = v_1\mathbf{e}_1 + v_2\mathbf{e}_2$.

<Callout type="info" title="Orthogonal bases are special">
  When basis vectors are mutually orthogonal (all pairwise dot products = 0), projection onto any basis vector is simply the dot product — no cross-term contamination. This makes orthogonal bases computationally clean and geometrically intuitive.
</Callout>

## Linear independence and span

Vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k$ are **linearly independent** if no one of them can be written as a combination of the others. Equivalently, $\sum_i c_i \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$ implies all $c_i = 0$.

The **span** of a set is all vectors you can reach by linear combination — it describes the "reachable" subspace.

### Worked example: dependent vs independent

Take $\mathbf{a} = [1, 2]$ and $\mathbf{b} = [2, 4]$. Notice $\mathbf{b} = 2\mathbf{a}$, so $2\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$ with non-zero coefficients — they are **dependent**. Their span is just the *line* through $\mathbf{a}$; no combination ever leaves it, so they cannot describe a 2D plane.

Now take $\mathbf{a} = [1, 2]$ and $\mathbf{c} = [2, 1]$. Setting $c_1\mathbf{a} + c_2\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ gives the system:

$$
c_1 + 2c_2 = 0 \quad \text{(top row)}, \qquad 2c_1 + c_2 = 0 \quad \text{(bottom row)}
$$

From the first equation $c_1 = -2c_2$. Substituting into the second: $2(-2c_2) + c_2 = -3c_2 = 0$, so $c_2 = 0$ and therefore $c_1 = 0$. The only solution is the trivial one, so $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ are **independent** and their span is *all* of $\mathbb{R}^2$ — they form a basis.

<Callout type="tip" title="Computing rank with row reduction">
  Stack the vectors as columns of a matrix, then apply Gaussian elimination (row reduction): swap rows, scale rows, and subtract multiples of one row from another to reach **row echelon form**. Count the non-zero rows — that count is the **rank**. Full rank (rank = number of columns) ⇒ all vectors are independent.

  **Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}$ → subtract 2×row1 from row2 → $\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$. One non-zero row → rank 1 → vectors are **dependent** ($\mathbf{b} = 2\mathbf{a}$). ✓
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

1. **Confusing the dot product with the cross product** — the dot product $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ is a scalar; the cross product is a vector (and only defined in $\mathbb{R}^3$). Also, cosine similarity requires normalizing both vectors first — skipping normalization makes it length-dependent, not angle-dependent.
2. **Assuming all vector spaces are Euclidean** — $\mathbb{R}^n$ is not the only vector space. Function spaces (e.g. continuous functions on $[0,1]$) and probability distributions (under appropriate operations) are also vector spaces. ML uses many non-Euclidean spaces.
3. **Confusing a basis with just any spanning set** — a basis must be both linearly independent *and* spanning. A spanning set with redundant vectors is not a basis; removing the redundancy (e.g. via row reduction) is needed to obtain one.

## Key takeaways

- A vector is a list of numbers representing a point/direction in space.
- The **dot product** $\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}$ is the core of every linear model — it measures similarity and projects one vector onto another.
- **Orthogonal** vectors have zero dot product; orthogonal bases simplify projection.
- **Norm** measures length; normalizing a vector gives direction without magnitude.

## Connection to ML

The vector operations in this lesson appear at the core of almost every ML algorithm:

- **Linear models** — $f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$ is a single dot product between the weight vector and the input. The dot product is the prediction.
- **Neural network neurons** — each neuron computes a dot product $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}$ before applying a nonlinearity. A fully-connected layer applies this for every row of the weight matrix simultaneously.
- **Cosine similarity** — used in word embeddings and recommendation systems to measure how similar two vectors are independent of their length: $\text{sim}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \hat{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}}$.
- **Least-squares regression** — the OLS solution projects the target vector $\mathbf{y}$ onto the column space of the design matrix $\mathbf{X}$. The projection formula derived above is exactly what $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$ computes.
- **PCA** — finds the orthogonal basis of maximum variance. Projecting data onto principal components is a sequence of dot products with those basis vectors.
- **Rank deficiency** — if a feature matrix is rank-deficient, two features are linearly dependent. The model gains no new information from the second feature, and OLS becomes unstable.
