# Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

When you apply a matrix $\mathbf{A}$ to most vectors, both the direction and the length change. But there exist special vectors — **eigenvectors** — that only get scaled, never rotated. These special directions reveal the matrix's fundamental geometry.

**Real-world examples:** PCA uses eigenvectors of the covariance matrix to find directions of maximum variance; Google PageRank is the dominant eigenvector of the web link graph; neural network stability analysis uses the spectral radius (largest absolute eigenvalue) to determine whether gradients will explode or vanish.

## The eigenvalue equation

A non-zero vector $\mathbf{v}$ is an **eigenvector** of $\mathbf{A}$ if:

$$
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}
$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is the corresponding **eigenvalue** — it tells you how much the eigenvector stretches or shrinks.

- $\lambda > 1$: stretches along this direction
- $0 < \lambda < 1$: shrinks along this direction
- $\lambda < 0$: flips and scales
- $\lambda = 0$: collapses — information is lost in this direction (rank deficiency)

## Finding eigenvalues

Rewrite the equation: $(\mathbf{A} - \lambda \mathbf{I})\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. For this to have a non-zero solution, the matrix must be singular:

$$
\det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda \mathbf{I}) = 0
$$

This is the **characteristic equation**. For a $2 \times 2$ matrix, it's a quadratic in $\lambda$.

For $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix}$, the characteristic equation $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0$ gives $\lambda_1 = 5$, $\lambda_2 = 2$. Substituting each eigenvalue back into $(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$ yields eigenvectors $[1,1]^\top$ and $[1,-2]^\top$. Both rows collapse to one constraint because the matrix is singular by construction — a whole line of solutions exists. ## Eigendecomposition

If $\mathbf{A}$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, it can be **diagonalized**:

$$
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{-1}
$$

where $\mathbf{V} = [\mathbf{v}_1 | \cdots | \mathbf{v}_n]$ and $\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$.

<Callout type="info" title="Symmetric matrices are special">
  For **symmetric** matrices ($\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}^\top$), eigenvalues are always real, eigenvectors are orthogonal, and $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{Q}^\top$ where $\mathbf{Q}$ is orthogonal. Covariance matrices are symmetric — this is why PCA always gives orthogonal components.
</Callout>

## Connection to PCA

PCA of a dataset $\mathbf{X}$ finds the eigenvectors of the **covariance matrix** $\mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$.

- The eigenvector with the **largest eigenvalue** is the direction of maximum variance (first principal component).
- The eigenvector with the second largest eigenvalue is the next orthogonal direction of maximum variance.
- Projecting $\mathbf{X}$ onto the top $k$ eigenvectors gives a rank-$k$ approximation that captures as much variance as possible.

$$
\mathbf{X}_\text{PCA} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}_k, \quad \mathbf{V}_k = [\mathbf{v}_1 | \cdots | \mathbf{v}_k]
$$

```python
# PCA via eigendecomposition
X = np.random.randn(100, 5)       # 100 samples, 5 features
X -= X.mean(axis=0)               # center the data

C = (X.T @ X) / len(X)           # covariance matrix
eigenvalues, V = np.linalg.eigh(C) # eigh for symmetric matrices

# Sort by descending eigenvalue
idx = np.argsort(eigenvalues)[::-1]
eigenvalues, V = eigenvalues[idx], V[:, idx]

explained_variance = eigenvalues / eigenvalues.sum()
print("Variance explained by PC1:", explained_variance[0])

X_2d = X @ V[:, :2]              # project to 2D
```

## Trace and determinant

Two scalar summaries of a matrix are directly tied to eigenvalues:

$$
\text{tr}(\mathbf{A}) = \sum_i A_{ii} = \sum_i \lambda_i, \qquad \det(\mathbf{A}) = \prod_i \lambda_i
$$

- **Trace** = sum of eigenvalues = total variance preserved.
- **Determinant** = product of eigenvalues = signed volume scaling factor.
  - $\det = 0$ means at least one eigenvalue is 0 → matrix is singular, not invertible.

## Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

For **any** matrix (not just square or symmetric), SVD generalizes eigendecomposition:

$$
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top
$$

- $\mathbf{U}$: left singular vectors (orthonormal)
- $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$: diagonal matrix of singular values $\sigma_i \geq 0$
- $\mathbf{V}$: right singular vectors (orthonormal)

SVD is used for **low-rank approximations** (keeping only the top-$k$ singular values), matrix completion (Netflix Prize), and computing the pseudo-inverse.

<Callout type="tip" title="SVD everywhere in ML">
  For a square symmetric PSD matrix like a covariance matrix, SVD and eigendecomposition coincide: $\sigma_i = \lambda_i$ and $\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{Q}$.
  For general weight matrices in neural networks, SVD reveals the effective rank and is used for compression.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

1. **Assuming all matrices are diagonalizable** — defective matrices (repeated eigenvalues with too few independent eigenvectors) cannot be diagonalized. The $2\times2$ matrix $\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalue 0 with algebraic multiplicity 2 but only one eigenvector direction.
2. **Confusing eigenvalues with singular values** — they coincide only for symmetric positive semi-definite matrices. For a general matrix, singular values are always non-negative, eigenvalues can be negative or complex, and the two sets are unrelated.
3. **Forgetting eigenvectors are defined only up to scalar multiple** — only the *direction* of an eigenvector is meaningful, not its length or sign. Multiplying an eigenvector by any non-zero scalar gives another valid eigenvector for the same eigenvalue.

## Key takeaways

- Eigenvectors are directions that a matrix only **scales** — the eigenvalue is the scale factor.
- Symmetric matrices have **real, orthogonal** eigenvectors — guaranteed clean decomposition.
- PCA = finding eigenvectors of the covariance matrix; eigenvalues = variance along each component.
- **SVD** generalizes to any matrix shape; singular values are always non-negative.
- $\det(\mathbf{A}) = \prod \lambda_i$ — zero determinant means lost information (singular matrix).
