# Audio Representations & Spectrograms

Audio is a wave — a one-dimensional signal of air pressure over time. Feeding a raw waveform to a model is possible but hard: it's enormously long (tens of thousands of samples per second) and the information you care about lives in its *frequencies*, not its raw amplitudes. The key move in audio ML is to transform sound into a **spectrogram** — a 2-D time-frequency image — which turns audio into something CNNs and transformers handle naturally. This lesson covers that pipeline. It's the **data** stage of the [ML project loop](/wiki/ml-project-loop) for sound: the representation you feed the model (waveform vs mel spectrogram vs MFCC) decides what's even learnable, before any architecture choice.

**Real-world examples:** every speech recognizer, music classifier, voice assistant, sound-event detector, and audio generation model starts by computing spectrogram-style features (or learns an equivalent front-end).

## The waveform and sampling

A microphone records a continuous pressure wave; a computer stores **samples** of it at a fixed **sampling rate** $f_s$ (e.g. 16 kHz for speech, 44.1 kHz for music). The **Nyquist theorem** says you can faithfully represent frequencies only up to $f_s/2$ — so 16 kHz audio captures content up to 8 kHz, enough for intelligible speech.

The result is a long 1-D array: one second of 16 kHz audio is 16,000 numbers. Raw, this is unwieldy and frequency information is implicit.

## From time to frequency: the Fourier transform

The **Fourier transform** rewrites a signal as a sum of sinusoids — revealing *which frequencies* are present and how strongly. A pure 440 Hz tone is a flat waveform but a single spike in the frequency domain. For audio, frequency content is exactly the perceptually and linguistically meaningful part (pitch, formants, timbre).

But a single Fourier transform over a whole clip loses *when* each frequency occurred. Speech changes constantly, so we need both time and frequency.

## The spectrogram (STFT)

The **Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)** solves this: slide a short window across the signal and take the Fourier transform of each window. The result is a 2-D map — **time on one axis, frequency on the other, magnitude as intensity** — the **spectrogram**.

$$
\text{STFT}: \text{1-D waveform} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{2-D } (\text{time} \times \text{frequency}) \text{ image}
$$

<Callout type="info" title="The time–frequency resolution trade-off">
  The window length sets a fundamental trade-off: a **short** window localizes events precisely in time but resolves frequency poorly; a **long** window resolves frequency finely but blurs timing. You can't have perfect resolution in both (a signal-processing uncertainty principle). Speech systems pick a window around 25 ms with a 10 ms hop as a practical balance.
</Callout>

A spectrogram is an **image**, which is why CNNs (and now transformers) are the natural models for audio — the whole 2-D toolkit transfers directly.

## Mel spectrograms and MFCCs

Human hearing isn't linear in frequency: we distinguish low frequencies far more finely than high ones. Two perceptually-motivated transforms make features that match how we hear:

- **Mel spectrogram** — warp the frequency axis onto the **mel scale** (roughly logarithmic), so the representation devotes resolution where the ear does. This is the dominant input feature for modern deep speech and audio models.
- **MFCC (Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients)** — apply a further transform (a discrete cosine transform of the log-mel energies) to get a compact, decorrelated feature vector. MFCCs were *the* feature for classical (pre-deep-learning) speech recognition and remain common for lightweight systems.

| Representation | What it is | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| Raw waveform | 1-D samples | end-to-end models (wav2vec) |
| Spectrogram (STFT) | time × linear-frequency | general audio |
| **Mel spectrogram** | time × mel-frequency | **most deep speech/audio models** |
| MFCC | compact cepstral coefficients | classical ASR, small models |

<Callout type="tip" title="Learned front-ends">
  Modern systems sometimes skip hand-designed features and **learn** the front-end from the raw waveform (e.g. wav2vec 2.0's convolutional encoder). But mel spectrograms remain a strong, cheap default — even Whisper uses a log-mel spectrogram as its input.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

1. **Feeding raw waveforms by default.** It's long and frequency information is implicit; spectrogram features are usually easier and cheaper to learn from.
2. **Ignoring the sampling rate / Nyquist limit.** Mismatched sample rates or under-sampling discards or aliases the frequencies you need.
3. **Wrong window length.** Too short blurs frequency, too long blurs time; tune the STFT window to the signal.
4. **Using a linear frequency axis for speech.** The mel scale matches human hearing and consistently works better for speech.

## Key takeaways

- Audio is a **1-D waveform** sampled at rate $f_s$; the **Nyquist** limit caps representable frequency at $f_s/2$.
- The **STFT** turns a waveform into a 2-D **spectrogram** (time × frequency), making audio an *image* that CNNs/transformers handle — subject to a **time–frequency resolution trade-off**.
- **Mel spectrograms** warp frequency to match human hearing and are the dominant deep-audio feature; **MFCCs** are a compact classical variant.
- Some modern models **learn** the front-end from raw audio, but mel spectrograms remain a strong default (e.g. Whisper).
