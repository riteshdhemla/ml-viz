# Calibration & Uncertainty

A model that outputs probabilities is making a promise: "I'm 90% confident." **Calibration** asks whether that promise holds — among all the times it says 90%, is it actually right 90% of the time? A model can be highly *accurate* yet badly *calibrated*, and in high-stakes settings (medicine, autonomous systems, anything with a human in the loop) miscalibrated confidence is dangerous. This lesson covers how to measure calibration, how to fix it, and the two fundamentally different kinds of uncertainty a model faces.

A point forecast has no confidence to check, but the [GP version](/courses/bayesian-methods/02-gaussian-processes) produces a predictive interval — and a well-calibrated 90% band should contain the actual demand 90% of the time. Quantile loss and empirical coverage are exactly the calibration tools of this lesson, applied to a forecast instead of a classifier.

**Real-world examples:** a diagnostic model whose "85% malignant" must mean what it says; selective prediction (abstain when unsure); out-of-distribution detection; deciding when to escalate an LLM answer to a human.

## Confidence ≠ correctness

Modern neural networks — especially deep ones with softmax outputs — are typically **overconfident**: they report 99% on predictions that are right far less often. Accuracy measures *whether* the top prediction is correct; calibration measures whether the *probability* attached to it is trustworthy. They're independent: you can be accurate and overconfident, or mediocre but well-calibrated.

## Where miscalibration comes from

A model's *stated* job is to minimize a loss on a finite training set — recovering the true $P(Y\mid X)$ is only a **byproduct**, guaranteed only under conditions real training never fully meets. The gap between predicted confidence and true probability has several distinct sources:

- **The loss barely aims at calibration — and over-optimizing it hurts.** *Proper scoring rules* (cross-entropy, Brier) are minimized *in expectation* exactly at the true $P(Y\mid X)$, so log loss genuinely "wants" to be calibrated. But (a) **margin losses** like the SVM hinge don't estimate probabilities at all — the output is a score, not a probability, which is why SVMs need post-hoc scaling; and (b) modern nets drive training loss toward zero long past the calibrated point, pushing logits to extremes → **systematic overconfidence**.
- **Model-class mismatch.** If the true $P(Y\mid X)$ isn't representable by your model family, the best fit is a biased *projection* onto it — miscalibrated wherever the family can't bend to match. This is the approximation-error term of the [bias–variance decomposition](/wiki/bias-variance-decomposition).
- **The training data distribution** — several separate effects: **finite-sample noise** in the estimates; **class imbalance / reweighting**, which makes the model learn $P(Y\mid X)$ under the *training* prior rather than the deployment base rate; and **label noise**, which pushes confident predictions onto inherently ambiguous inputs.
- **Distribution shift (train ≠ deploy).** The biggest real-world cause. Calibration is *not* preserved when the input distribution moves — a model calibrated on validation data drifts the moment deployment differs.
- **Irreducible noise the labels never reveal.** Where the true probability is genuinely $0.7$ (overlapping classes), hard $0/1$ labels give the model no signal to *stop* at $0.7$. This is the **aleatoric** floor (below), distinct from **epistemic** confident-extrapolation into regions with no data.

Regularization cuts across several of these: weight decay and especially **label smoothing** deliberately trade likelihood fit for generalization, moving the optimum away from the raw-calibrated point — sometimes *improving* calibration (label smoothing softens overconfidence), sometimes not. The upshot: because the causes live in the *training* process, the standard fixes are **post-hoc** — they leave the model alone and re-map its scores onto the diagonal using held-out data.

| Source | Mechanism | Fixable with more data? |
|--------|-----------|:-----------------------:|
| Margin loss (SVM hinge) | output is a score, not a probability | No — needs post-hoc scaling |
| Over-optimizing a proper loss | logits driven to extremes → overconfidence | No — inherent to the training dynamics |
| Regularization / label smoothing | trades likelihood fit for generalization | No — a deliberate bias |
| Model-class mismatch | true $P(Y\mid X)$ not representable | No — approximation error |
| Finite-sample estimation error | noisy parameter estimates | **Yes** (epistemic) |
| Class imbalance / reweighting | learns the training prior, not the deploy base rate | Yes — with base-rate correction |
| Label noise | hard labels on ambiguous inputs | Partly |
| Distribution shift (train ≠ deploy) | calibration set ≠ deployment distribution | No — must recalibrate on target |
| Aleatoric noise | true probability is intrinsically non-$0/1$ | **No — irreducible** |
| Epistemic ignorance | confident extrapolation off the data | **Yes** |

## Measuring calibration

**Reliability diagram.** Bin predictions by their confidence, and for each bin plot the *actual accuracy* against the *average confidence*. Perfect calibration lies on the diagonal (confidence = accuracy); bars below it mean overconfidence. The viz shows this, plus the effect of the fix below:

<CalibrationViz />

**Expected Calibration Error (ECE).** Summarize the diagram in one number — the average gap between confidence and accuracy, weighted by how many predictions fall in each bin:

$$
\text{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{n_b}{N}\,\big|\,\text{acc}(b) - \text{conf}(b)\,\big|
$$

Lower is better; $0$ is perfectly calibrated. (ECE has known flaws — it depends on the binning — but it's the standard scalar.)

## Fixing calibration: temperature scaling

The simplest and most effective fix is **temperature scaling**: divide the logits by a single scalar $T$ before the softmax, tuned on a validation set:

$$
p_i = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{T}\right)_i
$$

- $T = 1$: unchanged.
- $T > 1$: **softens** the distribution → lower confidence (fixes overconfidence).
- $T < 1$: sharpens → higher confidence.

Because $T$ is a single parameter applied to all logits, it **rescales confidence without changing the ranking** — so accuracy is untouched while calibration improves. It's a cheap *post-hoc* step: train normally, then fit $T$ on held-out data. (Platt scaling and isotonic regression are alternatives.)

<Callout type="tip" title="Temperature scaling can't change which class wins">
  Dividing all logits by the same $T$ is monotonic, so the argmax — the predicted class — is identical for every input. That's why it improves calibration *for free*: top-1 accuracy is exactly preserved, only the confidence numbers move. (This is the same temperature knob used in decoding; here it's tuned for calibration, not sampling.)
</Callout>

Temperature scaling is the right tool when one global knob suffices. When the miscalibration isn't a uniform stretch — you need a per-score correction rather than a single scalar — the two standard alternatives are **Platt scaling** (fit a logistic sigmoid on the scores) and **isotonic regression** (fit a monotonic step function, non-parametrically). ## Two kinds of uncertainty

Not all uncertainty is the same, and the distinction drives what you can do about it:

| | Aleatoric | Epistemic |
|---|---|---|
| Source | inherent **noise** in the data | the **model's ignorance** |
| Example | overlapping classes, label noise, a blurry image | a region with no training data |
| Reducible? | **No** — irreducible, even with infinite data | **Yes** — shrinks with more data |
| Detect by | the predictive distribution's spread | disagreement across models / Bayesian posterior |

**Aleatoric** uncertainty is the noise floor — the irreducible noise variance $\sigma^2$ that appears in the bias-variance decomposition, present even with infinite data. **Epistemic** uncertainty is what a Gaussian process or Bayesian model reports as it moves away from data — and it's why ensembles are useful: models trained from different seeds *agree* where they're confident and *disagree* where they're uncertain.

## Estimating uncertainty in deep nets

- **Deep ensembles** — train several models; the spread of their predictions estimates epistemic uncertainty. Simple and strong.
- **MC dropout** — keep dropout on at inference and average many stochastic forward passes — a cheap approximation to a Bayesian posterior.
- **Bayesian methods** (GPs, Bayesian NNs) — model the posterior directly (see the Bayesian Methods course).

## Common mistakes

1. **Trusting raw softmax probabilities.** Deep nets are usually overconfident; calibrate before treating outputs as probabilities.
2. **Confusing accuracy with calibration.** A 95%-accurate model can still be badly miscalibrated; measure ECE separately.
3. **Calibrating on the training set.** Fit temperature/Platt scaling on held-out validation data, or you'll just memorize.
4. **Conflating aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty.** More data fixes only the epistemic part; the aleatoric noise floor remains.

## Key takeaways

- **Calibration** asks whether predicted confidence matches empirical accuracy; it's independent of accuracy, and deep nets are typically **overconfident**.
- Measure it with a **reliability diagram** and summarize with **ECE** (mean confidence–accuracy gap).
- **Temperature scaling** (divide logits by a tuned $T$) is a cheap post-hoc fix that improves calibration **without changing accuracy**.
- **Aleatoric** uncertainty (data noise) is irreducible; **epistemic** uncertainty (model ignorance) shrinks with data — estimate it with **ensembles, MC dropout, or Bayesian methods**.
