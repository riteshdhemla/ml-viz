# Learning Theory & Generalization

Training error is easy to drive to zero — just memorize. The real question, the one all of machine learning rests on, is *why does fitting the training data ever help on data you haven't seen?* **Learning theory** answers it: it formalizes what we minimize, what generalization costs, and why bigger models don't always overfit the way classical intuition says they should.

**Real-world examples:** every decision about model size, regularization strength, and dataset size is a bet about generalization; the bias-variance trade-off underlies model selection; double descent reshaped how we think about overparameterized deep nets.

## Empirical risk minimization

What we *want* to minimize is the **true risk** — expected loss over the real data distribution $\mathcal{D}$, which we can never see:

$$
R(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[L(f(\mathbf{x}), y)\big]
$$

What we *can* minimize is the **empirical risk** — average loss over our finite training sample:

$$
\hat{R}(f) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} L(f(\mathbf{x}_i), y_i)
$$

Training a model is **empirical risk minimization (ERM)**: pick the $f$ that minimizes $\hat{R}$ and *hope* it also minimizes $R$. Generalization theory is the study of when that hope is justified — i.e. how close $\hat{R}(f)$ stays to $R(f)$.

## The generalization gap

The quantity that matters is the **generalization gap**:

$$
\text{gap} = R(f) - \hat{R}(f) \quad (\approx \text{test error} - \text{training error})
$$

A small training error is worthless if the gap is large. Theory shows the gap tends to **grow with model capacity** (a more flexible model can fit the particular sample more tightly, including its noise) and **shrink with more data** (a bigger sample is harder to fit by coincidence). This is the formal backing for "more data helps" and "simpler models generalize better."

## The bias–variance decomposition

For squared loss, expected test error splits into three pieces:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}))^2\big] = \underbrace{\text{Bias}^2}_{\text{too simple}} + \underbrace{\text{Variance}}_{\text{too sensitive}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}}
$$

- **Bias**: error from wrong assumptions; a model too simple to capture the truth (**underfitting**).
- **Variance**: error from sensitivity to the particular training sample; an over-flexible model that fits noise (**overfitting**).
- **Irreducible noise**: the floor no model can beat.

<BiasVarianceViz />

Classically, capacity trades one for the other: increasing model complexity lowers bias but raises variance, giving the familiar **U-shaped** test-error curve with a sweet spot in the middle.

<Callout type="info" title="Regularization shifts the trade-off">
  L2/L1 penalties, dropout, and early stopping all *reduce variance* at the cost of a little bias — deliberately moving left on the complexity axis to land nearer the sweet spot. That's why a regularized complex model often beats an unregularized simpler one.
</Callout>

## Capacity: VC dimension

How do we quantify "model capacity"? The classic measure is **VC (Vapnik–Chervonenkis) dimension**: the size of the largest set of points the model class can **shatter** (classify in *all* possible label arrangements). A linear classifier in 2D has VC dimension 3. It can shatter 3 points but not 4.

VC theory bounds the generalization gap roughly as:

$$
R(f) \le \hat{R}(f) + O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{d_{\text{VC}}}{n}}\right)
$$

More capacity ($d_{\text{VC}}$) or less data ($n$) loosens the bound. It's the formal statement of "complex models need more data."

## Double descent: the modern surprise

Classical theory predicts test error should rise once a model is complex enough to interpolate (memorize) the training set. Deep learning violated this. As you keep growing an already-interpolating model **past** the point where it hits zero training error, test error often **falls again**: the **double descent** curve.

The visualization below is not a sketch of that claim, it is the experiment. Random-ReLU-feature ridge regression on $n = 40$ noisy points, sweeping the model width $p$ from 1 to 160 and measuring train and test error at every width.

<DoubleDescentViz />

Three things happen in order, and the third is the one classical theory does not have a slot for:

1. **The classical U**, up to about $p = 14$. Test error falls as the model gains enough capacity to represent the teacher, then starts to climb as it begins fitting the noise. This is the bias-variance trade-off exactly as the section above describes it.
2. **The spike at $p \approx n$.** Test error does not just climb, it *explodes* — around 12× the classical best. At $p = n$ there is exactly **one** function in the class that fits all 40 training points, so the model has no choice but to take it, however violently it has to wiggle to pass through 40 noisy targets.
3. **The second descent.** Past the threshold there are now *infinitely many* interpolating functions, and the fit is free to choose among them. Test error falls again, and keeps falling — ending up roughly a quarter below the classical sweet spot.

### What actually drives it

The `‖w‖` panel is the mechanism rather than a decoration. **The size of the fitted weights peaks at exactly the width where test error peaks**, and decays as the model grows past it. That co-location is the whole explanation:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \arg\min \|\mathbf{w}\| \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Phi\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{y}
$$

Among the many interpolating solutions, least squares (and gradient descent from a zero initialization, which converges to the same place) picks the one of **minimum norm**. At $p \approx n$ that constraint set has a single element and the minimum is whatever it is. Widening the model *enlarges the set of solutions to choose from*, so the smallest-norm member gets smaller, and a low-norm function is a smooth one. Extra parameters do not buy more fitting, they buy more **choice about how to fit**.

<Callout type="tip" title="Test the explanation instead of trusting it">
  If the spike really is a near-singular system rather than a deep truth about
  capacity, then a little explicit regularization should erase it. Click through
  the λ buttons: the peak drops from ~750 at λ ≈ 0 to 13.9 at $10^{-3}$ to 5.7 at
  $10^{-2}$, and by λ = 0.1 there is no interior peak left at all — just the
  ordinary monotone curve. **Double descent is a phenomenon of unregularized
  interpolation.** A properly regularized model never rides the spike in the
  first place.
</Callout>

This is why massively overparameterized networks (which classical bias-variance would predict to overfit catastrophically) generalize fine. It doesn't repeal the bias-variance idea; it shows that raw parameter count is the wrong x-axis. What matters is the norm of the solution your optimizer actually lands on, and **implicit regularization** is what decides that.

## Common mistakes

1. **Trusting training error.** Low $\hat{R}$ with a large generalization gap is overfitting; only held-out error estimates $R$.
2. **Equating more parameters with more overfitting.** Double descent shows overparameterized models can generalize — capacity isn't the whole story.
3. **Ignoring the data axis.** The gap shrinks with $n$; sometimes more data beats any modeling trick.
4. **Reading bias-variance off training loss.** The decomposition is about *expected test* error over datasets, not a single training run.

## Key takeaways

- Training is **empirical risk minimization** — minimizing average training loss as a proxy for the unseen **true risk**; theory studies the **generalization gap** between them.
- The gap **grows with capacity** and **shrinks with data**; the **bias-variance decomposition** splits test error into underfitting (bias), overfitting (variance), and irreducible noise.
- **VC dimension** measures capacity and bounds the gap as $\sim\!\sqrt{d_{\text{VC}}/n}$ — formalizing "complex models need more data."
- **Double descent**: past the interpolation threshold, test error can fall again, so overparameterized models generalize — implicit regularization, not raw capacity, decides.
